Peer review: Carbohydrate gene content and ten-day relative abundance of Bifidobacterium angulatum in one person's gut metagenome

Reviews of the submitted version of the article (frozen on 4 Nov 2026, before the reviews). Three anonymous reviewers each covered one aspect, and a handling editor weighed their reports and made the decision. Editorial decision

Manuscript: Carbohydrate gene content and ten-day relative abundance of Bifidobacterium angulatum in one person's gut metagenome

Decision: Reject, with encouragement to resubmit

Summary of the reviews

All three reviewers find the manuscript honest and careful. It states its hypothesis and expected outcome in advance, keeps gene potential apart from activity, and reports a negative answer to its own question. The reviewers checked the numbers against the data file and found them correct. They agree on three main problems. The "day 3-4-5 group" of 12 MAGs is no larger than chance would give (R1-M3, R2-M1). The fold changes rest on samples of very different depth and on shifting baselines (R1-M1, R2-M2, R2-M3). Technical explanations for the day-5 peak, mainly DNA extraction, are not considered (R1-M2, R2-M4). Where they differ is how much this matters. Reviewer 2 recommends minor revision. Reviewers 1 and 3 recommend major revision, Reviewer 1 because the methods are incomplete and Reviewer 3 because the gap and the novelty are not stated.

Basis for the decision

The ISME Journal publishes work that advances microbial ecology in general. This manuscript follows one low-abundance taxon (at most 0.29% of the bases) in one person over ten days, and diet is confounded with time and calories. The authors' main conclusion, that the period means fit the hypothesis but the timing does not, is well supported, and it is stated with suitable caution. But that conclusion is about one species in one person, and the manuscript does not say what it adds to the field (R3-M1, R3-M2). The secondary result, a group of carbohydrate users that rise together, is presented in the Abstract as a shared response. Under random day order about 24 of the 191 MAGs would show the same pattern, so the 12 observed do not support a shared response (R1-M3, R2-M1). The closed, circular B. angulatum genome is a solid technical result, but on its own it is not enough for this journal.

The issues of reframing, statistics and context go beyond what a revision can address. I therefore decline the manuscript in its current form. I would welcome a new submission built around a question that this design can answer.

Essential points for the authors

  1. State a gap and an aim that the data can address, for example how a whole community varies from day to day within one person. Place this in the literature on dense time series and long-read gut metagenomes (R3-M1, R3-M2, R3-M3).
  2. Give a chance expectation for the day 3-4-5 group, either 1/8 under random order or from permuted days. Add a minimum effect size, and present the group as exploratory unless it exceeds chance (R1-M3, R2-M1, R2-M5).
  3. Report bases and the B. angulatum read depth for every sample, including about 0.4x on day 2. Use one robust baseline, the median of days 1-4, throughout. Add uncertainty by subsampling or bootstrapping reads (R1-M1, R2-M2, R2-M3).
  4. Discuss technical causes of the day-5 peak (extraction bias across cell-wall types, compositional effects), and the return to the habitual diet after day 5 (R1-M2, R2-M4).
  5. Complete the methods: extraction, mapping and filters, tool versions, GH13 subfamilies, and an ethics and consent statement. Remove the web-page wording from the figures and use a log axis for Figure 3 (R1-M4, R1-M5, R1-M6, R3-M4, R3-M5).

Notes on the reviews

I found no factual errors in the reviews. I checked the depth estimates, the 1/8 chance expectation and the small day-5 changes of individual MAGs against data.json. Reviewers 1 and 3 ask for public deposition of the reads (R1-M6, R3-M4). A journal would require this, and a real submission would use controlled-access deposition (for example EGA) after removing human reads. The reads are withheld here to protect the donor's privacy, so I have not counted this against the authors. The small log2 inconsistency (R1-m1, R3-m6) and the two "87%" figures (R1-m3, R2-m4) should be fixed, but they did not affect the decision.

What would make this publishable

A study at ISME J level would need more than one person, or repeated diet switches in a crossover design, so that diet can be separated from time. Alternatively, it could reframe the ten days as a community-level analysis of daily variation, with a statistical null model, depth-normalised abundance and technical replicates. The complete bifidobacterial genomes could then support a comparison of carbohydrate gene content across hosts or strains. A narrower descriptive version could suit a journal that publishes short genome or data reports.

Reviewer 1: Methods and data

Summary of the manuscript The authors co-assembled Nanopore R10.4.1 reads (166.5 Gbp after filtering) from ten daily stool samples of one person into 421 MAGs. They describe the carbohydrate gene content of one complete, circular Bifidobacterium angulatum MAG and follow its relative abundance (share of each day's bases) across a diet switch after day 5. They also define a "day 3-4-5 group" of 12 MAGs that rose up to day 5 and fell on day 6. Their main conclusion is that the period means agree with the hypothesis but the timing does not: the rise and peak came before the switch.

Overall assessment The analyses are described in the Results honestly and cautiously. Units are stated (share of bases, percentage points, log2), the study design limitations are acknowledged, and the claims are kept descriptive. The data-quality section (bases in HQ/MQ MAGs, unbinned contigs) is a real strength. The main problem is that sequencing depth differs about 15-fold between samples, and the manuscript does not account for this. The headline 5.9-fold rise starts from day 2, which has only 1.67 Gbp. The Methods also leave too much unspecified for anyone to reproduce the work: extraction, mapping, tool versions and data deposition. Major revision.

Major points

  1. Uneven sequencing depth and counting uncertainty. The per-sample yield ranges from 1.67 Gbp (day 2) to 25.62 Gbp (day 4) (data file, context), but only days 4 and 5 are mentioned. From the reported share and genome size, B. angulatum has about 0.4x mean depth on day 2 and about 1.4x on day 10. So the 5.9-fold rise "within the high-carbohydrate days" (day 2 to day 5) and the day-10 minimum rest on very few reads, and the day-to-day ranks and CVs include unknown counting noise. Please report the bases and read N50 for every sample. Then give the day-4-to-day-5 and day-2-to-day-5 comparisons with an uncertainty estimate: subsample reads to equal depth (the "random read sample" in S3 appears to exist, so bring it into the article), or bootstrap reads per day. Stating the fold rise from the median of days 1-4 (3.5-fold, already computed) instead of from the single lowest day would also be more robust.

  2. No technical replicate; extraction and library effects cannot be separated from biology. Each day is one extraction and one rapid-barcoding library. The day-5 sample yielded fewer bases than its neighbours, and the 12-MAG group roughly doubled its share on that same day (6.2% to 11.6%). Extraction efficiency differs between Gram-positive and Gram-negative cells, so differences in extraction can move many Bacillota and Actinomycetota together. The extraction method is still "[to be filled in]". It must be described (kit, bead beating), and the authors should discuss whether the day-5 peak could be a technical artefact. A re-extraction or re-sequencing of day 5 would be the direct test.

  3. The day 3-4-5 group has no null expectation. If the four daily shares (days 3-6) of a MAG were in random order, the pattern d3 < d4 < d5 > d6 would occur with probability 1/8. That is about 24 of 191 MAGs. The 12 observed are therefore not more than chance would give. The authors note that 12-23 MAGs rise on later days, which points the same way. Please state the chance expectation, or compare against permuted day orders, and soften the "rise together" framing to match. Shares are also compositional: a rise of about 5 percentage points in some MAGs must lower the shares of the others. The group analysis should be treated as exploratory.

  4. Abundance estimation is under-specified. "Mean read depth times contig length, from the read mapping of mmlong2" does not name the mapper, the MAPQ or identity filters, how secondary and supplementary alignments are handled, or whether depth is trimmed at contig ends. These choices matter for a 0.01-0.3% MAG that has close relatives (B. adolescentis, B. longum) in the same sample. Please specify them and report breadth of coverage per day for B. angulatum.

  5. The annotation methods limit the functional claims. (a) Bakta was run with the light database, so names rest on clusters at 50% identity or more (as stated). The full database would give more specific names. (b) dbCAN3 recommends agreement between at least two of its tools (HMMER, DIAMOND, dbCAN-sub). The Methods describe HMM profiles only. (c) GH13 is a large polyspecific family. Reporting GH13 subfamilies (dbCAN-sub) would show whether the 9 genes are amylases or sucrose phosphorylases and similar enzymes, which directly addresses the starch claim. (d) The median-MAG comparisons include MQ MAGs down to 50% completeness, which biases counts downward. Please restrict them to HQ MAGs or normalise per 1,000 genes, as was done for the risers. (e) Nanopore assemblies can contain indel errors that split genes. State whether polishing was done, and compare the gene count and ANI with the type strain genome [3] as a check.

  6. Reproducibility and data availability. Versions are given for Bakta, its database and GTDB, but not for chopper, Porechop_ABI, mmlong2, myloasm, the mapper, CheckM2, GTDB-Tk, dbCAN or its database, or Dorado. The basecaller version is still to be confirmed. The HQ definition should state which rRNA genes and how many tRNAs are required. The journal requires raw data to be deposited. Reads can be screened for human sequence and deposited (for example in ENA with controlled access), and the MAG of B. angulatum can be deposited without privacy concerns.

Minor points 1. Methods define log2 changes with a 0.01% pseudocount, but the step across the switch (-0.31) and the day-to-day ranks match log2 without a pseudocount (-0.30 with it). Use one definition. 2. The genome length is 1,988,448 bp in numbers but 1,988,444 bp in map, and the gene count is 1,564 CDS in the text but 1,578 in mags. Please explain these differences. 3. Two different "87%" figures appear (MAGs with at least one GH13 gene; MAGs with fewer GH13 genes than B. angulatum). The Discussion sentence is easy to misread. 4. The CVs use the sample standard deviation of five values. Say so, or drop them. 5. Reference [14] (a review of resistant-starch type 2 interventions) does not support "within days" in the Introduction. Cite [15] and [21] there instead. 6. Reference [11] studied 14 strains. The claim "nearly all cultivated human-gut bifidobacteria" should rest on [12] alone.

Checks performed - References (PubMed): [3] Morita 2015 (PMID 26142062) exists and is the type-strain complete genome, as stated. [16] Sereika 2025 (mmlong2), [20] Zheng 2023 (dbCAN3) and [11] Jarocki 2014 exist and are correctly attributed. [14] Bendiks 2020 exists and supports the variable B. adolescentis response, but not "within days" (minor point 5). - Numbers against data.json: the period means 0.128% and 0.07% and log2 -0.8 (recomputed: 0.1285 and 0.0698, -0.80); the 5.9-fold rise (0.2943/0.0497); CV 76% and 136%; Spearman 0.94 and 0.43; the share in MAGs of 76.5% against 81.9%; the total of 166.5 Gbp; and the 14.5x depth on day 5 (0.294% × 9.82 Gbp / 1.99 Mb). All agree. The pseudocount inconsistency is in minor point 1. - Figures: Figure 3 matches the daily shares and its legend. In Figure 4, rows are ordered by the day-4-to-day-5 fold change, consistent with Table 2 (B. angulatum is third).

Recommendation to the editor: major revision. The descriptive analysis is careful, but the uneven sequencing depth, the missing null expectation for the co-varying group, and the incomplete methods (extraction, mapping, versions, data deposition) must be addressed before the abundance conclusions can be judged.

Reviewer 2: Results and interpretation

Summary of the manuscript The authors follow the relative abundance of a single, circular Bifidobacterium angulatum MAG over ten daily stool samples from one person. The person switched from a high-carbohydrate, high-calorie diet (days 1-5) to a low-carbohydrate, low-calorie diet (days 6-10). They describe the genome's predicted carbohydrate genes (9 GH13) and compare its time course with those of B. adolescentis and B. longum. They also describe a group of 12 MAGs whose share rose on days 3-5 and fell on day 6. The authors conclude that the hypothesis (lower abundance after the switch) is supported only by the period means, because the rise happened before the switch.

Overall assessment This draft is unusually careful. It states its hypothesis and its expected outcome in advance, separates measured results from interpretation, reports a negative answer to its own question ("not the largest riser") and names most limitations. Nearly every number I checked matches data.json. The main problem is that the day-5 analysis is given more weight than the data can carry. The 12-MAG "day 3-4-5 group" is defined by a rule that would select a similar number of MAGs by chance, and its members include MAGs that hardly change. The day-4 to day-5 ranking depends on day 4 being a low point for many MAGs. Technical explanations (DNA extraction, sequencing depth, compositional effects) are not considered as fully as dietary ones. These are problems of framing and of missing checks, not of errors, and they can be fixed.

Major points

  1. The day 3-4-5 group cannot be told apart from noise. (Methods, statistics section; Results, "MAGs that rise..."; Figure 4; Abstract.) If the four daily values of a MAG were in random order, the chance of day 3 < day 4 < day 5 > day 6 would be 1/4 × 1/2 = 1/8. That gives about 24 of the 191 MAGs, more than the 12 observed. The authors already report 12-23 MAGs passing the same rule on days 6-10. Several members barely move: Coprococcus_A (1.438) goes 0.141 → 0.153 → 0.160% (1.0-fold, +0.01 percentage points), COE1 (2.346) rises 1.2-fold, and Zag111 (c.34) 1.1-fold. In Figure 4, the strongest colours for Zag111 and Roseburia_D fall on days 9-10, not on day 5. Why it matters: the Abstract and Discussion present "12 MAGs rose" as a shared response and link it to a starch- or fibre-rich food. What would fix it: add a minimum effect size (for example at least 1.5-fold on day 5 against day 4), state the number expected by chance (for example by permuting days), and say in the Abstract that the count is similar to what other days give.

  2. Day 4 is a low point, so day 5 / day 4 is not a clean "rise". (Table 2; "Is B. angulatum the MAG with the highest increase".) Many of the top risers dipped on day 4 rather than rising on day 5: Prevotella copri_B 2.359 (7.89 → 4.39 → 6.84%), Catenibacterium (0.52 → 0.21 → 0.68%), Fusicatenibacter, Dorea, R. callidus, A. rectalis c.38. In relative data, a dip shared by many taxa on one day means that something else rose that day. Day 4 is also the deepest sample (25.62 Gbp). The rank of B. angulatum (16 of 191) therefore depends on the choice of baseline. What would fix it: also rank against the median of days 1-4, which the report's riser definition already uses. Show which MAGs rose on day 4 and fell on day 5, and say which baseline the conclusion depends on.

  3. Baselines for "fold" are inconsistent, and the largest one rests on the thinnest sample. The Abstract's "5.9-fold" is day 5 over day 2 (0.294/0.050). Elsewhere the text uses 3.5-fold (day 5 over the median of days 1-4) and 2.2-fold (day 5 over day 4). Day 2 had only 1.67 Gbp. From the share and genome size, I estimate the B. angulatum read depth on day 2 at about 0.4x, and about 1.4x on day 10. The authors check depth only for the peak (14.5x). What would fix it: use one baseline in the Abstract (the median is more robust), and give the mapped bases or read depth of this MAG on every day, especially the low days 2 and 7-10.

  4. Alternative explanations for the shared day-5 change are incomplete. (Discussion, "Hypothesis" and "The group of MAGs".) The authors list a food eaten on days 4-5, chance and the part of the stool sampled. Eleven of the 12 group members are Gram-positive (Bacillota, Actinomycetota), so a difference in cell lysis during DNA extraction on day 5 would move them together. The extraction method is still "[to be filled in]". The authors could check this by plotting the summed share of Bacillota plus Actinomycetota against that of Bacteroidota per day. The Prevotella rise on day 5 argues partly against a lysis effect, which is itself worth saying. Also discuss that days 6-10 were closer to the habitual diet (Methods). The fall from day 7 may be a return to a habitual community, not a response to less carbohydrate. And because calories changed together with carbohydrate, the hypothesis should be worded as "high-carbohydrate, high-calorie" throughout. Walker et al. [21], already cited, report that E. rectale relatives decreased on a reduced-carbohydrate weight-loss diet. That is directly relevant to the days 6-10 diet and could be used.

  5. The GH13 community signal is partly circular. The abundance-weighted GH13 density peaks on day 5 because the MAGs selected for rising on day 5 include GH13-rich genomes. Even after removing three of them, the difference (2.76 vs at most 2.59 per 1,000 genes) is small, with no measure of how much it varies between days. "In line with it" should be softened, or the text should show how much this value varies across days 6-10.

Minor points

  1. Figure 3 uses a linear axis. B. angulatum and B. longum are squeezed near zero, so the 5.9-fold change described in the text is hard to see. Use a log axis or a separate panel. The shading of the high-carbohydrate period is barely visible.
  2. The Spearman correlation of 0.94 with B. longum (n = 10, both falling over days 6-10) mostly reflects the shared trend. Give it less weight, or remove the trend first. It also sits uneasily with the Abstract's emphasis on the day-5 peak shared with B. adolescentis.
  3. In the Abstract, "B. longum did not [peak on day 5]": its day-6 value (0.074%) is only 10% above day 5 (0.068%). Say "barely changed".
  4. Discussion: "more than in 87% of the MAGs" and "87% carry at least one GH13" are the same percentage for two different statistics. Please verify.
  5. Genome length is 1,988,448 bp in the numbers and 1,988,444 bp in the gene-map data. The mags table gives 1,578 genes against 1,564 CDS in the text; state that the former includes RNA genes, if that is the reason.
  6. Figure 1 shows the MAG fraction rising from 76.5% to 81.9% after the switch. Comment on whether this affects relative abundances (for example, binning that favours the later samples).

Recommendation to the editor: minor revision. The conclusions on B. angulatum are appropriately cautious, but the day 3-4-5 group and the day-5 ranking need a chance baseline, a robust reference day and a fuller set of technical alternatives before they are presented in the Abstract.

Reviewer 3: Context, novelty and presentation

Summary of the manuscript The authors follow the relative abundance (share of sequenced bases) of one Bifidobacterium angulatum metagenome-assembled genome (MAG) in ten daily stool samples from one person. The person switched from a high-carbohydrate to a low-carbohydrate vegetarian diet after day 5. Using a Nanopore co-assembly with 421 MAGs, they describe the carbohydrate gene content of a closed 1.99 Mb genome, compare it with two other bifidobacteria, and identify 12 MAGs that rise on days 3-5 and fall on day 6. They conclude that the period means fit the hypothesis of a lower abundance after the switch, but the timing (a rise before the switch) does not.

Overall assessment The manuscript is honest and careful. Limitations are stated clearly, the hypothesis is tested against the timing and not only the period means, and the gene-content claims are kept apart from claims about activity. The closed, circular B. angulatum genome from a long-read metagenome is a solid technical result. The main problem for this journal is novelty and framing. The core result describes one low-abundance taxon (at most 0.29% of the bases) in one person over ten days, with diet and time confounded. The introduction does not say what gap in microbial ecology this fills, and the literature on dense human gut time series and on long-read gut metagenomics, which is the natural context, is not cited. In its current form the study does not reach the ecological generality The ISME Journal expects. The submission is also incomplete: there are placeholders, no ethics statement and no data deposition.

Major points

  1. Gap and novelty are not stated (Introduction, para. 1 and 3). The only gap given is that B. angulatum "is less studied". The aim, to see how the abundance "is affected by the change in diet", cannot be reached with this design, as the authors say in the Discussion. This matters because editors judge fit from the gap and the aim. A fix: rewrite the last introduction paragraph around a question the data can answer. Two options are (a) how a whole community, and not one species, moves from day to day within one person, and whether a group of carbohydrate users rises together; or (b) what a closed bifidobacterial genome from a stool metagenome adds compared with short-read MAGs. Then state what is new.

  2. Key literature is missing (Introduction, Discussion). Day-to-day variation in one person is a well-studied topic, e.g. Caporaso et al. 2011 (Genome Biol 12:R50), David et al. 2014 (Genome Biol 15:R89) and Johnson et al. 2019 (Cell Host Microbe 25:789), on daily sampling and personal diet-microbiome links. These studies give the expected size of daily fluctuations, which the authors need in order to judge whether a 5.9-fold change in a taxon below 0.3% is unusual. Long-read gut metagenomes producing closed genomes (e.g. Moss et al. 2020, Nat Biotechnol) are also not cited, although "a long-read metagenome with many high-quality genomes" is part of the aim. Resistant-starch studies that track bifidobacteria in individuals (e.g. Venkataraman et al. 2016, Microbiome; Baxter et al. 2019, mBio) would back up the Discussion better than a single review.

  3. Some citations do not fully support their sentence. Ref. 14 (Bendiks et al. 2020) is a review of the conserved and variable responses to resistant starch type 2. It supports the Discussion sentence on B. adolescentis, but not the Introduction claim that communities "change within days of a diet change". Cite only ref. 15 there, or add a time-course study. Ref. 8 (Roberfroid 2001) is old and is used for "most bifidobacteria are adapted to use inulin-type fructans". Growth on long-chain inulin is strain-dependent, so cite species-level work or soften the claim. Ref. 9 concerns B. breve DSM20213, so name the species in the text. Ref. 13 is about prophages; a primary CRISPR-immunity reference would fit the sentence better.

  4. Journal requirements are not met (Methods, Data availability, back matter). Seven "[to be filled in by the group]" items remain: extraction, storage, loading, basecaller version, accession, contributions and funding. There is no ethics approval or consent statement for human stool samples. The journal requires one. The journal also expects sequence data in a public archive. Privacy can be handled by removing host reads before deposition or by using controlled access (e.g. EGA); the MAGs, at least, can be deposited. The analysis code is cited as a script path in an unnamed "course analysis repository". It needs a public repository with a DOI. Supplementary items S1-S6 point to a project report and must be supplied as Supplementary Information.

  5. Figures are made for a web page, not for the journal (Figures 1-4, Tables 1-2). The legends say "hover a bar/gene" and "click a column to sort", and the figures are on a dark background. Figure 3, the central figure, uses a linear axis from 0 to 1%, which squeezes B. angulatum and B. longum near zero. Their lines and end labels overlap, and the 5.9-fold rise is hard to see. Use a log axis or a separate panel. Figure 2 is dense: "other gene" and "part of a sugar-cleaving enzyme" are almost the same grey, and at the resolution I had I could not find all five CRISPR diamonds. Consider a linear map of the carbohydrate genes only, or larger symbols. Figure 4 has a colour bar labelled only at its ends, and no line marking the diet switch.

Minor points

  1. Title: it is descriptive but gives no finding. Consider something like "A within-period rise of Bifidobacterium angulatum and co-varying carbohydrate users in a ten-day single-person gut time series".
  2. Abstract: 225 words, within the 250-word limit, but it is mostly numbers. Add one sentence on the gap and end with an implication instead of only a limitation. "As a quality check" reads oddly in an abstract.
  3. Introduction para. 2 (the enzyme-family roles) reads like Results or Discussion material. Shorten it to two sentences and keep the details in Table 1.
  4. Table 1 has a CRISPR row in a table about carbohydrate genes. Move it to a separate row group or into the text.
  5. Discussion "Measured results" repeats the Results. "We looked for similar cases in PubMed" is informal; describe the comparison itself.
  6. The day-5 to day-6 step is given as log2 −0.31. With the stated pseudocount (0.01%) it is −0.30; −0.31 is the value without the pseudocount. Use one method throughout.
  7. Precision: write 0.070%, not 0.07%, next to 0.128%.
  8. Figure 1: the sentence about B. angulatum in the legend is not needed. The red and orange fills are close; a viridis scale would also be colour-blind safe.
  9. Remove the web-page header and the "Updates" text from the submitted file.

Refs 3, 7, 12 (99.76% of cultivated genomes carry a bile salt hydrolase), 16 (mmlong2) and 21 (diet "blooms" and the rise of E. rectale relatives on resistant starch) support their sentences. Ref. 9 supports its sentence but concerns B. breve. Ref. 13 supports it only indirectly.
