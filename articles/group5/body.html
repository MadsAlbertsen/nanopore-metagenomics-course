Carbohydrate gene content and ten-day relative abundance of Bifidobacterium angulatum in one person's gut metagenome

Authors: Group 5, Nanopore metagenomics course 2026 (author names are not published on this site). Built from the group's project report: version 2, version 1. Abstract

Bifidobacterium angulatum is a saccharolytic gut bacterium. We asked how its relative abundance changed over ten daily stool samples from one person, who ate a high-carbohydrate, high-calorie vegetarian diet on days 1-5 and a low-carbohydrate, low-calorie vegetarian diet on days 6-10 (information from the data owner), and what its genome predicts about carbohydrate use. Nanopore long reads ( Gbp after filtering) were co-assembled and binned into genomes (MAGs). As a quality check, high-quality MAGs held -% and all MAGs -% of each day's bases. The B. angulatum genome is one circular contig of with protein-coding genes, of them with a predicted carbohydrate role, including GH13 (alpha-glucan-degrading) genes. We expected a lower abundance after the switch. The mean share fell from % of the bases on days 1-5 to % on days 6-10, but the share first rose -fold within the high-carbohydrate period, peaked on day (%) and dropped from day 7; B. adolescentis peaked on the same day, B. longum did not. MAGs rose on days 3-5 and fell on day 6; B. angulatum was among them but not the largest riser from day 4 to day 5 (rank by fold change, by percentage points). The study describes relative abundance and gene potential, not activity, in one person, and diet cannot be separated from time.

Introduction

Members of the genus Bifidobacterium live mainly on carbohydrates that escape digestion in the small intestine, and their genomes carry many genes for carbohydrate-modifying enzymes and sugar transporters [1, 2]. B. angulatum is less studied than B. longum or B. adolescentis; the complete genome of its type strain, isolated from human faeces, was published in 2015 [3]. Because bifidobacteria are saccharolytic, their relative abundance is often expected to follow the supply of fermentable carbohydrate. We therefore state the following hypothesis, which the group formulated before the abundance data of this study were analysed: the relative abundance of B. angulatum decreases after a shift from a high-carbohydrate diet (days 1-5) to a low-carbohydrate diet (days 6-10).

Carbohydrate-active enzymes are grouped into sequence families in the CAZy database [4]. The families that matter most in the genome of B. angulatum (Table 1; counts in Results) have the following roles in the literature. Glycoside hydrolase family 13 (GH13) is the alpha-amylase family, which acts on starch, glycogen and related alpha-glucans [4]; resistant-starch-degrading amylases have been characterised in B. adolescentis [5]. GH43 and GH51 contain arabinofuranosidases and xylosidases that trim arabinoxylan, a plant fibre; in B. pseudocatenulatum, five GH43 enzymes and ABC transporters were induced together during growth on arabinoxylan-derived oligosaccharides [6], and a GH43 arabinofuranosidase of B. adolescentis cleaves arabinose from such oligosaccharides [7]. GH32 contains invertases and fructan hydrolases [4], and most bifidobacteria are adapted to use inulin-type fructans [8]. GH42 and GH2 contain beta-galactosidases; a GH42 enzyme from an infant Bifidobacterium isolate hydrolyses lactose and makes galacto-oligosaccharides [9]. Beyond these enzymes, bifidobacteria ferment sugars through the "bifid shunt", whose key enzyme is phosphoketolase [10]; bile salt hydrolases are found in nearly all cultivated human-gut bifidobacteria and may help them colonise the gut [11, 12]; and CRISPR systems defend against phages [13]. Gene families give a prediction of what a genome could do. They do not show which genes are expressed or which substrates are used.

Gut communities can change within days of a diet change [14, 15], so a ten-day series from one person shows how an individual bacterium moves relative to the others. Our aim was to investigate the relative abundance of B. angulatum and how it is affected by the change in diet, using a long-read metagenome with many high-quality genomes. We describe the quality of the data, the carbohydrate gene content of the genome, its abundance over the ten days compared with the two other Bifidobacterium MAGs, and the group of MAGs that rise and fall together around day 5.

Materials and methods

Study design and sample collection

One person gave one stool sample on each of ten consecutive days (samples DNA01-DNA10 are days 1-10). Both diet periods were vegetarian. Days 1-5 were high in carbohydrate and calories, days 6-10 low in both; days 6-10 were closer to the subject's normal diet, and the subject had eaten a similar high-calorie diet for about a week before day 1 (information from the data owner). Carbohydrate and calories therefore changed together, and food intake per day was not recorded. Sample handling and storage before extraction: [to be filled in by the group].

DNA extraction, library preparation and sequencing

DNA extraction (kit, bead beating, size selection, DNA quality control): [to be filled in by the group]. Libraries were prepared with the Oxford Nanopore Rapid Barcoding Kit 24 V14 (SQK-RBK114.24), and the ten barcoded libraries were sequenced on one PromethION flow cell (R10.4.1; flow cell identifier PBM34154 and run date 20 October 2026 from the project records; loading amount and run time [to be filled in by the group]). The project records state basecalling with Dorado (sup model v5.2.0 for 400 bases per second chemistry, with 4mC/5mC and 6mA modified-base calls, and barcode demultiplexing with default barcode trimming); the group should confirm the software version. No analysis of the raw electrical signal was made in this study; the signal enters only through the basecalls and the modified-base calls stored in the alignment files.

Read processing, assembly and binning

Reads of at least 1,000 bp and mean quality Q10 or higher were kept (chopper), and remaining adapter sequence was removed (Porechop_ABI); the filtered reads total Gbp. All ten samples were co-assembled and binned with mmlong2 [16] using the myloasm assembler and differential coverage from the ten samples (extended binning). MAG quality was assessed with CheckM2 [17]: high quality (HQ) means completeness above 90%, contamination below 5% and rRNA and tRNA genes present; medium quality (MQ) means completeness of at least 50% and contamination below 10%. MAGs were classified with GTDB-Tk against GTDB release 226 [18]. Relative abundance of a MAG on a day is the percent of that day's filtered bases that map to its contigs (mean read depth times contig length, from the read mapping of mmlong2). Reads were not classified by a taxonomy tool.

Gene annotation, reference databases and methylation

Genes were annotated with Bakta [19] and its light database (); a gene name rests on a match to a protein cluster with at least % identity. Carbohydrate-active enzymes were searched in the predicted proteins of every MAG with the dbCAN profile models as run by dbCAN3 [20] (domain E-value below , more than % of the model covered); degrading enzymes are glycoside hydrolases (GH), polysaccharide lyases (PL) and carbohydrate esterases (CE), and the substrate category of a family follows dbCAN's family-substrate table. Sugar transporter, bifid-shunt and sugar-conversion genes were found by their Bakta names. Methylation was called from the Dorado alignment files (modkit and Nanomotif) and was used here only to judge unbinned contigs (report Table 8); it is not a result of this article. Taxonomy of unbinned contigs came from Metabuli, run by mmlong2.

Statistics and the day-4 to day-5 comparison

All numbers are descriptive (one person, one sample per day). The change between periods is the log2 of the ratio of the period means with % added to both. For the comparison of day 4 and day 5 we used the MAGs with a mean share of at least % over the ten days (rarer MAGs have noisy daily shares) and computed, per MAG, the fold change (share on day 5 divided by share on day 4) and the absolute change in percentage points. The "day 3-4-5 group" is the set of these MAGs with share on day 3 < day 4 < day 5 and day 6 < day 5; this rule and the cut-off are our choices. Before the analysis we expected that B. angulatum would not be the largest riser, because it rose less than B. adolescentis and one other MAG in the report's day-5 table, and we checked that the day-4 and day-5 shares of B. angulatum equal that table. Analysis code: scripts/45_project_group5.py (course analysis repository); every number on this page is read from the data file written by that script.

Discussion

Measured results. The data are of good quality for this question: % of the filtered bases are in MAGs, % in HQ MAGs, and the B. angulatum genome is one circular contig. The unbinned contigs hold no listed contig above % of a day's bases, though they include very deep short contigs and several chromosome-sized contigs. The abundance of B. angulatum is low and moves strongly between days.

Hypothesis. Our hypothesis, a lower abundance after the shift to the low-carbohydrate diet, is supported only by the period means. The share rose -fold within days 1-5 on a diet that had not changed for about a week before day 1, peaked on day and fell from day 7, so the diet switch cannot explain the rise. The drop after day 6 is compatible with a lower carbohydrate supply if the stool of day 6 still reflected food eaten before the switch, but a particular food on days 4-5, day-to-day variation in a small population, or a relative effect of other bacteria changing are equally compatible. All shares are relative, and one person with one sample per day cannot separate diet from time.

Predicted gene functions. The genome of B. angulatum carries many genes for degrading starch-like alpha-glucans ( GH13 genes, more than in % of the MAGs), together with genes for plant oligosaccharides, fructans, beta-galactosides and the bifid shunt. This is a predicted broad carbohydrate repertoire that a high-carbohydrate vegetarian diet could feed, but gene presence is not expression: we did not measure RNA, protein or substrate use. The smaller number of GH43 genes compared with B. adolescentis and B. longum may be a strain difference rather than a division of resources in this gut.

The group of MAGs that rise together. MAGs, mostly Lachnospiraceae and two bifidobacteria, rose on days 3-5 and fell on day 6. We looked for similar cases in PubMed. In a controlled feeding study, "blooms" of specific bacterial groups occurred within days of a dietary change and were reversed by the next diet, and relatives of Eubacterium rectale (now Agathobacter rectalis, which is in our group) increased on a resistant-starch diet [21]. Resistant-starch interventions increase B. adolescentis in many, but not all, people and studies [14], and diet changes alter the human gut microbiome within days [15]. These reports fit the idea that a starch- or fibre-rich food around days 3-5 favoured several carbohydrate users at once, and the GH13 peak on day 5 is in line with it. They do not show it: no food was recorded, we found no study describing this particular combination of taxa, and rises of similar size occurred on later days. We therefore cannot name a food, and the common rise may also have non-dietary causes such as the part of the stool sampled.

Limitations and information still needed. One person, ten days; diet and time are confounded and carbohydrate and calories changed together; relative abundance is of DNA and not of living cells; gene functions are predictions (Bakta names at 50% identity or more); the day 3-4-5 pattern is a rule chosen by us and was not tested statistically. To be clarified by the group: the extraction method, the sample storage, the flow cell loading and basecaller version, and the accession of the raw reads.
