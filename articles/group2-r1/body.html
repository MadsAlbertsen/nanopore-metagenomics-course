Genome turnover without detectable compensation: carbohydrate-degradation potential of one person's gut community stays within what averaging predicts across a diet switch

Group 2 (original article) and Claude (revision); author names are not published.

How this revision was made

This page is a teaching example written by Claude, an AI model, not by the group. It starts from the group's submitted article and its peer review. A literature search (PubMed, every reference checked) found a gap next to the group's idea: a community whose genomes turn over while its gene content stays stable is usually called functionally redundant, but whether that stability is more than chance has rarely been tested at the level of genomes. The new question is therefore whether the near-constant carbohydrate-degradation potential the group found reflects compensation (rising genomes carry what falling genomes lose) or simply averaging over many changing genomes.

Kept from the group: the data, the questions about rising and falling genomes, the community CAZyme potential and the riser-faller trait comparison, and their careful handling of uncertainty. New: a trait-shuffling null model, a weighting by genes in the DNA, equal-depth read thinning, counts on the binned bases and on species, a step-versus-trend simulation and a family-matched test of whether CAZyme content predicts a genome's response. Each new analysis had its expected range written down before it was run and a positive control; several results fell outside the written range and are reported as such, and one control (the power of the step test) failed. An independent review by a second agent recomputed key numbers and found one error in a null model (fixed) and a missing control (added). Removed: the analysis restricted to the two-fold genomes (circular, as a reviewer noted) and the fibre and calorie mechanisms.

Abstract

Gut communities often keep their gene content while their members change, a pattern read as functional redundancy, but whether such stability exceeds what chance gives has rarely been tested at the level of genomes. We followed one person's stool community over ten consecutive days, five on a high-carbohydrate, high-calorie diet and five on a low-carbohydrate, low-calorie diet (information from the participant), with nanopore long reads and metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs). Composition shifted (Bray-Curtis dissimilarity between periods, and within), and of common MAGs changed at least two-fold; the shift held when every sample was thinned to equal depth. Degrading carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZyme) genes per 1,000 genes of the binned community changed by %. When CAZyme repertoires were shuffled among MAGs, keeping the observed turnover, the typical change was %: the observed change lay at the th percentile, smaller than average but well within chance, also when shuffling only within phylum or family. A genome's CAZyme content did not predict its response within families (Spearman ρ = ). Host-glycan genes rose by %, more than within-family shuffling gives; within family this depended on two Prevotella copri MAGs, and within genus it was not seen. In this person, carbohydrate-degradation potential stayed within what averaging over changing genomes predicts, with no evidence of compensation beyond chance. One person, one switch and relative abundance limit these results to hypotheses.

Keywords: functional redundancy; null model; gut microbiome; diet; nanopore sequencing; metagenome-assembled genomes; carbohydrate-active enzymes

Introduction

Diet changes the human gut microbiome within days. Switching volunteers to an entirely plant-based or animal-based diet changed community structure within a few days [1], daily sampling showed that composition depends on several days of dietary history and that responses differ between people [2], and lowering carbohydrate intake (in a four-week, high-protein weight-loss diet in obese men) reduced the Roseburia/Eubacterium rectale group and faecal butyrate [3]. Whether such changes in membership also change what the community can do is less clear. A high-fibre diet increased microbiome-encoded glycan-degrading CAZymes while diversity stayed stable [4], so gene content can respond to diet.

At the same time, gene content is often more stable than membership. In the Human Microbiome Project, the metagenomic carriage of metabolic pathways was stable among individuals despite large differences in community structure [5], and within a person, metagenomic profiles varied less over time than metatranscriptomic ones [6]. This pattern, many taxa able to do the same thing, is called functional redundancy; it is common in microbial systems and is thought to arise from ecological interactions and environment rather than drift [7]. Functional redundancy has been proposed to underlie the stability of the gut microbiome, but, as Tian and colleagues put it, this hypothesis had not been tested quantitatively [8]; most evidence compares how much taxa and functions vary [5, 7, 9].

A stable community value can come about in two ways. Under compensation, the genomes that rise carry the gene repertoire of the genomes that fall, so the value is more stable than if repertoires were assigned to genomes at random. Under averaging, many genomes change and their differences cancel about as well as random genomes would. The two are told apart with a null model that keeps the observed changes in abundance and only breaks the link between a genome and its genes. This needs gene content per genome together with the abundance of each genome over time: 16S rRNA data have no gene content, and gene-centred short-read profiles do not tie genes to genomes. Traits are also conserved to different depths of the tree [10], so any link between a genome's genes and its response has to be compared within lineages.

Carbohydrate-degradation potential is a good test case. Gut bacteria carry a large and varied CAZyme repertoire [11], and Bacteroidota organise much of theirs in polysaccharide utilisation loci, each usually dedicated to one glycan [12]. Long-read metagenomics recovers complete or near-complete genomes from stool [13, 14], so CAZyme repertoires per genome are less truncated than in fragmented assemblies.

The group behind this article sequenced ten consecutive daily stool samples from one person with nanopore long reads. According to the participant, days 1-5 were on a high-carbohydrate, high-calorie diet and days 6-10 on a low-carbohydrate, low-calorie diet, both vegetarian; the diet of days 6-10 was closer to the person's normal diet, and a similar high-calorie diet had been eaten for about a week before day 1. The group asked which genomes rose or fell, how the community's carbohydrate-degradation potential changed, and which genomic traits differ between rising and falling genomes; they found that the community potential barely changed. Here we ask whether that stability is compensation or averaging, and whether a genome's CAZyme content predicts its response beyond its lineage. We first test whether the compositional shift is robust to sequencing depth, the abundance measure and the timing model, as the reviewers asked.

Materials and methods

Participant, sampling and diet

One adult gave one stool sample on each of ten consecutive days. Diet information comes from the participant (Introduction); there are no food records, no sample from before day 1, no replicate extractions and no RNA, protein, metabolite or cell-count data. Carbohydrate and calorie intake changed together, and the change at day 6 coincided with a return towards the habitual diet. The participant agreed to the use and publication of derived data; no human sequence is published.

DNA extraction, library preparation and sequencing

The DNA extraction method and stool storage are not recorded in the project notes available for this revision. Libraries were prepared with the Rapid Barcoding Kit (SQK-RBK114-24), one barcode per day, and sequenced together on one PromethION flow cell (R10.4.1, run of 20 October 2026). Reads were basecalled with Dorado (model dna_r10.4.1_e8.2_400bps sup v5.2.0, with 4mC/5mC and 6mA modification calling, read splitting on) and demultiplexed with Dorado's default barcode trimming.

Read processing

Reads with a mean quality of at least Q10 and at least 1,000 bp were kept with chopper 0.14.1, and adapters were trimmed and chimeric reads split with Porechop_ABI 0.5.1 (built-in adapter set), followed by the length filter again. This left Gbp, from Gbp (day 2, million reads) to Gbp per day (Fig. 1b lists the depth of each day).

Assembly, binning and MAG quality

The reads of all ten days were co-assembled and binned with mmlong2 1.2.1 [14] (assembler myloasm 0.2.0; binning with the coverage of each day by MetaBAT 2.18 [15], SemiBin 2.2.0, VAMB 5.0.4 and COMEBin 1.0.4, combined with Binette 1.1.2, in up to three rounds). Completeness and contamination were estimated with CheckM2 1.1.0 [16]. A MAG is high quality (HQ) when it is at least 90% complete, at most 5% contaminated and carries 5S, 16S and 23S rRNA genes and at least 18 different tRNAs, following MIMAG [17]; medium quality (MQ) when at least 50% complete and at most 10% contaminated. This gave HQ and MQ MAGs. Taxonomy was assigned with GTDB-Tk 2.5.2 [18] (GTDB release 226). MAG labels give the GTDB name and, in brackets, mmlong2's identifier: c. for a circular contig kept as a genome, s. for a long single linear contig, and 1-3 for the binning round.

Abundance

Each day's reads were mapped to the assembly by mmlong2 with minimap2 2.30 [19] (-ax lr:hq, contigs of at least 3,000 bp), keeping primary alignments only (samtools -F 2308); reads that fit several near-identical genomes are therefore placed once. Mean depth per contig came from jgi_summarize_bam_contig_depths [15] with at least 95% identity. The share of MAG i on day d is the percent of the day's sequenced bases on its contigs,

ai,d = 100 × Σc ∈ i (Lc × depthc,d) / Bd,

with Lc the contig length and Bd the day's bases. Shares are relative abundance [20], not cell numbers [21]. 97.6-98.0% of each day's reads mapped to the co-assembly; human reads were not counted separately. The period change of a MAG is log2((mean a in days 6-10 + 0.01) / (mean a in days 1-5 + 0.01)); MAGs with a mean share of at least 0.1% are called common, and a change of at least two-fold (|log2| ≥ 1) marks a riser or a faller.

Community composition and depth checks

Bray-Curtis dissimilarity between days used all MAG shares rescaled to sum to 1 per day, and principal coordinates analysis (classical scaling) placed the days. To test whether depth drives the shift, the reads per MAG and day (share × reads of the day, assuming equal read length across MAGs within a day) and the unbinned reads were thinned binomially to equal depth, in two settings: (a) to day 5's million reads with day 2 left out, and (b) to day 2's million reads with all days, times each. This tests the read-sampling part of depth, as rarefaction does in amplicon studies [22]; re-mapping subsampled reads was not possible without the server, and depth effects on library preparation or mapping are not covered. The noise floor is the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity between two independent thinnings of the same day. Two further checks repeat the two-fold counts on shares of the binned bases (removing the unbinned fraction from the denominator) and on GTDB species (about 95% average nucleotide identity), summing the MAGs of a species, as a stand-in for dereplication.

Step or trend

For a series of ten values (ordination axis 1, or log2(share + 0.01%) of a MAG), the best split is the division into an early and a late block whose two means explain most of the variation (R²). Because the best of nine splits is chosen from the data, it has an advantage over a single straight line. We measured that advantage with a null model: a straight line plus first-order autoregressive noise, with the line, the noise standard deviation and the lag-1 autocorrelation fitted to the series, simulated times; the observed advantage (best-split R² minus line R²) is placed among the simulated ones. Planted series served as controls: planted lines should pass the 95th percentile rarely, planted steps (a step after day 6 of the size of the observed range) often.

Gene annotation and community CAZyme potential

Genes were predicted with Pyrodigal 3.7.1 [23] and searched against the dbCAN HMMs (release db_v5-2-9_5-5-2026) [24] with pyhmmer 0.12.3 [25]. Degrading families (glycoside hydrolases, polysaccharide lyases, carbohydrate esterases) were grouped by main substrate (starch, fructan, plant fibre, host glycan, mixed, other) with the course's own family-to-substrate table, which follows the substrate annotations of dbCAN3 [24] but is not its prediction method. KOfam profiles [26] and Bakta 1.11.4 [27] (light database v6) gave marker genes and gene names. Per day, the community value of a group of genes is

Vd = 1,000 × Σi ai,d ci / Σi ai,d gi,

with ci the genes of the group and gi all genes of MAG i: genes per 1,000 genes, each MAG weighted by its share times its gene number. Because a share already scales with genome length, this weights large genomes more. As an alternative, each MAG's own rate was weighted by its share alone, V'd = 1,000 × Σ ai,d (ci/gi) / Σ ai,d, which counts genes in proportion to the DNA. The change of a group is the percent difference of the period means of Vd.

Trait-shuffling null model

To ask whether the observed change is smaller than chance, whole gene profiles (all CAZyme counts and the gene number of a MAG) were permuted among the MAGs while the shares stayed in place, and the change was recomputed, times. Permutations were free, within phylum, within family or within genus. The statistic is the percentile of the observed |change| among the permuted |changes|: below the 5th percentile would mean compensation beyond chance, around the middle averaging, above the 95th a change larger than chance. For the share-only weighting, each MAG's own rate ci/gi is permuted and weighted by the share of the MAG it is assigned to. Controls: the identity permutation returns the observed change, identical profiles for all MAGs give no change, a planted link (CAZyme rate rising with a MAG's log2 change) lies above the 95th percentile, and a planted compensation (the risers' CAZyme genes scaled until the community change is zero) lies below the 5th percentile.

Does CAZyme content predict a genome's response?

Over the common MAGs, degrading CAZyme genes per 1,000 genes were compared with the log2 change (Spearman ρ), overall and within families (one pooled correlation of ranks centred within each family with at least three common MAGs, not a separate ρ per family), with p-values from permutations of the changes (free, or within family). The group's riser-faller comparison of 138 curated features (CAZy families and substrate groups, KOfam markers, genome traits, gene-name keyword groups) is kept: Fisher's exact test for presence, Wilcoxon rank-sum test for values, Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (q), MAGs as units, and a repeat within Bacillota.

Statistics and software

The ten days are a time series of one person, not replicates, so changes over time are described with effect sizes and checks, not tested; tests are used only where MAGs are the units (permutations and riser-faller comparison). Expected ranges were written before each new analysis. All analyses ran in Python 3 with numpy and pandas in the course's analysis script (seed 1); each stops if a positive control fails.

Discussion

In ten daily samples from one person, the composition of the gut community shifted between two diet periods, about thirty common genomes changed at least two-fold, and the community's carbohydrate-degradation potential changed by a few percent. The shift survived thinning to equal depth, rescaling to binned bases, species-level units and the use of high-quality genomes only, which answers the main technical concern of the review. Its timing is less certain than the group's article suggested: a split after day 6 fits best, but a trend with noise produces such a split often, so we describe a change that becomes clear from day 7 without calling it a delayed step. Whole-gut transit time is linked to stool composition [28], and stool passed on day 6 partly reflects earlier meals; transit was not measured, so transit, a slow community response and a drift that was under way anyway cannot be told apart.

The new result concerns why the potential stayed stable. Shuffling CAZyme repertoires among genomes, while keeping their trajectories, gave changes of about 4-5% for all degrading genes; the observed change was smaller than that but within the range of chance under every null (the share-only weighting was tested within family only), while a planted compensating community fell clearly below that range. So the stability we see is within what averaging over many changing genomes gives; there is no evidence that the rising genomes were selected for the repertoire of the falling ones, although weaker compensation than the planted one cannot be excluded with one person. This is a narrower statement than "functional redundancy": the community clearly contains many genomes with similar CAZyme sets [5, 7], but in this case that redundancy did not have to be invoked to explain the stability. It agrees with the observation that a genome's CAZyme content did not predict its response within families: the genomes that rose and fell were not chosen by their carbohydrate genes, as far as these data show.

The exception, host-glycan genes, shows how such tests can mislead without lineage-aware nulls. Their rise exceeded within-family shuffling, and the genus null and the run without P. copri suggest this is because GTDB places Prevotella together with Bacteroides and Phocaeicola, and the two P. copri MAGs fell while Phocaeicola rose. P. copri is a complex of clades that differ in carbohydrate metabolism [29], and our two MAGs may be two strains or one genome split in two, which we cannot resolve. A rise in host-glycan genes is compatible with gut bacteria turning to host glycans [30], but it is a change in gene potential among a few genomes, and both diets were vegetarian.

The group's riser-faller comparison is kept and placed in this frame. Fallers are Bacillota, mostly Lachnospiraceae, and risers are largely Bacteroidota; the features that differ between them (SusC/SusD, phosphotransferase systems, propionate and formate genes) are lineage traits, and few survive within Bacillota. Compared with a high-fibre intervention that increased CAZyme genes [4], the change here was small; the two designs differ in diet, length and number of people, so the comparison only shows that the size of such responses varies.

Limitations. One person, one switch and ten days: diet is confounded with time, with the order of the diets, with the return to a habitual diet and with the preceding week of a similar high-calorie diet, and carbohydrate and calories changed together. Shares are relative abundance of DNA, not cell numbers [21], and only binned bases enter the CAZyme values. The depth check covers read sampling, not library or mapping effects, and assumes equal read lengths across genomes within a day. Near-identical genomes share reads through primary-alignment mapping; species-level units gave the same picture. CAZyme calls come from one HMM search and a substrate table of our own; gene potential is not expression [6]. The trait-shuffling null keeps abundance fixed and treats genomes as exchangeable within the chosen groups; other nulls (for example phylogenetic distance-based) could differ. The step-versus-trend test has low power at ten points.

Conclusions

A diet-associated shift in one person's gut community, robust to sequencing depth and to how abundance is measured, left the community's carbohydrate-degradation potential nearly unchanged. A trait-shuffling null model, which can detect planted compensation, places this stability within what averaging over many changing genomes gives; there is no sign that rising genomes compensated for falling ones, and CAZyme content did not predict a genome's response within its family. Genome-resolved time series with replicate people, a baseline and food records could test whether compensation appears under stronger dietary contrasts.

Data availability

The sequencing reads are not public, to protect the privacy of the donor (stool metagenomes contain human DNA); for a journal submission, the reads with human reads removed would be deposited in a controlled-access archive such as the European Genome-phenome Archive. Derived tables (MAG shares per day, CAZyme counts per MAG, all values behind the figures) are in this page's data file in the course repository, and the analysis code (scripts/42_project_group2.py and the np26 Python package) is kept with the course material.
