Staggered genome turnover after a diet change in one person, and a null-model test of carbohydrate-enzyme gene content

Authors: Group 1 (original article) and Claude (revision); author names are not published. Submitted article · peer review and editorial decision. Every number in the text is read from the data behind the figures; hover over a chart for its values.

How this revision was made

The gap. The gene content of the gut microbiome differs much less between people than its species do [5], and this "functional redundancy" is often taken as the reason a community keeps its metabolic potential when its members change [7, 9]. Tian and colleagues note that the link between redundancy and stability has not been tested quantitatively [8]. Within one person, gene content is also more stable than taxonomy over months [6], but such work is gene-centred and sparsely sampled, so the genomes carrying the genes are not followed through a change.

The new question. The group's idea is the starting point: a delayed community shift after the diet switch, read from Bray-Curtis dissimilarity, and the carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZyme) gene content over the ten days. The revision adds the question whether the near-constant community CAZyme gene density is more constant than chance, tested with a null model that keeps the daily abundances and shuffles gene content between genomes.

What was kept and what changed. Kept: the data, the Bray-Curtis timing, the two-fold rule and settled day, the family-matched CAZyme comparison and the honest limitations. New: a step fit per genome with a shuffled-day null, a late contrast (days 1-5 against days 8-10), the Prevotella copri_B collapse with an alignment of the two genomes, compositional checks, in-silico subsampling to one sequencing depth, restricted permutations, pseudo-count sensitivity, the null models and a shift-share decomposition. The methods section is complete, and the ecological comparison with earlier diet studies is new.

What was checked. Each new analysis had its expected range written down before it ran (except two sensitivity checks of the null model, added after the first run showed a result far outside its range, and named as such below) and a positive control that reproduces a known number (for example, the new change rule reproduces the 35 changing MAGs of the submitted article; a planted step is recovered; a genome aligned against itself gives 100%). The analysis code is in the course repository's analysis project (script 41, section R1).

What it is not. This page was written by Claude, an AI model, as teaching material: it shows one way the work could be taken further after peer review. It is not the group's text, and it is not a submitted manuscript. The design is unchanged, so nothing here can show cause.

Abstract

Functional redundancy is widely held to keep the metabolic potential of the gut microbiome stable while its members change, but this has rarely been tested at genome resolution across a perturbation. We followed one person's stool community over ten consecutive days with Oxford Nanopore long-read metagenomes ( metagenome-assembled genomes, MAGs; Gbp after filtering), across a switch from a high-carbohydrate, high-calorie to a low-carbohydrate, low-calorie vegetarian diet after day 5, as reported by the participant. The community stayed close to its early state on day 6 and moved on days 7 and 8; among splits of the ordered days, days 1-6 against days 7-10 separated them best (pseudo-F against for the diet split). Of MAGs with a mean share of at least 0.1%, showed a step of at least two-fold in their daily shares, against expected when the days are shuffled; of these steps lay between days 5 and 8 and on day 7 or later. The largest single change, two closely related Prevotella copri_B MAGs falling from % to % of the sequenced bases, began only after day 7 and was missed by a comparison of the two diet periods. Degrading CAZyme genes per 1,000 genes of the community stayed between and . Permuting gene content between all MAGs gave a day-to-day variation whose median was smaller than the observed one (observed at the th percentile), so the stability is not greater than chance; only within-family permutation put the observation low (th percentile; th without P. copri_B and th with genus as the group, two checks added after the first run). A shift-share decomposition splits the small period change ( to per 1,000 genes) into a fall from changing family shares () and a rise within families (). Genome turnover was staggered over days; the gene content was not buffered beyond chance. One person, one switch: these are hypotheses.

Introduction

Diet changes the human gut community within days. In a controlled feeding study the community changed detectably within 24 hours, while the broad community type stayed the same over ten days [1]. Diets of only animal or only plant products changed it within days, lowering plant-polysaccharide degraders such as Roseburia and Eubacterium rectale [2]. Daily sampling of 34 people showed that the community depends on several days of diet history and that responses differ between people [3]; in mice carrying a human community, a switch to a high-fat, high-sugar diet shifted the community within a single day [4]. Most of this work follows taxa or pooled genes rather than the genomes that carry them.

Gene content is far more similar between people than species composition is: metabolic pathways were carried stably across individuals of the Human Microbiome Project despite very different communities [5], and within one person gene content was more stable than taxonomy over six months [6]. This functional redundancy is widely held to underlie the stability and resilience of the community [7, 9], yet Tian and colleagues point out that the hypothesis has not been tested quantitatively [8]. Testing it through a disturbance needs four things: a perturbation, dense sampling, genomes that tie genes to populations whose abundance is followed, and a null model that says how stable the community's gene content would be if gene content and abundance change were unrelated.

Carbohydrate-active enzymes are a good test case. They are the most diverse functional group of the gut microbiome [39], they differ strongly between families, and their substrates are what a carbohydrate-centred diet change acts on. Lower carbohydrate intake lowered Roseburia and E. rectale and faecal butyrate [10, 11]; Prevotella copri is associated with fibre-rich diets and carbohydrate catabolism [1, 12, 13], and its strains differ in which polysaccharides they use [14, 15]. Long-read metagenomes recover many near-complete genomes from a time series [24, 25], so each enzyme gene can be assigned to a genome whose daily share is known.

Here we follow ten consecutive daily stool samples from one person across one reported diet switch. According to the participant (information from the participant, not measured by us), days 1-5 were a high-carbohydrate, high-calorie and days 6-10 a low-carbohydrate, low-calorie diet; both were vegetarian, the days 6-10 diet was closer to the person's habitual diet, and a similar high-calorie diet had been eaten for about a week before day 1. We ask: (1) when, day by day, does each genome change, and is the timing more ordered than chance? (2) Does a comparison of the two diet periods capture the changes, including the late ones? (3) Does the community's carbohydrate-degrading gene density stay more constant than a null model with reassigned gene content predicts, and which families drive what change there is?

Our expectations, written before the new analyses: about 30-60 genomes with a clear step, most of them between days 5 and 8 and more than the shuffled-day null; a late contrast finding 40-60 changing genomes, among them both P. copri_B MAGs; and a community gene density whose variation lies inside the null distribution, that is, no buffering beyond chance.

Materials and methods

Participant, sampling and diet

One adult provided one stool sample per day on ten consecutive days (n = 10 samples, one person). The diet is information from the participant: days 1-5 high in carbohydrate and calories, days 6-10 low in both, both periods vegetarian, the days 6-10 diet closer to the habitual diet, and a similar high-calorie diet for about a week before day 1. No food records, no sample before day 1 or after day 10, and no record of the time of day at which a sample was passed; transit time, metabolites and cell counts were not measured.

DNA extraction, library preparation and sequencing

The extraction kit and any bead-beating step are not recorded in the project notes and cannot be stated here; this is a limitation for reproduction. Libraries were made with the Oxford Nanopore Rapid Barcoding Kit SQK-RBK114-24 (one barcode per day) and sequenced together on one PromethION R10.4.1 flow cell. Basecalling used Dorado 2.1.2, model sup v5.2.0 (r10.4.1 e8.2 400 bps) with modified-base models for 4mC/5mC and 6mA, read splitting on and adapter trimming off; reads were demultiplexed with dorado demux, which trims the barcode region.

Read processing

Reads were filtered with chopper 0.14.1 [37] to mean Q at least 10 and length at least 1,000 bp, then adapter-trimmed and split with Porechop_ABI 0.5.1 [38] and filtered to at least 1,000 bp again. This left million reads and Gbp (0.25% of bases removed by trimming). Per day: - Gbp, read N50 - kb and mean read quality Q-Q (Table 1). Reads were not subsampled for the main analysis; the effect of the unequal depth is tested below.

Assembly, binning and MAG quality

All ten read sets were co-assembled and binned with mmlong2 1.2.1 [24] (myloasm , minimap2 [26], CoverM , MetaBAT2 [27], binning with differential coverage over the ten samples). This gave MAGs: high-quality and medium-quality by the MIMAG tiers [31] as implemented in mmlong2, with median CheckM2 [28] completeness % and contamination % (high quality) and % and % (medium quality); lowest completeness %, highest contamination %. MAGs are a single contig and a single circular contig. Taxonomy was assigned with GTDB-Tk [29] against GTDB release [30]. The MAGs were not dereplicated: four species are represented by two MAGs each among the genomes discussed here, and we compare those pairs by alignment (below) instead of merging them. Median coding density is % (5th percentile %), which is what is expected for assemblies without frameshift-causing indel errors; the assembly is the polished mmlong2 output.

Share of a MAG on each day

Each day's filtered reads were mapped with minimap2 (preset lr:hq, as mmlong2 runs it) to the contigs of the co-assembly, keeping primary alignments. Mean depth per contig and day was computed by MetaBAT2's jgi_summarize_bam_contig_depths [27] (alignments of at least 95% identity, about 75 bp ignored at each contig end). The share of MAG m on day d is

sharem,d (%) = 100 × Σc ∈ m (depthc,d × lengthc) / basesd,

where the sum runs over the contigs of the MAG and basesd is all bases of that day's filtered reads. Bases rather than read counts are used because read lengths differ. The MAGs hold -% of each day's bases; the rest is unbinned and left out. A share of DNA is not a cell count, and shares are compositional: a fall of one genome raises every other share [20]. The depth tool was written for short reads; it is used here only to sum base coverage per contig, and the resulting pooled abundances agree with CoverM (Spearman 0.97 in the project's overview analysis).

Bray-Curtis dissimilarity and reference states

Bray-Curtis dissimilarity was computed between every pair of days on the MAG shares, each day first rescaled to sum to one over the MAGs, so the varying binned fraction does not enter. Each day was also compared with three reference states: day 1, the mean composition of days 2-5 and the mean of days 8-10. Period means are used because one day can differ from the next by 0.28 without any diet change; day 1 is kept separate as a check that the early state is stable. The late state is the second block of similar days seen in Figure 1, so that choice was made after looking at the data.

Change between periods, settled day and the new step fit

For a MAG with a mean share of at least % over the ten days, the period change is log2 of the ratio of its mean share in the second period to that in the first, with percentage points added to both so that absent MAGs give a finite value; a change of at least two-fold counts as "changing". The settled day is the first day from which the share stays on the new side of the midpoint between the two period means. Both are descriptions, not tests: there is no standard threshold, differential-abundance tests disagree across data sets [22] and treat samples as independent replicates, which neighbouring days of one person are not, and one person's community varies from day to day without any intervention [23].

New in this revision. Because period means hide changes that happen late, each common MAG's log2 share series was also fitted with a single step: the ten days are split at each of the nine day boundaries into two segments with their own means, and the split with the smallest sum of squares is kept. A MAG counts as a "step MAG" if the step is at least one log2 unit and the two means explain at least half of the variance of its series. The null expectation was obtained by repeating the whole fit on random orders of the ten days (the same order for all MAGs in a round), which keeps each MAG's values and destroys only their order.

Carbohydrate-active enzymes

Genes of all MAGs were predicted with Pyrodigal 3.7.1 [33], a binding to Prodigal [32], in metagenome mode, which reproduces the gene calls of the annotation pipeline exactly (same stop codon for all 1,017,373 annotated coding sequences), giving genes. CAZyme domains were found with PyHMMER 0.12.3 [34] (HMMER3 algorithms [35]) against the dbCAN HMM database, release , with run_dbcan's HMMER settings: domain i-Evalue below 1e-15, HMM coverage above 0.35, overlapping hits resolved to the better i-Evalue. This is the HMMER step only; run_dbcan's recommended set requires two of three methods, so some calls here would not survive that consensus. Degrading families are the glycoside hydrolases, polysaccharide lyases and carbohydrate esterases ( genes); glycosyl transferases and binding modules are left out. Substrate groups (plant fibre, starch, fructan, host glycan, mixed, other) are our own mapping, not a dbCAN3 prediction: each family is assigned to a group when at least two thirds of its rows in dbCAN3's family-substrate table [36] fall into that group, else to "mixed". The table is published with the analysis code. A gene with domains in two groups counts in both; "all degrading" counts each gene once.

Per MAG, the measure is degrading CAZyme genes per 1,000 predicted genes, so it does not grow with genome size or completeness. For a set of MAGs on one day, the community density is

density (per 1,000 genes) = 1000 × Σm sharem,d × countm / Σm sharem,d × genesm,

that is, each MAG is weighted by its share of the DNA times its number of genes. Gene content is potential, not activity.

Materials and methods (continued)

Null models for the community gene density (new)

Two permutation nulls test whether the observed day-to-day stability of the community density is more than chance. The daily MAG shares are kept exactly as observed; only the pairs (degrading CAZyme genes, total genes) are shuffled between MAGs, times. Null A shuffles among all MAGs: it asks what the density series would look like if gene content were unrelated to which genomes rose and fell. Null B shuffles only within a GTDB family: it keeps each family's gene content and asks the same question within that constraint. For each permutation we record the standard deviation of the density over the ten days and the difference between the period means, and report where the observed values fall in those distributions. Positive controls: the identity permutation reproduces the observed series; gene content planted to track the change (density 50 per 1,000 genes in rising and 20 in all other MAGs) puts the observed variation above the 95th percentile of null A; a constant density in every MAG gives no variation at all.

Shift-share decomposition (new)

The change of the community density between two states is split into three parts: a between-family part (family weights change, family densities held at the first state), a within-family part (family densities change, weights held at the first state) and their interaction. A family's weight is its share of the abundance-weighted genes. The three parts add up to the total change, which the code checks.

Robustness: composition, depth, permutation scheme and pseudo-count (new)

Four checks address how much the change list depends on choices. (i) Compositional effects: the change list was recomputed after removing the two Prevotella copri_B MAGs and rescaling the remaining shares to the same binned total, and again as a log ratio against a reference frame [19] of the core MAGs that change less than 1.5-fold (-% of the bases on every day). (ii) Sequencing depth: each day was subsampled in silico to the reads of the shallowest day ( reads, day ) by drawing reads multinomially from that day's MAG shares ( replicates); Bray-Curtis dissimilarities and the change list were recomputed. The control subsamples each day to its own read number and must reproduce the observed dissimilarities. (iii) Permutation scheme: besides the exact permutation over all 252 ways of labelling five of ten days [21], pseudo-F was computed for the nine splits of the ordered days into a first block and the rest, and for the ten cyclic shifts of the period labels, both of which respect the time order. (iv) Pseudo-count: the change list was recomputed with 0.001, 0.01 (the value used) and 0.1 percentage points.

Comparing the two MAGs of one species (new)

Each pair of MAGs assigned to the same species among the changing genomes, and the two P. copri_B MAGs, were aligned against each other with minimap2 (-c -x asm20, primary alignments of at least 1 kb). We report the fraction of the smaller genome covered and the gap-compressed identity (block-weighted mean of minimap2's de tag), with a genome aligned against itself as the positive control (% identity over % of the genome).

Statistics and reporting

The ten days are a time series from one person, not replicates. Permutation tests treat days as exchangeable, which neighbouring days are not, so they are read as descriptions; elsewhere we report effect sizes and how much they depend on single days (leave-one-day-out, as in the submitted article). Units are stated throughout: shares are percent of a day's sequenced bases; gene densities are genes per 1,000 predicted genes; changes are log2 ratios of period means. Software: mmlong2 , myloasm , minimap2 , CoverM , GTDB-Tk (GTDB ), CheckM2 1.1.0, MetaBAT2 2.18, chopper 0.14.1, Porechop_ABI 0.5.1, Dorado 2.1.2 (sup v5.2.0), Pyrodigal 3.7.1, PyHMMER 0.12.3, dbCAN HMMs , Python 3.12 with NumPy, pandas and SciPy.

Which genomes change is not explained by their enzyme gene content

The changing MAGs carry different numbers of degrading CAZyme genes: falling MAGs a median of per 1,000 genes (range ), rising MAGs (), and the rising MAGs above every falling one are all Bacteroides or Phocaeicola (Figure 4). Compared within a family, however, changing and non-changing genomes look alike: rising Bacteroidaceae against for non-changing ones (host glycan against , plant fibre against ), and falling Lachnospiraceae against for non-changing ones. The difference between rising and falling genomes therefore follows family membership, not gene content: Bacteroides and Phocaeicola carry large, broad enzyme sets, Lachnospiraceae smaller ones, whether or not they changed.

The direction of the taxonomic change matches earlier diet work. Of the falling MAGs, are Lachnospiraceae, among them Roseburia intestinalis and Agathobacter rectalis (named Eubacterium rectale in older work); of the rising MAGs are Bacteroides or Phocaeicola. Lower carbohydrate intake reduced Roseburia and E. rectale together with faecal butyrate in controlled diets [10, 11], and an animal-based diet raised Bacteroides and lowered the same plant-polysaccharide degraders [2]. The fall of Prevotella copri_B fits the association of Prevotella with carbohydrate- and fibre-rich diets [1, 12]. Our switch lowered carbohydrate and calories together, so this agreement is with the direction, not with a cause.

Across the ten days the community carried between and degrading CAZyme genes per 1,000 genes (mean in days 1-5 and in days 6-10; Figure 5a). That is a standard deviation of per 1,000 genes, % of the mean, and the difference between the periods, , is smaller than the spread within days 1-5 alone (-). Is that stability more than the turnover allows? Shuffling gene content between all MAGs while keeping the daily shares (null A, permutations) gives a median standard deviation of per 1,000 genes (5th to 95th percentile ). The observed lies at the th percentile of that distribution: the community's density varies more than a typical random assignment of gene content, not less (Figure 5b). The same holds for the period difference (th percentile of the absolute differences) and for plant-fibre (th), host-glycan (th) and starch genes (th). Our expectation before running was a percentile between 10 and 60 (no buffering) and a null median of 1.5-3 per 1,000 genes; both results are outside those ranges (th percentile, median ): the null varies less than we guessed, and the observed series sits above its middle rather than inside the expected band. We have not explained why the guess was too high; the code was checked with the positive controls in the Methods.

Shuffling only within families (null B) gives a different picture: the median standard deviation is then () and the observation falls at the th percentile, far below the 30-70 we expected. The reason is not redundancy across the community but one group: within Bacteroidaceae the gene density ranges from about 16 to 94 per 1,000 genes, and the two genomes that fall most, P. copri_B, sit at the low end of that range ( and ) while the genomes that replace them sit higher. Swapping those values between family members therefore produces large swings. Two sensitivity checks were added after seeing this first run. With the two P. copri_B MAGs removed, the observed variation is and its within-family percentile ; grouping by genus instead of family gives the th percentile. So P. copri_B explains much of the extreme value, but not all of it: in both checks the observation is still in the lowest tenth of the within-group null. Because this null was not our planned test, depends on the grouping and stays at the edge once the largest mover is removed, we do not read it as evidence of functional buffering; it is a lead for a design with more people. The honest statement is: in this person, over these ten days, the community's carbohydrate-degrading gene density stayed within about % of its mean, and that stability is what the observed abundance changes produce with gene content assigned at random.

Discussion

Following one person day by day at genome resolution gives a picture that a two-period comparison flattens. The whole community stayed in its early state on day 6 and moved over days 7 and 8, and the best split of the ordered days put day 6 with the early block. Below that average, genomes changed on their own days: steps concentrate between days 5 and 8, far more of them than the same values in random order produce, but genomes stepped before the switch and at day 7 or later, and the largest change of the whole series, the fall of the two Prevotella copri_B genomes, began only after day 7. A staggered response is what one would expect if genomes differ in growth rate and in how directly their substrates changed, but with one switch in one person we cannot separate that from the ordinary day-to-day movement of a single community [23].

A one- to two-day delay is consistent with the time food needs to pass through the gut [16, 17] and with feeding studies in which the community changed within one to a few days [1, 2, 4], and the dependence on several days of diet history [3] fits a further day. It is only consistent, not shown: transit time was not measured, the time of day of each sample is not recorded, and a sample passed in the morning of day 6 largely reflects day 5's food, so no biological delay need be involved at all. The alternatives are equally open. Days 6-10 are both the new diet and later days; the new diet was also a return towards the person's habitual diet after about a week of a high-calorie diet, so part of the movement may be a return to a usual state rather than a response to less carbohydrate; carbohydrate and calories changed together; and the largest single step of the series lies inside the first period.

The functional result is the one we set out to test. The community's degrading CAZyme gene density stayed within about % of its mean while a quarter to a third of the binned DNA changed hands, which looks like textbook functional redundancy [5, 7, 9]. The null model says otherwise: with gene content shuffled between genomes and the same daily abundances, a density that stable or more stable is common, and the observed series is at the th percentile. The reason is simple once stated: the community average is taken over hundreds of genomes, so even large movements of a few per cent of the DNA move it little, whatever gene content those genomes carry. Our within-family null is more extreme only because Bacteroidaceae span a wide range of gene densities and the genomes that fell sit at its low end. We know of no earlier test of the redundancy-stability link of this kind on a dense genome-resolved series [8]; in one person it does not support buffering beyond what averaging gives. The shift-share decomposition replaces the causal wording of the earlier draft: between-family and within-family changes were of similar size and opposite sign, so they partly cancelled; that is an accounting of the change, not a mechanism.

The taxa that moved agree with the diet literature in direction: Lachnospiraceae including Roseburia intestinalis and Agathobacter rectalis (E. rectale) fell, Bacteroides and Phocaeicola rose, and Prevotella copri_B collapsed, matching reductions of Roseburia and E. rectale on reduced-carbohydrate diets [10, 11], the rise of Bacteroides on animal-based diets [2] and the association of Prevotella with fibre-rich diets [1, 12]. The two P. copri_B genomes have slightly different plant-fibre gene densities ( and per 1,000 genes; their gene sets were not compared) and fell in step with each other; strains of this species differ in polysaccharide use [13, 14, 15].

Limitations

One person, one switch, one sample per day: the timing is described, not tested, and responses differ between people [3]. Diet is confounded with time and with a return to the habitual diet, and carbohydrate and calories changed together. The data are relative abundances of DNA on the -% of bases that are binned; a fall of one genome raises all other shares [18, 20], which is why we repeated the analysis without P. copri_B and against a reference frame [19]. MAGs were not dereplicated, so a species with two MAGs is counted twice and reads may be split between them. Gene content is potential: we have no transcripts, no metabolites and no enzyme assays, and dbCAN HMM hits without the consensus of three methods overcall some families. Substrate groups are our own mapping. The permutation tests treat neighbouring days as exchangeable. There is no sample before day 1 or after day 10, so neither the habitual state nor the end of the change is observed, and the wet-lab record is incomplete (extraction kit not recorded). Strain-level change within genomes could not be followed: the variant file pools all ten days.

Conclusions

In ten daily long-read metagenomes from one person across a diet switch, the community moved one to two days after the switch, and the genomes beneath that average moved on their own days, several of them only at the end of the series. A comparison of the two diet periods misses those late changes, including the largest one in the data. The community's carbohydrate-degrading gene density stayed nearly constant, but a null model shows that this stability is what the same abundance changes produce with gene content assigned at random, so it is not evidence of functional buffering. Testing that idea properly needs several people, samples before and after the change, and ideally expression or metabolite data; the analysis shown here is ready for such a design.

Data availability

The sequencing reads are not public, to protect the privacy of the sample donor: stool metagenomes carry human DNA. For a journal submission the route would be controlled access, for example a repository such as the European Genome-phenome Archive, with human reads removed. The derived tables behind every figure are published with this page and can be read by hovering over the charts, and the analysis code (script 41, section R1, with the shared functions it uses) is distributed with the course material.
