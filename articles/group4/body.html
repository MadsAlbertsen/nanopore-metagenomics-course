Functional stability despite taxonomic turnover: a gut microbiome buffered by redundancy across genomes and within populations

Authors: Group 4, Nanopore metagenomics course 2026 (author names are not published on this site). Built from the group's project report: version 2, version 1. Figure numbers in this article are its own.

Abstract

Diet changes gut microbiome composition within days, but it is less clear whether the genes carried by the community change as much. Daily stool samples from one person were sequenced for ten days with Oxford Nanopore long reads, during a high-carbohydrate, high-calorie vegetarian diet on days 1-5 and a low-carbohydrate, low-calorie vegetarian diet on days 6-10 (dietary information provided by the participant). The reads were assembled and binned into genomes (MAGs), and carbohydrate-degrading potential was quantified as CAZyme genes per 1,000 genes. Species composition shifted after a lag of about one day: day 6 resembled days 1-5, and the distance to the days 1-5 mean rose from day 7 (Bray-Curtis , then -). The CAZyme gene profile changed far less (between-period Bray-Curtis against ). Genes carried by more MAGs varied less from day to day (Spearman for CAZy families, for all named genes). Lachnospiraceae accounted for % of the species change but % of the CAZyme change; common Bacteroidota carried a median of degrading CAZyme genes per 1,000 genes against for Bacillota. MAGs with more variant sites had slightly steadier shares (Spearman ), and MAGs that rose were mostly those with few. These results are consistent with buffering of gene potential by redundancy across MAGs and, more weakly, by strain diversity within MAGs. The data derive from one person, describe gene potential and not activity, and lack a pre-intervention baseline.

Introduction

Diet is one of the strongest short-term influences on the human gut microbiome: an animal-based diet changed community composition a single day after the new food reached the distal gut [1], and in 34 people sampled daily composition depended on several days of dietary history [2]. Across people, however, the metabolic pathways of the metagenome are far more even than community structure [3], and it remains unclear whether the carbohydrate-degrading gene potential of a single gut follows its composition when the diet changes within days.

Here we show that a dietary switch reshuffles gut microbiome composition within one to two days while carbohydrate-degrading gene potential remains stable, consistent with buffering by redundancy at two nested levels: shared gene families across genomes (functional redundancy [4], here the carbohydrate-active enzymes, CAZymes, of the CAZy database [5]) and, more weakly, strain diversity within them [6]. We examined this with ten daily nanopore metagenomes from one person across one dietary switch, which track species composition and carbohydrate gene content at the same time at genome resolution (MAG-resolved).

Materials and methods

Sampling and dietary intervention. One stool sample was collected from one adult participant on each of ten consecutive days. Dietary information was provided by the participant: days 1-5 comprised a high-carbohydrate, high-calorie vegetarian diet and days 6-10 a low-carbohydrate, low-calorie vegetarian diet. The diet of days 6-10 was closer to the habitual diet of the participant, and a similar high-calorie diet had been consumed for about a week before day 1. Carbohydrate and energy intake changed together. An overview of the bioinformatic workflow is given in Supplementary Figure 1.

Sequencing. Sequencing libraries were prepared with the Oxford Nanopore Rapid Barcoding Kit (SQK-RBK114-24), and the ten barcoded samples were sequenced on one PromethION flow cell (R10.4.1 chemistry; flow cell PBM34154). Reads were basecalled with Dorado 2.1.2 using the super-accuracy model v5.2.0 (R10.4.1, 400 bases per second) with modified-base calling (4mC, 5mC and 6mA), without adapter trimming and with read splitting enabled, and were demultiplexed with dorado demux using default barcode trimming. The run yielded Gbp, - Gbp per sample.

Read processing and quality control. Reads were filtered with chopper to a mean quality of at least Q10 and a length of at least 1,000 bp; residual adapter sequences were trimmed and chimeric reads were split with Porechop_ABI (built-in adapter database), after which the length filter was applied again. The retained reads (about million reads, Gbp) were used for assembly. Before filtering, read N50 was - kb and the median read quality Q- per sample.

Genome-resolved metagenomics. Reads of all ten samples were co-assembled and binned with the mmlong2 workflow v1.2.1 [7] (assembler myloasm v0.2.0 within mmlong2; binning mode extended). Differential-coverage binning used one coverage profile per sample plus a pooled profile. Contigs were split at likely misassemblies and contigs shorter than 3 kb were discarded, leaving contigs (N50 kb). Binning yielded MAGs. The share of a MAG on a given day was calculated from the read depth of its contigs on that day, weighted by contig length, as a fraction of the DNA of the day. MAGs with a mean share of at least 0.1% over the ten days were defined as common ( MAGs).

Genome quality assessment. Completeness and contamination were estimated with CheckM2. A MAG was classified as high quality at a completeness of at least 90%, a contamination of at most 5%, 5S, 16S and 23S rRNA genes, and at least 18 tRNAs for the 20 amino acids, and as medium quality at a completeness of at least 50% and a contamination of at most 10%. This gave high-quality and medium-quality MAGs. Taxonomy was assigned with GTDB-Tk v2.5.2 against GTDB release 226.

Gene prediction and annotation. Genes of all MAGs were annotated with Bakta v1.11.4 (database v6.0, light) in metagenome mode, giving coding sequences. Genes were grouped by Bakta product name; only specific names (not hypothetical, domain-only or family-only) were counted ( of genes in names) [8].

CAZyme gene potential. Genes were predicted for the CAZyme and marker-gene searches with Pyrodigal v3.7.1 (single mode, trained on each MAG). CAZymes were identified by searching the dbCAN HMM profiles (run_dbcan release db_v5-2-9_5-5-2026, 875 profiles) with pyhmmer v0.12.3 [9] using the run_dbcan criteria (domain i-Evalue below 1e-15, HMM coverage above 0.35). Only glycoside hydrolase, polysaccharide lyase and carbohydrate esterase families (degrading families, found) were analysed. Families were assigned to plant fibre, starch, fructan or host glycans when at least two thirds of their substrate annotations in the dbCAN family-substrate mapping fell into that group, and otherwise to mixed or other groups. Thirty KOfam marker genes (butyrate, propionate, acetate, hydrogen and related functions) were searched with the KOfam profiles and their score thresholds (ko_list of 26 October 2026) [10]. For each day, gene potential was expressed as genes per 1,000 genes of the binned community, with each MAG weighted by its share of the DNA times its number of genes.

Strain diversity. Single-base variant sites were called on the reads of all ten days pooled with Longshot [11] (at least 6 reads at a position, the alternative base in at least 3 reads and at least 10% of them). The variant share of a MAG was the number of variant sites divided by its length. Variant shares therefore describe the whole study, not single days.

Statistical analysis. Compositions were compared by Bray-Curtis dissimilarity, and days were ordinated by principal coordinates analysis. Day-to-day variation of a family, gene name or MAG was the coefficient of variation (CV, %) over the ten days, and redundancy was the number of common MAGs carrying a family. Associations were tested with Spearman rank correlation; variation of function sets was compared at the same number of carrier MAGs, with a 95% interval from resampling the functions 2,000 times. The change between the period means was split exactly into the part of each MAG. MAG changes were log2 ratios of mean shares between days 1-6 and days 7-10; Figure 2c shows the 20 largest changes and 5 abundant MAGs that changed by less than 1.5-fold. For the strain analysis, correlations were repeated with sequencing depth removed (partial Spearman) and within the two largest phyla, and the split of rising MAGs over thirds of the variant share was tested by shuffling variant shares within fifths of depth (5,000 permutations); this analysis was exploratory (about correlations were examined). Expected results were written down before the analyses.

Results

Species composition shifted detectably about one day after the dietary switch (from day 7) and drifted further from the days 1-5 samples thereafter. Day 6, the first sample on the new diet, still resembled days 1-5: its Bray-Curtis distance to the days 1-5 mean was , inside the range of days 1-5 (-). In the ordination, axis 1 held % of the variation, days 1-6 lay on one side and days 7-10 on the other, and the days from day 7 followed a steady path away from days 1-5 (Figure 1b). The drift was directional, not erratic: neighbouring days differed about equally in the two periods (Bray-Curtis in days 1-5, in days 6-10), while the distance to days 1-5 grew after the switch. Days 1-5 showed no trend with time. So the community did change within a day or two of the switch, and the question that follows is why the gene potential did not follow.

Despite this compositional shift, the carbohydrate-degrading gene potential of the community changed little, and its stability scaled with how many genomes shared each gene family. The median CAZy family varied % from day to day against % for the median common MAG. The more common MAGs carried a family, the less it varied (Spearman over families; Figure 1c). Redundancy is therefore a candidate reason why function stayed steady while composition did not, and the next step is to see which taxa carried the composition change.

Discussion

These results indicate that the gut microbiome of this person absorbed a substantial compositional change without a large change in carbohydrate-degrading gene potential, consistent with the taxa that changed most after the switch contributing least to that potential. The species composition was reshuffled (Bray-Curtis between the period means ) while the gene potential changed little (): Lachnospiraceae accounted for % of the species change, and the decline of their share removed few CAZyme genes. Stability was greater for genes carried by many MAGs, consistent with redundancy as a first level of buffering: a gene present in many genomes is unlikely to be lost when a few of them decline. This agrees with the Human Microbiome Project finding, across people, that metagenomic pathways are more even than community structure [3] and with the concept of functional redundancy [4], and shows the same pattern within one person over ten days.

The timing agrees with earlier daily-sampling studies. A lag of about one day, with day 6 still similar to days 1-5, matches the compositional change one day after new food reached the distal gut [1] and the dependence on several days of dietary history [2]. It is not a measurement of the lag, because transit time is unknown. The one-day response in the controlled study applied to an animal-based diet, whereas a plant-based diet gave no significant shift [1], and both diets here were vegetarian. Day-to-day variation without a diet change can also be large in healthy adults [12], and Bray-Curtis values are not directly comparable between studies.

A second level of buffering, strain diversity within MAGs, is a hypothesis. A more mixed population may hold its share because another strain can take over when one declines, as redundancy does for genes one level down. The supporting correlation is weak (Spearman ), absent within Bacteroidota, and exploratory. Variant calls derive from the reads of all days pooled, so changes of the strain mixture between days cannot be seen, and variant sites may include reads from related species. The rise of mostly clonal MAGs can also be explained without buffering, by clonal populations growing from low numbers. Per-day variant data are needed to distinguish them.

Limitations. The study covers one person and ten days, so the results describe this gut and are not tested. The data are relative abundances (shares of the sequenced DNA) and gene potential, not gene expression or activity: the presence of a gene does not show its use. A pre-intervention baseline is lacking: a similar high-calorie diet had been consumed for about a week before day 1, so days 1-5 are not a neutral reference, and days 6-10 were closer to the habitual diet. Carbohydrate and energy intake changed together, diet and time are confounded, and the data do not show that diet caused the changes. The evidence for the lower CAZyme content of Lachnospiraceae rests on the 25 MAGs of Figure 2c, two of the five stable reference MAGs are Lachnospiraceae, and the 1.5-fold limit and the choice of the top 20 are arbitrary.
