A dietary switch reshuffles gut microbiome composition, but carbohydrate-degrading gene potential barely moves: a ten-day long-read metagenomic time series

Authors: Group 4, Nanopore metagenomics course 2026 (author names are not published on this site). Built from the group's project report: version 2, version 1. Figure numbers in this article are its own.

Abstract

Diet changes gut microbiome composition within days, but it is less clear whether the genes the community carries change as much. We followed one person for ten days with daily stool samples, on a high-carbohydrate, high-calorie vegetarian diet on days 1-5 and a low-carbohydrate, low-calorie vegetarian diet on days 6-10 (information from the data owner). Nanopore long reads were assembled and binned into genomes (MAGs), and the potential to degrade carbohydrates was counted as CAZyme genes per 1,000 genes. Species composition shifted after a lag of about one day: day 6 still resembled days 1-5, and the distance to the days 1-5 mean rose from day 7 (Bray-Curtis , then -). The CAZyme gene profile moved far less (between-period Bray-Curtis against ). Genes carried by more MAGs varied less from day to day (Spearman for CAZy families, for all named genes). Lachnospiraceae accounted for % of the species change but % of the CAZyme change; common Bacteroidota carried a median of degrading CAZyme genes per 1,000 genes against for Bacillota. MAGs with more variant sites had slightly steadier shares (Spearman ), and MAGs that rose were mostly those with few. We read this as redundancy across MAGs, and possibly strain diversity within MAGs, buffering gene potential. The data are one person, gene potential and not activity, with no baseline before the diet change.

Introduction

Diet is one of the strongest short-term influences on the human gut microbiome. In a controlled study, an animal-based diet changed gut community composition a single day after the new food reached the distal gut, and plant-polysaccharide-degrading Firmicutes such as Roseburia and Eubacterium rectale fell [1]. With daily shotgun metagenomes from 34 people, composition depended on several days of dietary history [2]. The speed of the compositional response is therefore known, and with daily samples it can be followed day by day.

Composition and function need not move together. Across people, the Human Microbiome Project found that the metabolic pathways of the metagenome were far more even than community structure [3]. A likely reason is functional redundancy: many coexisting microbes encode the same function [4]. For carbohydrate degradation, the relevant genes are the carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) of the CAZy database [5]. If many genomes carry a CAZyme gene, one genome falling can be offset by another rising, and the community's gene potential stays steady while its composition changes. Within a species, a population made of several strains might add a second layer of the same kind [8].

We tested this idea in one person, using long-read metagenomics with genome-resolved assembly. We asked four questions, in order. When does the species composition shift after a dietary switch, and does it drift in one direction? Is the CAZyme gene potential stable, and does its stability depend on how many genomes carry a gene? Which taxa move most, and how many CAZyme genes do they carry? And are populations with more strain diversity steadier than clonal ones? Our contribution is daily, MAG-resolved nanopore metagenomics in a single individual, which tracks species composition and carbohydrate gene content at the same time at genome resolution.

Materials and methods

Sample and diet. One person gave one stool sample on each of ten days. Information from the data owner: days 1-5 were a high-carbohydrate, high-calorie diet and days 6-10 a low-carbohydrate, low-calorie diet, both vegetarian; the days 6-10 diet was closer to the person's normal diet; a similar high-calorie diet was eaten for about a week before day 1. Carbohydrate and calories changed together.

Sequencing and genomes. The ten samples were sequenced on one PromethION flow cell (Oxford Nanopore, Rapid Barcoding Kit, R10.4.1). Reads were assembled and binned with the mmlong2 workflow [6] into MAGs; a MAG's share of a day is its share of that day's DNA (bases, not cells). The common MAGs have a mean share of at least 0.1%. Taxonomy is from GTDB.

Gene potential. Degrading CAZyme genes were found with dbCAN [7] and counted per family. For each day, genes per 1,000 genes of the binned community weighted each MAG by its share of the day's DNA times its number of genes. Named genes ( names) are those with a specific Bakta name [9].

Distances and stability. Compositions were compared with Bray-Curtis dissimilarity. Each day was compared with the mean of days 1-5 (for days 1-5, the mean of the other four) and ordinated by principal coordinates analysis. Day-to-day variation of a family or MAG was its coefficient of variation (CV, %) over the ten days; redundancy was the number of common MAGs carrying a family. The change between the two periods was split exactly into the part of each MAG (species mix: its part of the Bray-Curtis between the period means; CAZyme profile: its part of the summed change of all degrading families). MAG changes between days 1-6 and days 7-10 are log2 ratios of mean shares; the MAGs in Figure 2b are the 20 with the largest change and 5 of the most abundant stable ones (within 1.5-fold, our limit).

Strain diversity. Single-base variant sites were called with Longshot [10] on the reads of all ten days pooled; the variant share of a MAG is its variant sites over its length. This is a property of the whole study, not of single days. Spearman rank correlations are reported with the day-to-day CV. This part is exploratory (about correlations were looked at). Expected results were written down before the analysis, and the Bray-Curtis values reproduce the course overview analysis.

Results

The species composition shifted about one day after the switch and then moved in one direction. Day 6, the first sample on the new diet, still resembled days 1-5: its Bray-Curtis distance to the days 1-5 mean was , inside the range of days 1-5 (-). The distance rose from day 7 () and reached , and on days 8-10 (Figure 1a). In the ordination, axis 1 held % of the variation, days 1-6 lay on one side and days 7-10 on the other, and the days from day 7 followed a steady path away from days 1-5 (Figure 1b). The drift was directional, not erratic: neighbouring days differed about equally in the two periods (Bray-Curtis in days 1-5, in days 6-10), while the distance to days 1-5 grew after the switch. Days 1-5 showed no trend with time. So the community did change within a day or two of the switch, and the question that follows is why the gene potential did not follow.

The CAZyme gene potential was far steadier than the species composition. The Bray-Curtis between the period means was for the CAZyme profile and for the species mix, and each day's distance to the days 1-5 mean stayed between and (Figure 1a). Stability depended on redundancy: the more common MAGs carried a family, the less it varied (Spearman over families; Figure 1c). The same held for all named gene functions (), whose most widely carried names ( names in more than 100 common MAGs) varied by a median of only %. Redundancy is therefore a candidate reason why function stayed steady while composition did not, and the next step is to see which taxa carried the composition change.

Discussion

In this person, a switch between two vegetarian diets reshuffled the species composition (Bray-Curtis between the period means ) while the carbohydrate-degrading gene potential changed little (). Our interpretation, consistent with the data but not shown by them, is that the taxa that moved most were never the main carriers of these genes: the Lachnospiraceae made % of the species change, and a fall in their share took few CAZyme genes with it. The stability was larger for genes carried by many MAGs, which fits redundancy as the first level of buffering: a gene in many genomes is unlikely to be lost when a few of them fall. This agrees with the finding across people that metagenomic pathways are more even than community structure [3] and with the idea of functional redundancy [4]. Our data add that the same pattern appears within one person over ten days.

The timing agrees with earlier daily-sampling work. A lag of about one day, with day 6 still like days 1-5, matches the change a day after the new food reached the distal gut [1] and the dependence on several days of dietary history [2], but it is not a measurement of the lag: we do not know the person's transit time or when the day 6 sample was passed. The one-day response in the controlled study was for an animal-based diet, whereas the plant-based diet gave no significant shift [1], and our two diets are both vegetarian. Day-to-day variation without a diet change can also be large in healthy adults [11], and our Bray-Curtis values are not directly comparable with theirs (different profiles and methods).

The second level of buffering, strain diversity within MAGs, is a hypothesis. A more mixed population might hold its share because when one strain falls another can take over, as redundancy does for genes, one level down. The correlation is weak (Spearman ), absent within Bacteroidota, and exploratory; variant calls come from the reads of all days pooled, so we cannot see whether the strain mix changed from day to day; and variant sites may include reads from related species. The alternative reading of the second finding is that the MAGs that rose were clonal populations growing from low numbers, which does not need a buffering effect. Per-day variant data would be needed to tell the two apart.

Limitations. This is one person, so the results describe this gut and are not tested. The data are relative abundances (shares of the sequenced DNA) and gene potential, not gene expression or activity: a gene present is not a gene used. There is no baseline before the intervention: the person had eaten a similar high-calorie diet for about a week before day 1 (information from the data owner), so days 1-5 are not a neutral reference, and days 6-10 were closer to the normal diet. Carbohydrate and calories changed together, diet and time are confounded, and nothing here shows that diet caused the changes. The Lachnospiraceae argument rests on a figure with only 25 MAGs, two of the five stable reference MAGs are Lachnospiraceae, and the 1.5-fold limit and the choice of the top 20 are ours.
