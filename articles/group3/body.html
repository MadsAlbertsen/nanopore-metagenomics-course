Butyrate gene potential is maintained but redistributed after a carbohydrate reduction: a ten-day long-read metagenomic time series

Authors: Group 3, Nanopore metagenomics course 2026 (author names are not published on this site). Built from the group's project report: version 2, version 1. Figure and table numbers in this article are its own; "report" figures are those of the project report. Peer review and editorial decision.

Abstract

Butyrate, a short-chain fatty acid fuelling colonic epithelial cells, is formed by gut bacteria through a final step catalysed either by a CoA-transferase (but) or by a kinase route (ptb and buk). It is unknown whether the genetic potential for these two steps changes within a person over days when dietary carbohydrate is reduced, and how reliably long-read metagenomics resolves it. We analysed ten daily stool samples from one person who ate a high-carbohydrate, high-calorie vegetarian diet on days 1-5 and a low-carbohydrate, low-calorie vegetarian diet on days 6-10 (information from the data owner). Nanopore reads were assembled and binned into genomes (MAGs). High-quality MAGs held % of the sequenced bases, and most of each butyrate gene lay within them. Of gene calls, the fall of but was robust to removal of doubtful calls, whereas the kinase genes rested on calls close to their score thresholds. bcd was level ( and copies per 1,000 genes), but fell ( to ) and ptb and buk rose. The ratio but / (ptb + buk) decreased by log2 and first left its days 1-5 range on day , alongside a community-wide shift; few genomes carried the change, and dilution explained at most about a third of it. The Roseburia / E. rectale decrease agrees with earlier human diet studies. Butyrate gene potential was thus maintained but redistributed between final steps in this individual; this is gene potential, not butyrate production, and carbohydrate and calories changed together.

Introduction

Butyrate is the principal energy source of colonic epithelial cells, and short-chain fatty acids have been linked to the suppression of inflammation and colorectal cancer [1]. The amount of butyrate formed depends on which bacteria are present and on the substrates available to them [2, 3]. Most producers are Bacillota (Firmicutes) and use the acetyl-CoA pathway, in which butyryl-CoA is formed by butyryl-CoA dehydrogenase together with its electron-transfer flavoprotein partners (bcd, Etf). The final step releases butyrate either via butyryl-CoA:acetate CoA-transferase (but), which transfers the CoA moiety to acetate, or via phosphate butyryltransferase and butyrate kinase (ptb, buk), which conserves energy as ATP [4]. In stool metagenomes but is the more frequent final-step gene, and in isolates from human faeces ptb and buk are linked [4, 5].

In obese men on high-protein weight-loss diets, reduced carbohydrate intake lowered faecal butyrate and the Roseburia / E. rectale group, whereas most other large bacterial groups did not change significantly [6]. It remains unclear whether the gene potential for the two final steps shifts within an individual over days, and which genomes carry such a shift. We followed one person over ten days with a switch from a high-carbohydrate, high-calorie diet (days 1-5) to a low-carbohydrate, low-calorie diet (days 6-10), both vegetarian. The second diet was closer to the person's habitual diet, and the person had consumed a similar high-calorie diet for about a week before day 1 (information from the data owner).

Objective. The aim of this study was to describe, from long-read metagenomes of ten consecutive daily stool samples, how the butyrate gene potential of one gut changed after a reduction in dietary carbohydrate. We addressed three questions: (1) how complete and representative are the genomes and genes recovered (data quality); (2) how reliable are the butyrate gene calls (gene-call quality); and (3) which genes and genomes shift, and on which day (what shifts and when)?

Materials and methods

Data and genomes. One stool sample on each of ten days from one person was sequenced with Oxford Nanopore long reads (our own run of the ten samples), assembled and binned with mmlong2 [7] into MAGs, of them high quality (HQ) and the rest medium quality (MQ). A MAG's share is the percent of a sample's sequenced bases that fall on it. Genes were annotated with Bakta [8] and taxonomy assigned with GTDB-Tk [9] (GTDB release 226).

Gene definitions (used throughout). bcd, ptb, buk, atoD and atoA were found by KOfam profiles with their own score thresholds [10] (K00248, K00634, K00929, K01034, K01035), using Pyrodigal gene predictions [11] and PyHMMER [12]. The but gene has no clean KOfam profile and is defined by the exact Bakta name "butyryl-CoA:acetate CoA-transferase" ( genes in the MAGs); genes named only "acetyl-CoA hydrolase/transferase" are not counted as but (Table 1). The kinase route is ptb and buk found together, within three genes on one contig ( of MAGs with both genes); a buk without ptb ( route MAGs) is not counted as the route. A MAG has a complete route when it carries bcd plus but, ptb or buk. Gene abundance is gene potential, not activity: copies per 1,000 genes, each MAG weighted by its share of the day's bases times its number of genes.

Data quality. Each contig's read bases per sample are its length times its mean read depth. Bases were split into HQ MAGs, MQ MAGs, unbinned contigs ( contigs, Mbp) and bases not counted on any contig. Butyrate genes on the unbinned contigs were searched with the same profiles (but and the single-copy marker genes with a profile built from the MAGs' own proteins) and counted with the read depth of their contig. As a positive control, the contig-level counts on the MAGs alone reproduce the MAG-level gene abundances within 10%. The number of genomes was estimated from single-copy marker genes (rpoB and eleven ribosomal proteins).

Gene-call quality. For each of the gene calls in the MAGs we recorded the KOfam score relative to the threshold, the expected neighbour gene within three genes (Etf for bcd; buk for ptb; ptb for buk; atoA for atoD), the methylation verdict of the contig, the MAG's contamination (CheckM2) and, for but, the identity to two reviewed But enzymes (UniProt G2SYC0 and B0MC58 [13]). Classes were fixed in advance (Table 1 legend).

What shifts and when. Bray-Curtis dissimilarity of the ten days on all MAG shares with a PCoA; the first day of days 6-10 outside the range of days 1-5; ratios of two groups in the same sample, which do not depend on the sample's total; and a dilution check (if only a group outside the butyrate route had grown, every other group would shrink by the same factor). The analyses are descriptive: one person and ten days give no basis for a statistical test.

Discussion

The butyrate gene potential of this gut was maintained but redistributed: the CoA-transferase gene but fell, the kinase-route genes ptb and buk rose, bcd and the summed share of route MAGs stayed level. The change showed from day 7, in the butyrate producers and in the whole community, and a few genomes carried it. The shift persisted under all checks performed: the unbinned contigs, which hold a minor part of each gene, move the same way (Figure 2); the fall of but holds without the doubtful gene calls (Table 1); and ratios within each sample, which do not depend on the rest of the community, show it, with dilution explaining about a third at most.

The fall of the Roseburia / E. rectale group (Agathobacter and Roseburia: % to % of the bases, log2 ) agrees with Duncan et al. [6], where obese men were moved from maintenance ( g carbohydrate per day) to high-protein weight-loss diets with and g per day, and the group fell from % to % of bacteria by FISH while the larger groups stayed level. Our fall is smaller than their fall at g per day (log2 ) and close to their first step (log2 ); the doses cannot be matched because our subject's carbohydrate intake was not recorded, and their diets also raised protein. The same direction was found in 14 men on a reduced-carbohydrate diet [14], in a related trial with a similar design (not an independent repeat) [15], and in volunteers on an animal-product-only diet [16]. Each of these diets also changed protein or fat, which cannot be assessed here. For the kinase-route genomes we found no human diet study; Acetatifactor was described from a mouse on a high-calorie diet [17] and increased in virus-infected mice on a ketogenic diet [18], so the rise has no human counterpart that we could identify. Duncan et al. counted taxa, not genes; a level gene potential may still coincide with lower butyrate formation, which was not measured here.

Two interpretations remain open. Days 6-10 were closer to the subject's normal diet, and the subject had eaten a similar high-calorie diet for about a week before day 1 (information from the data owner), so part of the shift may be a return towards the usual community rather than a response to less carbohydrate. Carbohydrate and calories also changed together, and the data cannot separate them. Second, the shift may reflect functional redundancy, with different Lachnospiraceae filling the same butyrate-producing role; Acetatifactor and Butyrivibrio may also have risen for reasons unrelated to butyrate. Which final step dominates is not predicted by family, so a taxonomic profile alone would not show it.

Limitations. This is one person over ten days, with one sample per day, so the results describe this gut and are not tested. They concern gene potential, not gene expression or butyrate, and relative abundance (shares of the DNA, not cell counts). MAGs hold about -% of the bases, and genomes too rare to assemble are not seen. The gene calls are uneven: bcd is an upper bound, but is defined by a name, ptb lies close to its threshold, and the upstream genes and the amino-acid routes to butyrate were not searched. Carbohydrate and calories changed together, and protein and fat were not recorded.
