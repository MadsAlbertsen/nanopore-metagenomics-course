A reproducible rise of Bifidobacterium angulatum in a replicated ten-day long-read gut time series

Authors: Group 5 (original article) and Claude (revision); author names are not published. Submitted article: Group 5 article; peer review and editorial decision.

How this revision was made

The reviewers found the submitted article careful and honest, but asked for three things: a check of how much the uneven sequencing depth matters, a chance expectation for the group of MAGs that rose on days 3-5, and a question with a clear gap in the literature. A literature search (PubMed) showed that day-to-day changes of single gut bacteria within one person are large [5-8], that technical replicates are rare in such time series [9, 10], that co-varying groups in relative data need a null model [11, 12], and that family GH13 says little about the substrate of an enzyme unless its subfamily is known [16, 17].

The new question keeps the group's idea (B. angulatum, its carbohydrate genes, its ten-day course and the day 3-5 group) and asks which of these observations survive a measurement of noise. New in this revision: (1) sequencing depth and counting noise for every day; (2) a second, independent library of every day's DNA, made during the course and sequenced on another flow cell, used as a technical replicate at equal read numbers; (3) an exact permutation null for the day 3-5 group; (4) one baseline (the median of days 1-4) throughout; (5) GH13 subfamilies for the three Bifidobacterium genomes. Every analysis had its expected range written down before it was run and a positive control that reproduces a known number first (np26 scripts/45_project_group5.py, "Revision 1").

Abstract

Daily stool metagenomes show large changes in single bacteria, but few time series measure how much of a change is technical. We followed Bifidobacterium angulatum, a saccharolytic species with few studies, in ten daily stool samples from one person who changed from a high-carbohydrate, high-calorie to a low-carbohydrate, low-calorie vegetarian diet after day 5 (information from the participant). Nanopore reads ( Gbp) were co-assembled into metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs), including a closed, circular B. angulatum genome of Mb. A second library of each day's DNA, sequenced on another flow cell ( reads per day), was a technical replicate. Sequencing depth differed -fold between days, yet even the smallest sample held about B. angulatum reads. Library-to-library noise (standard deviation of log2 shares -) was a fraction of day-to-day change (-). In both libraries B. angulatum rose -fold above its median of days 1-4 to a peak on day 5, before the diet switch, and fell after day 6. The MAGs that rose on days 3-5 and fell on day 6 were fewer than expected by chance (); the pattern is reproducible but not a shared response. Of the GH13 genes of B. angulatum, fall in glycogen-related subfamilies and is a sucrose phosphorylase; a two-domain amylase-pullulanase-like gene occurred in only of MAGs, both bifidobacteria. A replicate library makes single-day changes of rare taxa interpretable; diet and time remain confounded.

Introduction

Bifidobacteria live mainly on carbohydrates that escape digestion in the small intestine, and their genomes carry many genes for carbohydrate-active enzymes and sugar transport [1, 2]. B. angulatum has received far less attention than B. adolescentis or B. longum: a PubMed search for the species name together with genome, carbohydrate or starch returned 15 records (4 November 2026). The complete genome of its type strain, isolated from human faeces, was published in 2015 [3], and the type strain degrades oligofructose and inulin quickly and outside the cell in co-culture experiments [4]. Little is known about how this species behaves in a person's gut from day to day.

Day-to-day change within one person is large. Dense time series found pronounced variation over days and weeks [5], links between daily fibre intake and next-day changes of many taxa [6], personalised responses to food choices [7] and up to 100-fold shifts in the absolute abundance of genera within six weeks [8]. To call a single day's change real, it has to be larger than measurement noise. Technical replicates are rare in such series; one shotgun study that measured them found technical variation small compared with variation over time (samples up to two years apart) [9], and a multi-laboratory comparison found that DNA extraction is a major source of technical variation [10]. A second problem is that sequencing data are relative: a rise of some taxa lowers the shares of all others, so correlations and co-varying groups need a null model [11, 12].

Long reads add two things. They can close bacterial genomes directly from stool [13, 14], so a rare species can be followed as a complete genome rather than as a 16S sequence or a fragmented short-read MAG. And a closed genome allows a finer prediction of function. Glycoside hydrolase family 13 (GH13), the alpha-amylase family, contains enzymes for starch, glycogen and sucrose [15]; its subfamilies predict the activity much better than the family [16]. Debranching enzymes fall in GH13_11 (glycogen debranching) and GH13_12-14 (pullulanase type I) and oligo-1,6-glucosidases in GH13_31 [17]; GH13_18 holds sucrose phosphorylases [18] and GH13_9 mostly bacterial glycogen branching enzymes [19]. In B. breve, the two-domain amylopullulanase ApuB is needed for growth on starch [20].

The submitted article found that B. angulatum peaked on day 5 of ten and that its mean share was lower after a diet switch, but did not show whether the peak exceeds noise. Here we ask three questions. Is the within-period rise of B. angulatum larger than counting noise and library-to-library noise? Is the group of MAGs that rose with it on days 3-5 larger than chance? And what do the GH13 subfamilies of its closed genome predict about which alpha-glucans it can use? We use a second, independent library of every day as a technical replicate and an exact permutation null for the co-rising group.

Materials and methods

Participant, samples and diet

One person gave one stool sample on each of ten consecutive days (samples DNA01-DNA10 are days 1-10). The following is information from the participant. Both diet periods were vegetarian. Days 1-5 were high in carbohydrate and calories, days 6-10 low in both; days 6-10 were closer to the participant's habitual diet, and a similar high-calorie diet was eaten for about a week before day 1. Food intake was not recorded. Carbohydrate and calories therefore changed together, and the change at day 6 was also a return towards the habitual diet. The participant agreed to the use and publication of the derived data; no ethics approval or waiver is recorded in the project notes, and a journal submission would need one from the responsible committee.

DNA extraction and libraries

The DNA extraction method and sample storage are not recorded in the project notes. Library 1 (the main data): the ten DNA samples were barcoded with the Rapid Barcoding Kit 24 V14 (SQK-RBK114.24) and sequenced together on one PromethION R10.4.1 flow cell (run 20 October 2026). Library 2 (the replicate): during the course, a second library of each of the same ten DNA samples was made in groups of two samples, barcoded, pooled and sequenced on a second PromethION flow cell (run started 2 November 2026); the kit used for library 2 is not recorded in the project notes. Library 2 therefore repeats library preparation, sequencing and read sampling, but not DNA extraction.

Basecalling and read processing

Library 1 was basecalled with Dorado 2.1.2 (super-accuracy model v5.2.0 for 400 bases per second, with 4mC/5mC and 6mA modified-base calls) and demultiplexed with default barcode trimming. Reads of at least 1,000 bp and mean quality Q10 or higher were kept (chopper 0.14.1), and adapters were trimmed and reads with internal adapters split (Porechop_ABI 0.5.1), leaving Gbp. Library 2 was basecalled live by MinKNOW 6.10.17 with the same model; from each sample, random reads of at least 1,000 bp and mean quality Q10 or higher (- Mbp per sample) were taken. Library 2 thus has an equal number of reads per day, which is a rarefaction of the replicate to equal depth.

Assembly, binning and MAG quality

All ten samples of library 1 were co-assembled with mmlong2 v1.2.1 [14] (myloasm 0.2.0) and binned with differential coverage of the ten samples (extended binning). MAG quality was estimated with CheckM2 1.1.0 [26]: high quality (HQ) means completeness above 90% and contamination below 5%, medium quality (MQ) completeness of at least 50% and contamination below 10% (these CheckM2 thresholds reproduce mmlong2's quality class of every MAG). MAGs were classified with GTDB-Tk 2.5.2 [27] against GTDB release 226. The result is MAGs ( HQ, MQ), which hold -% of each day's filtered bases.

Gene annotation and GH13 subfamilies

Genes were annotated with Bakta [28] (database ; a gene name rests on a protein-cluster match of at least % identity). For the CAZyme search, genes were called with Pyrodigal (Prodigal's algorithm [29], metagenome mode) and searched with the dbCAN profile HMMs as used by dbCAN3 [30] (domain E-value below , more than % of the model covered). The dbCAN models of GH13 are subfamily models, so each GH13 domain carries its subfamily. An activity is named only for subfamilies with a cited activity [17-19]; other subfamilies are listed without one. Only the HMM search was used (not the DIAMOND and dbCAN-sub searches of dbCAN3). Gene densities per 1,000 genes use the Pyrodigal count for every MAG ( in B. angulatum; Bakta lists protein-coding genes).

Relative abundance

Each sample's reads were mapped to the co-assembly with minimap2 [31] inside mmlong2, which writes the mean read depth of every contig per sample in the format of MetaBAT2's depth table [32]. The relative abundance (share) of MAG m in sample s, in percent of the sample's filtered bases, is

sharem,s = 100 × Σc ∈ m (depthc,s × lengthc) / basess

where the sum runs over the contigs c of the MAG. The mapping filters inside mmlong2 (for example identity cut-offs and the treatment of contig ends) were not inspected for this article. In library 2, the sampled reads were mapped with minimap2 2.31 (map-ont, no secondary alignments), and the share of a MAG is the aligned bases of the primary alignments on its contigs in percent of the sampled bases. All shares are relative: they describe the DNA, not the number of living cells.

Depth and counting noise

For B. angulatum in library 1, the mean read depth of a day is share × bases / genome length, and the expected number of reads is share × bases / mean read length of the sample (an approximation, because reads on one genome need not have the sample's mean length). An exact Poisson 95% interval on that count gives the counting noise of the share. The fold change between two days gets an approximate 95% interval from the two counts (log scale, standard error √(1/k1 + 1/k2)). In library 2, reads on the genome are counted directly.

Technical against day-to-day variation

For the MAGs with a mean share of at least % (rarer MAGs have few reads per day), technical noise is the pooled standard deviation of log2(share in library 2 / share in library 1) over the ten days, after removing each MAG's mean offset (a constant difference between the libraries, for example from read length, does not affect day-to-day comparisons). Day-to-day variation is the pooled standard deviation of log2(share) around each MAG's mean in library 1. All log2 ratios of shares in this article add % to both shares (one definition throughout). Both are reported for three classes of mean share.

Chance expectation for the day 3-5 group

The submitted article defined the group as the MAGs with share on day 3 < day 4 < day 5 > day 6. If a MAG's four values were in random order, this pattern would occur with probability 1/4 × 1/2 = 1/8. To keep the co-variation between MAGs (which a per-MAG calculation ignores), we used an exact permutation null: for every one of the ordered sets of four different days, the same days were used for all MAGs and the MAGs with the pattern were counted. The same was done for a stricter rule (day 5 at least -fold day 4) and for the runs of four consecutive days. Positive controls: days 3-6 reproduce the article's count, and on random data the null mean equals one eighth of the MAGs.

Statistics and software

One person, one sample per day and diet confounded with time: all results are descriptive, and no test of the diet effect is made. Coefficients of variation use the sample standard deviation (n - 1). Software: Dorado 2.1.2, MinKNOW 6.10.17, chopper 0.14.1, Porechop_ABI 0.5.1, seqkit 2.14.0, samtools 1.24, mmlong2 1.2.1 with myloasm 0.2.0, minimap2 2.30, MetaBAT2 2.18, CheckM2 1.1.0, GTDB-Tk 2.5.2 (GTDB release 226), Bakta , Pyrodigal and pyhmmer with the dbCAN HMMs; analysis in Python (pandas, numpy, scipy), np26 scripts/45_project_group5.py.

Discussion

What the replicate shows. The B. angulatum peak on day 5 is not a counting or library artefact. Even the thinnest sample held about reads of the genome, a second library on another flow cell reproduced the day-by-day course within --fold, and both libraries gave the same -fold rise over the median of days 1-4. Across MAGs, the technical standard deviation of log2 shares was at most % of the day-to-day standard deviation in each abundance class, in line with the shotgun replicates of Voigt et al. [9]. This matters for dense time series of rare taxa: a rise of a few-fold in a species below 0.3% of the DNA can be read as a change in the sample once library and sampling noise are measured.

What the null model shows. The day 3-5 group of the submitted article does not show a shared response. Its size () is below the chance expectation (), and a stricter rule or other runs of four days give similar or larger counts. Each member's pattern repeats in the second library, which shows that the measurements are reproducible, not that the MAGs respond together. In relative data, the rise of some taxa lowers the shares of the others [11, 12], so a group of "co-risers" chosen by a rule is expected on any day. A shared response would need a count clearly above the null, or the same MAGs rising again after a repeated exposure.

Diet, time and the return to the habitual diet. The share of B. angulatum rose within the high-carbohydrate, high-calorie period, which had begun about a week before day 1, and fell one day after the switch. The timing does not fit a simple response to the switch. Three readings remain open: a food eaten around days 3-5 (none was recorded), a transit delay so that day 6 still reflected the earlier diet, or a return towards the habitual community after day 6, as the days 6-10 diet was closer to the habitual one. Calories changed together with carbohydrate. In controlled feeding, bacterial groups bloomed within days of a diet change and reversed with the next diet, and relatives of Eubacterium rectale decreased on a reduced-carbohydrate weight-loss diet [21]; plant-based and animal-based diets change the community within days [22]. These studies show that such changes are possible, not that they happened here.

Gene potential. The GH13 subfamilies change the picture of B. angulatum from a starch specialist with GH13 genes to a genome in which GH13 genes are predicted to serve glycogen or sucrose metabolism, to cleave starch-like branch points, among them one candidate amylase-pullulanase, and have no cited activity. In resistant-starch trials, bifidobacteria such as B. adolescentis, or Ruminococcus bromii, increased in many but not all people [24, 25], and in co-culture R. bromii stimulated the use of resistant starch by B. adolescentis and others [23]. That the two-domain GH13_14 gene occurred only in the two bifidobacteria that peaked on day 5 is an observation from two genomes; it does not show that this gene caused the rise. The fructan genes (GH32: in B. angulatum) fit the fast extracellular fructan use of the type strain [4], but no fructan intake was recorded.

What a closed genome adds. A single circular contig gave the complete gene order, including the multi-domain GH13 gene, whose two domains could be split in a fragmented assembly. It also lets the same genome be counted in an independent library by read mapping.

Limitations. One person and ten days; diet confounded with time, with calories and with a return to the habitual diet. Shares are relative and describe DNA, not living cells. The replicate repeats library preparation, sequencing and read sampling, but not DNA extraction, and the extraction method is not recorded. Expected read counts in library 1 use the sample's mean read length. Breadth of coverage per day was not computed (it needs the alignment files). Gene functions are predictions from sequence (dbCAN HMMs only, Bakta's light database); no expression or enzyme activity was measured, and no comparison with the type-strain genome [3] was made.

Conclusions

A second, independent library of every day turns a single-library time series into one where day-to-day changes can be judged against measured noise. With it, the rise of B. angulatum to a peak on day 5 is reproducible and much larger than counting and library noise, while the group of MAGs that seemed to rise with it is no larger than chance. Its closed genome, read at the level of GH13 subfamilies, assigns of GH13 genes to glycogen or sucrose metabolism and to starch-like branch points, including one amylase-pullulanase-like gene shared only with B. adolescentis among the MAGs. Separating diet from time would need repeated diet switches or more people, and an extraction replicate would close the remaining technical gap.
