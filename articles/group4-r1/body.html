Carbohydrate-degrading gene potential after a diet switch is no more stable than averaging across genomes predicts: a null-model test in a ten-day long-read gut metagenome

Authors: Group 4 (original article) and Claude (revision); author names are not published. Submitted article · peer review and editorial decision · the group's project report. Figure and table numbers are this revision's own.

How this revision was made

This is a rewrite by Claude (an AI model) for teaching, not the group's text. The group found that species composition moved after the diet switch while the carbohydrate-degrading gene potential changed much less, and explained it by redundancy across genomes. All three reviewers and the editor asked the same question: is that more than the averaging that any sum over many genomes shows?

A literature search (PubMed) found that ecology has a name and a test for this question, the portfolio or averaging effect, with compensation and synchrony of the parts as the two ways to go beyond it [1, 2]. To our knowledge, no gut microbiome study has tested the time stability of a gene function against such a null. The revision therefore asks whether the stability exceeds averaging, and whether genomes that carry the same gene family compensate for each other or move together.

Kept from the group: the data, the drift and redundancy analyses, the split of the change between families of bacteria, and the strain question (now a hypothesis). New: two null models that move gene content between genomes while the daily genome shares stay fixed; a synchrony decomposition per gene family; a permutation test of the period split for both profiles; a simulated read-subsampling check; within-period variation for the strain analysis; a sensitivity check with high-quality genomes only. One period split (days 1-5 against days 6-10, as given by the participant) is used throughout.

Expected results were written down before any new analysis was run (np26 repository, notes/projects/group4_r1_plan.md). Each analysis first reproduced a number of the report (for example the between-period distance and the Spearman ), and planted test data checked that the null can detect both synchrony and compensation. The code was reviewed by a separate agent that did not write it. The main result was outside the expected range: the observed stability is what averaging alone predicts, and the title and conclusions follow that result. Every reference was checked in PubMed or Crossref.

Abstract

Gut microbiome studies often report that gene content is more stable than taxonomic composition and attribute this to functional redundancy. A sum over many genomes is less variable than its parts for purely statistical reasons, however, and this averaging effect is rarely tested. We sequenced daily stool samples from one person over ten days with Oxford Nanopore long reads, across a switch from a high-carbohydrate, high-calorie to a low-carbohydrate, low-calorie vegetarian diet (dietary information from the participant), and recovered metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs). Species composition moved away from days 1-5 from day 7. The profile of carbohydrate-degrading enzyme (CAZyme) families changed about a quarter as much, but it separated the two periods almost as clearly (permutation test p = , species p = ). Null models that kept the daily MAG shares and each family's number of carrier MAGs, but placed families on random carriers, reproduced the observed between-period distance (observed , null median ) and the relation between carrier number and day-to-day variation (Spearman , null ). Real families varied slightly more than random ones (-fold), because the MAGs that carry a family tended to rise and fall together rather than compensate. Host-glycan families rose %, more than random families did. In this person, the stability of CAZyme gene potential is explained by averaging across genomes; functional redundancy, in the sense of compensation, was not detected.

Introduction

Diet changes the human gut microbiome within days. In a controlled feeding study, composition changed within 24 hours of a diet change [3], an animal-based diet altered the community about one day after the food reached the distal gut [4], and in 34 people sampled daily the community depended on the diet of several preceding days [5]. Day-to-day variation without a diet change is large as well [6]. Whether the genes of the community follow these shifts is a separate question, because many unrelated microbes carry the same functions.

Across healthy people, the metabolic pathways of the metagenome vary much less than its taxonomic composition [7], and within people, metagenomic functional potential is more stable over time than the expression of those genes [8]. This pattern is commonly read as functional redundancy: if several taxa carry a function, the loss of one is compensated by others [9, 10]. Redundancy has been quantified from the overlap of genome content [11], but the claim that it stabilises function has rarely been tested, and in a marine time series composition and function were tightly linked [12].

Community ecology offers a test. A total summed over many fluctuating parts is less variable than a single part simply because independent fluctuations partly cancel; this averaging, or portfolio, effect makes some diversity-stability relationships statistically inevitable [1]. Stability beyond averaging requires that the parts compensate for each other (negative covariation, the insurance hypothesis [13]), while synchronous parts give less stability than averaging [14]. The variation of a total can be written exactly as the product of the parts' average variation and their synchrony [2]. A gene family summed over its carrier genomes is such a total. Gut microbiome studies that compare a gene profile with a taxonomic profile usually do not separate these components.

Two features of gut gene content make the answer non-obvious. Carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) are unevenly spread across taxa: Bacteroidota carry many polysaccharide utilisation loci, Bacillota fewer [15, 16], and most microbial traits are phylogenetically clustered [17]. Carriers of one family are therefore often relatives that may respond alike. Testing this needs the complete gene content of each genome together with its daily abundance, which short-read gene catalogues and 16S rRNA profiles do not provide. Long-read metagenomes give many complete or near-complete genomes from one sample series [18, 19].

We asked whether the stability of carbohydrate-degrading gene potential across a diet switch in one person exceeds the averaging expected from summing many genomes, and whether genomes that share a CAZyme family compensate for each other or move together. We used ten daily long-read metagenomes, MAGs and null models that move gene content between genomes while keeping each genome's daily abundance.

Materials and methods

Participant, sampling and diet. One adult participant collected one stool sample on each of ten consecutive days. The following dietary information was provided by the participant: days 1-5 comprised a high-carbohydrate, high-calorie vegetarian diet and days 6-10 a low-carbohydrate, low-calorie vegetarian diet. The diet of days 6-10 was closer to the participant's habitual diet, and a similar high-calorie diet had been eaten for about a week before day 1. Carbohydrate and energy intake changed together. No dietary records beyond this description were used.

Ethics. The participant (the data owner) agreed to the analysis and to the publication of derived data. A formal ethics approval is not recorded in the project notes; a journal submission would need the approval of the responsible committee and written informed consent.

DNA extraction and sequencing. The DNA extraction method is not recorded in the project notes. Libraries were prepared with the Rapid Barcoding Kit SQK-RBK114-24 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies) and the ten barcoded samples were sequenced on one PromethION R10.4.1 flow cell. Reads were basecalled with Dorado 2.1.2 (super-accuracy model v5.2.0, 400 bases per second, with 4mC, 5mC and 6mA calling), without adapter trimming and with read splitting on, and demultiplexed with dorado demux (default barcode trimming). The run gave Gbp (- Gbp per sample), with read N50 - kb and median read quality Q- per sample.

Read processing. Reads with a mean quality below Q10 or shorter than 1,000 bp were removed with chopper [20]; adapters were trimmed and chimeric reads split with Porechop_ABI [21], and the length filter was applied again. This left million reads and Gbp, - million reads (- Gbp) per day (Table 1).

Assembly, binning and genome quality. The reads of all days were co-assembled and binned with mmlong2 v1.2.1 [18] in its extended binning mode, which assembles with myloasm 0.2.0 [19], maps the reads of each sample back to the contigs with minimap2 2.30 [22], splits contigs at likely misassemblies, removes contigs shorter than 3 kb, and bins with several binners using the read depth of each sample (differential coverage). This gave contigs (N50 kb) and MAGs. Completeness and contamination were estimated with CheckM2 1.1.0 [23]. Following MIMAG [24], MAGs were high quality (completeness at least 90%, contamination at most 5%, 5S, 16S and 23S rRNA genes and at least 18 tRNAs) and medium quality (at least 50% and at most 10%); MAGs contained a circular contig. Taxonomy was assigned with GTDB-Tk 2.5.2 [25] against GTDB release 226.

Relative abundance. mmlong2 reports the mean read depth dc,t of each contig c in each day's reads (its default alignment filters were used and not changed). The share of MAG i on day t, in percent of the day's sequenced bases, is si,t = 100 × Σc∈i Lc dc,t / Bt, with Lc the contig length and Bt the bases of the day's filtered reads. The per-contig depths account for -% of each day's bases, and MAGs hold -% (Table 1). All values are relative abundances: a rise of one MAG lowers the share of all others [26]. MAGs with a mean share of at least 0.1% over the ten days were called common ( MAGs).

Genes and CAZymes. Genes were predicted with Pyrodigal 3.7.1 [27, 28] in single mode, trained on each MAG, giving genes in MAGs; these genes are the denominator of every "per 1,000 genes" value. (Bakta 1.11.4 [29], run inside mmlong2 in metagenome mode, gave coding sequences; Bakta product names were used only in the group's report.) CAZyme domains were found with the dbCAN HMM profiles [30] (release db_v5-2-9_5-5-2026) searched with PyHMMER 0.12.3 [31], using the run_dbcan rules (domain i-Evalue below 1e-15, HMM coverage above 0.35). Only the degrading classes were analysed: glycoside hydrolases, polysaccharide lyases and carbohydrate esterases of the CAZy database [32] ( families found). A family was assigned to plant fibre, starch, fructan or host glycans when at least two thirds of its substrate entries in the dbCAN family-substrate table fell in that group, and otherwise to mixed, other or unmapped. Both medium- and high-quality MAGs were included; the main test was repeated with high-quality MAGs only.

Community gene potential. The value of CAZyme family f on day t, in genes per 1,000 genes of the binned community, is Gf,t = 1000 × Σi si,t ni,f / Σi si,t Ni, where ni,f is the number of genes of family f in MAG i and Ni its number of genes. Each MAG thus counts with its share of the DNA times its number of genes. The CAZyme profile of a day is the vector of all family values; the species profile is the vector of MAG shares. Substrate groups are the sums of their families.

Distances, drift and the period split. Profiles were compared with Bray-Curtis dissimilarity after scaling each day to sum 1. The drift of a day is its distance to the mean profile of days 1-5 (for days 1-5, the mean of the other four days). The between-period distance is the mean over the 25 pairs of a day 1-5 and a day 6-10, and the within-period distance the mean over pairs inside each period; their ratio expresses the period difference in units of day-to-day variation. The period split was tested with a PERMANOVA pseudo-F [33] against all 126 ways to split the ten days into two groups of five (exact permutation; the smallest possible p is 2/252 because each partition appears twice). Consecutive days are autocorrelated, so p-values are descriptive.

Null models for aggregation. The nulls keep the daily MAG shares and the gene totals and move only which MAG carries which CAZyme family. In the carrier-preserving null, the MAG × family presence matrix was randomised with the curveball algorithm [34] separately for common and other MAGs, so each family kept its number of common and of other carrier MAGs and each MAG its number of families; each family's own per-carrier gene counts were then given to its new carriers in random order. In the gene-token null, every degrading CAZyme gene received a random family label, so each MAG kept its number of CAZyme genes and each family its total, but families spread over more MAGs. For each of replicates per null we recomputed the between-period distance, the between/within ratio, the median day-to-day coefficient of variation (CV, standard deviation over the ten days divided by the mean) of the families carried by at least one common MAG, the Spearman correlation of carrier number and CV, and the same correlation with the family's mean value taken out (partial Spearman on ranks). The observed value is reported with its percentile in the null distribution. Each replicate made curveball trades per family, starting from the observed matrix; doubling the trades gave the same medians (between-period distance and ). In the gene-token null the set of families with a common carrier changes between replicates, so its CV statistics cover slightly different families.

Synchrony decomposition. For a family carried by k MAGs, the daily value is the sum of the carriers' parts xi,t = 1000 si,t ni,f / Σj sj,t Nj. Its CV equals √φ × (Σi σi) / μ, the product of the square root of the synchrony φ = var(Σxi) / (Σσi)2 and the carriers' mean CV weighted by their parts [2, 14]. If carriers vary independently, φ equals φindep = Σσi2 / (Σσi)2. A ratio φ/φindep above 1 means the carriers rise and fall together; below 1 that they move against each other. Because the MAG shares of a day sum to a fixed total, shares covary negatively by construction, so the ratio is judged against random carrier sets (the carrier-preserving null), not against 1. Families with at least two carriers among all MAGs were analysed ( families), and the median ratio was compared with 300 carrier-preserving null replicates.

Positive controls. The observed statistics computed by the null code reproduce the report's numbers. In a planted test, ten artificial families placed on four MAGs that rose at least two-fold each, and ten placed on a falling MAG plus the rising MAG that made their summed share steadiest, were added to the real families: the synchronous families had a CV above the null's 95th percentile in of 10 cases (rule set beforehand: at least 8), the compensating ones below the null median in of (rule: at least 8; below the 5th percentile in ). The PERMANOVA of the species profile reproduced an earlier analysis of the same data (F = ).

Sequencing depth. Every day was subsampled in silico to the read count of the smallest day ( reads) by drawing reads from the day's MAG shares (multinomial; 50 replicates). This simulates the counting noise of a smaller sample but not mapping differences; a remapping of subsampled reads was not done.

Taxa behind the change. The Bray-Curtis distance between the two period-mean species profiles is Σi|p2,i − p1,i|/2, so each MAG's part is exact. For the CAZyme profile, the change of each family between the period means was split exactly over MAGs as (w2,i − w1,i)(ri − R1), with w the MAG's share of the community's genes, r its own genes of the family per 1,000 genes and R1 the days 1-5 value; MAG parts were summed by GTDB family.

Strain variation. Single-nucleotide variant sites were called by mmlong2 with Longshot 1.0.0 [35] on the reads of all days pooled. Longshot was designed for diploid genomes; here it serves only as a screen for sites where the reads of a MAG disagree. The variant share of a MAG is its number of variant sites divided by its length. Because a ten-day CV mostly reflects the diet-associated shift, the strain analysis used the within-period CV (the mean of the CVs over days 1-5 and over days 6-10), with sequencing depth taken out by partial Spearman correlation. This analysis is exploratory; it was the ninth correlation examined in this data set.

Software. Analyses used Python 3 with NumPy, pandas and SciPy in the course's conda environment; the code is in the np26 repository (scripts/44_project_group4.py, python/np26/nullmodels.py), which also writes this page's data file.

Discussion

The CAZyme gene potential of this gut changed less than its species composition, as the group found, but the difference had the size expected from summing genomes. Random placements of the same families on the same daily MAG shares gave the same between-period distance, and the same steep fall of day-to-day variation with carrier number. Stability of a summed function is therefore not, by itself, evidence of functional redundancy in the sense of one taxon replacing another. This is the averaging, or portfolio, effect known from plant and animal communities [1, 2], and our data show it for gene families summed over genomes.

Beyond averaging, the data pointed the other way from buffering. Carriers of the same family rose and fell together more than random carriers, and real families varied about a fifth more than random ones. This fits the phylogenetic clustering of microbial traits [17] and of CAZyme repertoires in particular [15, 16]: host-glycan families had the most synchronous carriers, and the largest parts of the host-glycan rise came from Bacteroidota MAGs (Phocaeicola and Bacteroides) that rose together after the switch. It also fits the marine finding that composition and function can be tightly linked [12]. Compensation beyond chance, which the insurance hypothesis requires [13], was not detected: real carriers were more synchronous than random ones, not less.

The CAZyme profile was not decoupled from composition. Its drift tracked the species drift day by day, at about a quarter of the scale, and relative to its own day-to-day variation it separated the two periods nearly as well as species did. Because the scale factor was the same before and after the switch, the compression is a property of the measure, not a response to the diet. The host-glycan rise is the exception: it exceeded what random families of the same size produce. It came mostly from Bacteroidota that rose after the switch, and mucin-degrading capacity rising when dietary carbohydrate falls is a plausible reading, but this design cannot show it.

What would distinguish redundancy from averaging in future work is a test like ours applied to activity data: if the carriers of a function compensate in expression, or if the function is measured directly, stability beyond the null would support redundancy. Several people, repeated switches or a crossover design are needed to separate diet from time. Per-day strain profiles from long reads could replace the pooled variant calls; a per-day strain workflow was prepared for this data set but not run in the course.

Limitations. The data come from one person over ten days with one switch, so they describe this gut and do not generalise. Diet is confounded with time: carbohydrate and energy changed together, a similar high-calorie diet had run for about a week before day 1, and days 6-10 were a return towards the habitual diet, so a drift back to the habitual community would explain the steady departure equally well. All values are relative abundances and gene potential, not activity or expression. The null models keep the observed MAG shares, so they test where genes sit, not why MAGs changed; they also inherit the compositional constraint that MAG shares sum to a fixed total. P-values ignore the autocorrelation of consecutive days. The subsampling check simulates counting noise only. The planted test flagged of 10 synchronous families and placed of compensating pairs below the null's 5th percentile, so single-family results are less reliable than the medians. Variant calls come from a diploid caller on pooled reads, and the variant share depends on depth.

Conclusions

In one person's gut across a diet switch, the carbohydrate-degrading gene potential changed about a quarter as much as species composition, but random placement of the same gene families on the same genomes gave the same stability. The stability is therefore explained by averaging across genomes. Genomes that share a family tended to move together rather than compensate, and only the host-glycan group rose more than random families. Claims of functional redundancy from metagenomic time series should be tested against an aggregation null of this kind.
