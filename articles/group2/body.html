Abstract

Diet alters the human gut microbiome within days, but daily, genome-resolved records of how single genomes and the community's carbohydrate-degrading gene content respond are scarce. We sequenced ten consecutive daily stool samples from one person with nanopore long reads, five days on a high-carbohydrate, high-calorie diet followed by five days on a low-carbohydrate, low-calorie diet, and recovered metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs). Community composition differed between the periods (Bray-Curtis dissimilarity between days of different periods, and within them); the largest change came one to two days after the switch, and diversity did not change. Of MAGs with a mean share of at least 0.1%, changed at least two-fold ( down, all Bacillota; up), and only changed most at the switch itself. Degrading carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZyme) genes per 1,000 genes of the community changed by % (day-resampling interval to %), with a larger rise in host-glycan genes (%); Bacteroidota replacing each other accounted for most of the change. Of curated genomic features, differed between rising and falling MAGs (false discovery rate below 10%), mostly along taxonomic lines. These are descriptions of one person over ten days: diet is confounded with time, carbohydrate and calories changed together, and the data show relative abundance and gene potential, not cell numbers or activity. The results are hypotheses for studies with replicate individuals.

Keywords: gut microbiome; diet; nanopore sequencing; metagenome-assembled genomes; carbohydrate-active enzymes; time series

Introduction

The gut microbiome responds quickly to diet. In a controlled study, switching volunteers to an entirely plant-based or animal-based diet changed community structure within days, and the animal-based diet lowered Firmicutes that degrade plant polysaccharides, among them Roseburia and Eubacterium rectale [1]. With daily sampling, community composition depended on several days of dietary history, and the responses were personal [2]. In a weight-loss study, lowering carbohydrate intake reduced the Roseburia/E. rectale group and faecal butyrate [3].

The carbohydrates a community can use are encoded in its carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZyme) genes. Gut bacteria carry a large and varied CAZyme repertoire [4], and Bacteroidota organise much of theirs in polysaccharide utilisation loci, gene clusters that are each usually dedicated to one type of glycan [5]. When dietary fibre is scarce, gut bacteria can turn to host mucus glycans, as shown in gnotobiotic mice colonised with a synthetic human gut community [6]. A change in diet could therefore shift the balance between plant-fibre and host-glycan enzymes. Sequencing measures shares, not amounts: the rise of one group pushes all others down, so relative abundance must be read as a composition [7].

Long-read metagenomics recovers many near-complete genomes from a series of samples [8], which allows single genomes and their gene content to be followed day by day. Here we use a nanopore data set from one person, sampled on ten consecutive days. Days 1-5 were on a high-carbohydrate, high-calorie diet and days 6-10 on a low-carbohydrate, low-calorie diet; both were vegetarian. According to the data owner, the diet of days 6-10 was closer to the person's normal diet, and the person had eaten a similar high-calorie diet for about a week before day 1, so there is no sample from before the first period. Carbohydrate and calories changed together, and the design has one switch and no replicate person.

We asked two questions, each with a hypothesis stated before the analysis. Question 1 (community dynamics): how do composition, diversity and single MAGs change over time and between the two diet periods, and is the shift of a MAG a step at the switch or a gradual trend? Hypothesis 1: community structure changes with the diet transition, with specific MAGs consistently rising or falling. Question 2 (functional dynamics): how does the community's carbohydrate-degradation potential change between the periods, and which MAGs contribute most? Hypothesis 2: carbohydrate-utilisation potential differs between the periods. A third, exploratory question asks which genomic traits distinguish MAGs that rose from MAGs that fell. The expected results were written down before each analysis.

Materials and methods

Study design and samples

One adult gave a stool sample on each of ten consecutive days. Days 1-5 were high carbohydrate and high calorie, days 6-10 low carbohydrate and low calorie, both vegetarian (diet details from the data owner, as stated in the Introduction). No food records, no sample from before day 1, no replicate extractions and no RNA, protein, metabolite or cell-count data are available (Supplementary Table 1 lists the inputs of the analysis).

Sequencing, assembly and binning

DNA was sequenced on one PromethION flow cell (R10.4.1 chemistry, Rapid Barcoding Kit) and basecalled with Dorado (super-accurate model). Reads were filtered to Q ≥ 10 and ≥ 1,000 bp (chopper 0.14.1) and adapters were trimmed (Porechop_ABI 0.5.1); per-sample yields are in Supplementary Table 2. The ten samples were co-assembled and binned with mmlong2 1.2.1 [8] (myloasm assembler), which gave MAGs. Completeness and contamination were estimated with CheckM2 [9]. MAGs were classed as high quality (HQ; ≥ 90% complete, ≤ 5% contaminated, rRNA and tRNA genes present, after the MIMAG standard [10]) or medium quality (MQ; ≥ 50% complete, < 10% contaminated): HQ and MQ MAGs. Taxonomy was assigned with GTDB-Tk 2.5.2 [11].

Relative abundance, ordination and diversity

The share of a MAG on a day is the percent of that day's sequenced bases that map to it, which is independent of total depth by construction; phylum shares are sums of MAG shares. For Bray-Curtis dissimilarity and diversity, each day's MAG shares were rescaled to sum to 1 (shares of the binned bases). Bray-Curtis dissimilarity between days used all MAGs, and principal coordinates analysis (classical scaling) mapped the days. As a compositional alternative, centred log-ratios (natural log of share + 0.01%, minus each day's mean over the MAGs) gave the Aitchison distance (Euclidean distance on them) [7]. Diversity is the Shannon index on the MAGs with a mean share of at least 0.1%, shown as its effective number of MAGs (the exponential of the index).

MAG trajectories: step or trend

The period change of a MAG is the log2 of its mean share in days 6-10 over days 1-5, with 0.01% added to both. On log2(share + 0.01%) per day, each of the nine splits of the ten days into an early and a late block was fitted by two block means, and its R² was compared with that of a straight line over the days (both have two parameters, so neither is favoured by size). The split with the highest R² is the best split: a best split at 5|6 fits a step at the switch, a later split a delayed step, and a line that beats the 5|6 step fits a gradual trend. Separation counts the low-carbohydrate days lying beyond all five high-carbohydrate days. The same check was applied to ordination axis 1.

Uncertainty and sensitivity

With one person and one sample per day there are no replicate samples, so every effect size received two descriptive checks of how much it depends on the particular days sampled. Day resampling: for each period, five of its own five days were drawn at random with replacement, each MAG's share was averaged over the drawn days, and the log2 change of the two period means was recomputed with the same pseudocount. Repeated times with a fixed seed, this gives log2 changes per MAG; their 5th and 95th percentiles form the 90% day-resampling interval. The same drawn days were used for every contrast. This interval is descriptive and too narrow: even for independent days, the spread of a resampled five-day mean is about √(4/5) of the true standard error; each period has only distinct resamples; and neighbouring days are autocorrelated. Intervals of MAGs selected for a large change exclude zero almost by construction. Leave one day out: each day was removed once and all contrasts recomputed on the other nine days; a MAG counts as two-fold in all runs if its change stays at least two-fold and its mean share over the nine days stays at or above 0.1% in every run ( fail only the share cut-off). Cut-off grid: fold-change cut-offs 1.5, 2, 3 and 4, mean-share cut-offs 0.05, 0.1, 0.25 and 0.5%, and pseudocounts 0.001, 0.01 and 0.1% ( settings). Quality and depth: all analyses were repeated with HQ MAGs only; bases per sample were compared with ordination axis 1 and the binned share (Spearman correlation), and Bray-Curtis dissimilarity was recomputed on the MAGs with a mean share of at least 0.5% only.

CAZyme annotation and community potential

Genes were predicted with Pyrodigal 3.7.1 [12] and searched against the dbCAN HMMs (release db_v5-2-9_5-5-2026) with pyhmmer 0.12.3 [13]. Degrading families (glycoside hydrolases, polysaccharide lyases, carbohydrate esterases) were grouped by substrate following dbCAN3 [14]. Per day, the community value is Σ(share × CAZyme genes) / Σ(share × all genes) × 1,000 over all MAGs, that is, each MAG weighted by its share of the DNA times its number of genes; a period value is the mean of its five days. Expressed per 1,000 genes, the value does not grow with genome size or completeness. The contribution of a MAG to the change is 1,000 × (its change in mean share) × (its genes in the group − the community's days 1-5 rate × its genes) / (the community's share-weighted gene count in days 6-10); contributions add up exactly to the change at the period-mean shares, and phylum values are sums over their MAGs. For the two-fold MAGs, the same value was computed with only the MAGs that changed at least two-fold, with period means, percent change and interval as for the whole community; the same code with all MAGs returns the main values. CAZy-family analysis repeated the weighting per family; its cut-off (at least genes per 1,000 genes) was lowered after seeing the data, so this analysis is exploratory.

Genomic traits of rising and falling MAGs

MAGs with a log2 change of at least 1 (risers) or at most −1 (fallers) among those with a mean share of at least 0.1% were compared, with MAGs as the units. Features per MAG were degrading CAZyme genes per substrate group and genes per CAZy family (each per 1,000 genes); presence of KOfam marker genes (KOfam HMMs [15] with their thresholds); genome size, GC content, gene number and CRISPR arrays (mmlong2, Bakta 1.11.4 [16], light database); genes per 1,000 genes in keyword groups of Bakta gene names; and presence of every informative Bakta gene name. A feature was tested if present in at least four MAGs (and absent from at least four, for presence): Fisher's exact test for presence, Wilcoxon rank-sum test for values, two-sided, with Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rates (q) reported at q < . The curated features (CAZy, KOfam, genome traits, keyword groups) are the primary set, with their own false discovery rate; the single gene names are a separate candidate screen with their own rate, because the same gene can be named differently in different lineages. Because all fallers are Bacillota, the differing features were tested again on the Bacillota only ( fallers, risers), uncorrected, with the number expected by chance at p < 0.05. A butyrate route was defined as butyryl-CoA dehydrogenase plus a final step (butyryl-CoA:acetate CoA-transferase by name, phosphate butyryltransferase or butyrate kinase). Features were grouped into metabolic potential (CAZy, KOfam and enzyme-like gene names) and other capabilities; only the metabolic-potential features are shown in Fig. 8, whereas all tested features are listed in Supplementary Table 12.

Statistics, controls and reporting

No hypothesis tests and no p-values were computed for changes over time: the ten days of one person are a time series, not replicates, so we report effect sizes with resampling intervals and sensitivity ranges. The only tests are those of the riser-fallers comparison, where the MAGs are the units, with a false discovery rate over all features tested; they describe these MAGs, are exploratory and do not separate traits from taxonomy. Expected results were written before each analysis. Positive controls: recomputed phylum shares, Bray-Curtis values and two-fold counts equal the course's earlier tables; weighting a single MAG at 100% returns its own CAZyme rate (Bacteroides cellulosilyticus: genes per 1,000 genes); resampling with the original days, leaving no day out and the default cut-off setting each return the main numbers. The analysis script stops if any control fails.

Exploratory question: genomic traits of rising and falling MAGs. Which genomic features are shared within the MAGs that rose at least two-fold (risers) and the that fell (fallers), but differ between the two groups? exploratory

Risers and fallers differ in some curated metabolic features, mostly along taxonomic lines

With MAGs as the units, we compared the two groups on curated features present in at least four of the MAGs: CAZyme substrate groups and CAZy families, KOfam marker genes, genome traits and keyword groups of gene names. of them differed at a false discovery rate below 10%; the that concern metabolic potential are shown in Fig. 8, and the remaining , which concern other capabilities, are listed in Supplementary Table 12 but not discussed here. Single Bakta gene names were screened separately, with their own false discovery rate, as a list of candidates only (Supplementary Table 12): the same gene often carries differently worded names in different lineages (for example, "Cell division protein FtsW" is in of fallers but of risers, because most other MAGs name the same gene "putative peptidoglycan glycosyltransferase FtsW"), so name differences mostly follow lineage, not capability.

Risers carried SusC/SusD outer-membrane glycan-uptake genes ( of risers, fallers; Supplementary Table 12), methylmalonyl-CoA mutase of the succinate route to propionate (mcmA; risers, fallers) and CAZy families that no faller has (for example GH92 and GH97, mannosidases and glucosidases typical of Bacteroidota; of families differed). Fallers more often carried formate-tetrahydrofolate ligase (fhs; of fallers, risers) and had more genes for sugar phosphotransferase systems (median against per 1,000 genes; Supplementary Table 12). They also had more ABC transporter genes ( against ), but ABC transporters move many kinds of substrates and the difference vanished within the Bacillota (p = ). Butyrate kinase (buk) was in risers but faller; of these risers are Bacteroidota without butyryl-CoA dehydrogenase or phosphate butyryltransferase, so this is not a butyrate route. A complete butyrate route was found in fallers and risers, and the CoA-transferase by name in only fallers, although several fallers belong to genera known as butyrate producers (Roseburia, Agathobacter, Anaerostipes); gene names may miss some of these genes. Adenylylsulfate reductase (aprA) was in fallers and riser, but without dissimilatory sulfite reductase (dsrA, in MAGs) it does not indicate sulfate reduction, and we do not interpret it. All fallers are Bacillota ( of Lachnospiraceae), whereas risers are Bacteroidota and the Bacillota risers include Lachnospiraceae. Repeating the tests on the Bacillota only, of the differing features that could be tested kept an uncorrected p < 0.05 (about expected by chance), among them the CAZy family GH179; are absent from all Bacillota (Bacteroidota traits). Most differences therefore separate Bacteroidota from Bacillota, or Lachnospiraceae from other families, rather than risers from fallers as such. Many fallers are Lachnospiraceae with sugar phosphotransferase systems for the uptake of simple sugars, which a diet with less carbohydrate could feed less (hypothesis). The risers include Bacteroidota whose SusC/SusD systems and glycan families could let them use complex plant polysaccharides and host mucus glycans when dietary sugars are scarce [5, 6], and succinate-route propionate producers (hypothesis). Because the groups differ in taxonomy, these traits cannot be separated from which lineages rose and fell.

Discussion

In this ten-day series from one person, community structure differed between the two diet periods, and specific MAGs rose or fell consistently, in line with Hypothesis 1. The pattern was robust to day resampling, leaving out single days, other cut-offs, quality filtering and a compositional transform. The change came one to two days after the switch rather than at it, for the community as a whole and for most single MAGs. One explanation is that composition depends on several days of dietary history [2], consistent with diet changing community structure within days [1]. Bacteroidota is the exception: it rose at the switch and then fell, and the two phases may be different responses (for example, to the change in food and then to lower calories), which ten days cannot resolve. The fallers include the butyrate-producing lineages that earlier work saw decline with less carbohydrate [3], consistent with a diet effect; however, diet is confounded with time and order. The sampled person ate a diet close to their normal diet in days 6-10 and a richer diet before and during days 1-5, so the shift may equally reflect a return towards a habitual state; and because carbohydrate and calories changed together, neither can be singled out.

Hypothesis 2 is not supported in any large way. Total CAZyme potential of the community changed by a few percent with an interval that includes zero, and no common family changed 1.5-fold. Host-glycan genes rose and starch and fructan genes fell, with intervals on one side of zero, but these are a few genes per 1,000 and were among several groups examined. One reading is functional redundancy: MAGs with similar repertoires replaced each other, so the community kept its toolkit while its membership changed (our interpretation). The small shifts trace to Bacteroidota, whose utilisation loci make them major carriers of CAZymes [4, 5]. Gene content says nothing about how much each enzyme is used; in [1] diet changed microbial gene expression, which a DNA-only study cannot see. When only the MAGs that changed two-fold were weighted, the potential did shift (% for all CAZyme genes, % for host glycan), which shows that the MAGs that changed differ in their repertoires, but that they hold too small a part of the binned DNA to move the community value much.

The comparison of risers and fallers points the same way, but mostly along taxonomic lines: risers carry Bacteroidota traits for complex and host glycans (SusC/SusD, GH92, GH97) and the succinate route to propionate, and fallers carry the sugar phosphotransferase genes of Lachnospiraceae and other Bacillota. Whether these traits caused the rise or fall cannot be told here. They come with the lineages, few of them hold within the Bacillota alone, and the screen is exploratory with many features tested.

Limitations. The design has one person and one switch: days are not replicates, so no change over time is tested (the only tests compare MAGs in the exploratory question), and diet is confounded with time, order and the preceding week of the same high-calorie diet. Shares of DNA are relative abundances, not cell counts, and only the binned -% of bases are counted. Sequencing depth was higher in days 6-10; well-covered MAGs show the same pattern, but within days 1-5 deeper samples sat further along axis 1 (Spearman ρ = on five days), which we cannot explain. The plant-fibre result depends on two medium-quality P. copri_B MAGs. CAZyme calls come from one HMM search, substrate groups are broad, and incomplete MAGs miss genes (completeness and CAZyme genes per 1,000 genes were unrelated, Spearman ρ = ). Gene potential is not expression or activity.

Outlook. Several questions remain open: whether the community returns towards a usual state (samples from a normal week were not measured), whether the two P. copri_B MAGs are one genome split in two or two strains, whether butyrate potential fell with the Bacillota, which glycans the rising and falling Bacteroidota specialise in, and whether the unbinned fraction of the bases changes in the same way. Replicate individuals, ideally with food records and a baseline, and measurements of expression or metabolites, would be needed to test the hypotheses raised here.

Data availability

The sequencing reads are not publicly available, to protect the privacy of the sample donor (stool metagenomes contain host-derived DNA). Processed data behind every figure and supplementary table (derived tables of the group's own sequencing run) can be downloaded from this page as CSV or TSV. The analysis was run with the course's analysis script (Python with pandas and numpy; it runs in seconds on a laptop); the code is available from the course teacher.
