Delayed community shift and level carbohydrate-enzyme gene content after a diet change in one person

Authors: Group 1, Nanopore metagenomics course 2026 (author names are not published on this site). Built from the group's project report: version 2, version 1. All analyses were done once, on one data set, in the report; nothing was re-analysed for this article, and every number in the text comes from the data behind the figures. The article follows the structure of The ISME Journal but is not a submitted manuscript. Hover over a chart to read its values.

Abstract

The gut microbiome can follow a change of diet within days, but genome-resolved daily records of the timing of this response, and of the community's carbohydrate-degrading gene content, are scarce. We sequenced ten consecutive daily stool samples from one person with Oxford Nanopore long reads and recovered metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs). According to the data owner, days 1-5 were a high-carbohydrate, high-calorie and days 6-10 a low-carbohydrate, low-calorie vegetarian diet. Mean Bray-Curtis dissimilarity was within days 1-5, within days 6-10 and between the periods. Day 6, the first day of the new diet, still resembled days 1-5 (dissimilarity to day 1, ); the distance to day 1 rose from on day 7 to on day 8. Of MAGs that changed at least two-fold between the periods ( down, up), reached their new level on day 6 and only on day 9 or 10; the two Prevotella copri_B MAGs were still falling on day 10. Degrading carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZyme) genes per 1,000 genes of the community stayed near in days 1-5 and in days 6-10, because Bacteroidaceae and Lachnospiraceae with different gene sets replaced one another; among the changing MAGs alone the value rose after the switch and fell again by day 10. These are descriptions of one person over ten days: diet is confounded with time, carbohydrate and calories changed together, and the data show relative abundance of DNA and gene potential, not cell numbers or enzyme activity.

Keywords: gut microbiome; diet; nanopore sequencing; metagenome-assembled genomes; Bray-Curtis dissimilarity; carbohydrate-active enzymes; time series

Introduction

The gut community can follow a change of diet quickly. In a controlled feeding study of ten people, the community changed detectably within 24 hours of a new diet, while the broad community type stayed the same over the 10-day study [1]. When people ate only animal or only plant products for a few days, the community changed within days; the animal-based diet raised Bacteroides and lowered Firmicutes that break down plant polysaccharides, such as Roseburia and Eubacterium rectale [2]. Daily sampling of 34 people showed that the community depends on several days of diet history, and that responses differ between people [3].

A stool sample is not a snapshot of one day's food. Gut transit time varies between people and is itself linked to the community and its metabolism [4, 5], so a delay between a diet switch and a change in stool can come from transit, from the time the community needs to change, or both. Which single genomes change, and when, is rarely resolved day by day, and it is open whether the carbohydrate-degrading gene content of the community follows the same timing. Long-read metagenomics recovers many near-complete genomes from a set of samples [6], which makes it possible to follow single genomes and their gene content through a time series.

Here we use ten consecutive daily stool samples from one person. According to the data owner (information from the data owner, not measured by us), days 1-5 were a high-carbohydrate, high-calorie diet and days 6-10 a low-carbohydrate, low-calorie diet; both were vegetarian. The person had eaten a similar high-calorie diet for about a week before day 1, and the days 6-10 diet was closer to the person's normal diet. The diet therefore changed once, between day 5 and day 6, and carbohydrate and calories changed together.

We ask three descriptive questions. (1) How different are the community compositions of the ten days from each other, and does the shift between days 1-5 and days 6-10 start on day 6 or later? (2) Which genomes change between the periods, and on which day does each reach its new level? (3) How does the community's carbohydrate-degrading gene content change over the ten days, and which of the changing genomes carry which enzymes? The expectation, written before the analysis, was that days 1-6 would be alike, that a change would start from day 7 and that days 8-10 would be the most different. A hypothesis on strain diversity during the transition could not be tested with these data and is mentioned in the Discussion.

Materials and methods

Samples and diet

One person gave one stool sample per day for ten days (n = 10 samples). The diet description is information from the data owner (see Introduction); no food records, no sample from before day 1 or after day 10, and no transit-time, metabolite or cell-count data are available.

Sequencing, assembly and binning

DNA from the ten samples was sequenced on the Oxford Nanopore platform (R10.4.1 chemistry). The reads of all ten samples were assembled together and binned with mmlong2 [6] (myloasm , minimap2 , CoverM ), which gave MAGs. Taxonomy was assigned with GTDB-Tk .

Share of a MAG on each day

Each day's reads, after quality filtering and adapter trimming, were mapped with minimap2 to the contigs of the co-assembly (primary alignments only). For each contig and day, the mean depth (the number of reads covering an average position, leaving out about 75 bp at each contig end) was computed with MetaBAT2's jgi_summarize_bam_contig_depths, counting only alignments of at least 95% identity. Depth times contig length gives about the bases of that day's reads aligned to the contig, and the sum over the contigs of a MAG the bases on the MAG. These bases divided by all bases sequenced on that day, times 100, give the share of the MAG in percent of the sample's bases. Bases are used rather than read counts because the reads differ in length. The MAGs hold -% of each sample's bases; the rest is outside MAGs and is left out. A share of the DNA is not a cell count.

Bray-Curtis dissimilarity and reference states

Bray-Curtis dissimilarity (0 = same composition, 1 = nothing shared) was computed on the MAG shares between every pair of days. To follow the community day by day, each day was also compared with reference states: day 1, the mean composition of days 2-5 (early state) and the mean composition of days 8-10 (late state). The reasons for using period means as references are given with Figure 2 in the Results. The mean of all pairs within days 1-5, within days 6-10 and between the periods was computed from the pairwise values. As a test of the whole community, a PERMANOVA pseudo-F [7] was computed for days 1-5 versus days 6-10 on the Bray-Curtis distances, with an exact permutation test over all ways to assign five of the ten days to the first group (each division counted twice, once per group label, so different divisions); R2 is the share of the squared distances explained by the split. A check with Euclidean distances on simulated data, where pseudo-F must equal the ordinary analysis-of-variance F, passed.

Changing MAGs, settled day and robustness

For each MAG the change is log2 of the ratio of its mean share in days 6-10 to that in days 1-5, with % added to both so that a MAG absent in one period gives a finite value (1 = doubled, -1 = halved). A MAG counts as changing if its mean share over the ten days is at least % and it changes at least two-fold (log2 of at least 1 up or down). The midpoint is halfway between the two period means; the settled day is the first day from which the share of the MAG stays on the new side of its midpoint up to day 10. The two-fold rule is a description, not a test: there is no standard threshold for calling a taxon changed; two-fold was the usual cut-off in early gene-expression studies, and studies that combined a statistical test with a fold change used cut-offs between 1.3- and 4-fold [8]. Tests of differential abundance disagreed strongly across 38 data sets [9] and treat samples as independent replicates, which neighbouring days of one person are not; shares are compositional [10]; and one person's community varies from day to day without any intervention [11]. The list depends on the threshold: MAGs change at least 1.5-fold, at least two-fold, at least three-fold and at least four-fold. Robustness was checked by recomputing the changing rule and the settled day ten times, each time without one day (period means from the remaining days), and, per changing MAG, by the largest log2 change between consecutive days within days 1-5 as a measure of day-to-day noise.

Carbohydrate-active enzymes

Genes of all MAGs were predicted with Pyrodigal 3.7.1 [12] and searched against the dbCAN HMMs with PyHMMER 0.12.3 [13] (the HMM step of dbCAN only, without its consensus of three methods). Degrading families (glycoside hydrolases, polysaccharide lyases and carbohydrate esterases) were grouped by their main substrate following dbCAN3 [14]: plant fibre, starch, fructan, host glycan, mixed and other substrates. A gene with domains in two groups counts in both; "all degrading" counts each gene once. Per MAG, the value is degrading CAZyme genes per 1,000 predicted genes, so that it does not grow with genome size or completeness. For a group of MAGs on a day, each MAG was weighted by its share of the DNA times its number of genes. Groups were compared by medians; the numbers describe the MAGs and are not tested. Core MAGs are those with at least % of the bases on every day ( MAGs); MAGs are both core and changing.

Statistics and reporting

The ten days of one person are a time series, not replicates. The only test is the permutation test above, which treats days as independent and is therefore descriptive; elsewhere we report effect sizes and how much they depend on single days. Bray-Curtis values reproduce the course's overview analysis for all pairs, and the changing MAGs match the earlier analysis. All analyses were run with the course's analysis script; an independent reviewer recomputed the numbers of the report.

Why period means are used as reference states. A single day is a noisy reference: one day can differ from the next by as much as 0.276 without any change of diet (day 4 to day 5), and a distance to one day would then mix the effect we look for with that day's own deviation. The mean composition of several days is less sensitive to one unusual day, so we use two period means as the two states of the study design: the early state (mean of days 2-5) and the late state (mean of days 8-10). Day 1 is kept apart and shown as its own reference, because it followed about a week of a similar diet and so serves as a check that the early state is stable (day 1 is as close to days 2-5 as day 6 is). Days 6 and 7 are not part of the late state because they are the days in which the transition is sought, and days 8-10 are the second block seen in Figure 1; this choice was therefore made after looking at the data and is not independent of them. Plotting the distance to both states for every day makes the question "how far from each state" readable day by day: a day in the early state is close to the early mean and far from the late mean, and the day at which the two lines cross is the first day that is closer to the late state. In the plot, days 2-5 and 8-10 are part of their own reference mean, so their distance to it is lower by construction; the crossing at day is the first day closer to the mean of days 8-10 than to the mean of days 2-5.

Interpretation. A lag of about one day fits the time food needs to pass through the gut [4, 5]; most of the move happens within two days (days 7 and 8), and whether it continues more slowly after day 8 cannot be told from three days. The largest step, before the switch, shows how much one day can differ from the next without a diet change.

Discussion

The community of this person differed between days 1-5 and days 6-10, with a short delay: day 6, the first day of the new diet, was as close to days 1-5 as those days were to each other, the community moved on days 7 and 8, and no trend followed on days 9 and 10. A lag of about one day fits transit time [4, 5] and the feeding studies in which the community changed within one to a few days [1, 2]; a further day fits a community that depends on several days of diet history [3]. At the level of single genomes the picture was more varied: some genomes reached their new level on day 6 and others on day 9 or 10, and the largest late changes, the fall of the two Prevotella copri_B MAGs, were still under way at the end. The distance of a day to period-mean reference states is a transparent way to read the timing, but the reference days were chosen after looking at the data, and the settled day of a single MAG is uncertain by about a day.

The enzyme gene content of the community stayed level across the switch, apart from a small and transient rise after day 6, because Bacteroidaceae with large CAZyme sets and Lachnospiraceae with smaller sets replaced each other. Within a family, MAGs that changed and MAGs that did not carried similar CAZyme sets, so gene content does not explain which genomes changed; what separated rising from falling MAGs was mostly family. In gnotobiotic mice, Bacteroides turn to host mucus glycans when the diet holds no polysaccharides [15, 16], but a low-carbohydrate diet is not a fibre-free one, and gene content says nothing about which enzymes were active. The delayed rise of the changing MAGs' enzyme gene content (peak on days 7 and 8, return by day 10) coincides with the delayed change in their membership; with one rise and a high first day it is a description, and gene content is not a measure of enzyme expression.

Limitations. This is one person, one diet switch and one sample per day, so the timing is described and not tested, and responses differ between people [3]. Diet is confounded with time: days 6-10 are both the new diet and later, and a change that was under way anyway, or an event on day 6, would look the same; the largest day-to-day step comes before the switch. Carbohydrate and calories changed together, both diets were vegetarian, and the diet was not recorded in detail, so no cause can be named. The shares are percent of DNA on the binned MAGs (-% of the bases), not cell numbers, and a rise of one genome lowers the others. There is no sample before day 1 or after day 10, so neither the usual community nor the end of the change is observed, and the permutation test treats neighbouring days as independent. Gene content is potential, not activity, and transit time was not measured.

A second hypothesis, that strain diversity in the genomes present on every day is highest around the switch, could not be tested in this course: the variant file holds the reads of all ten days pooled, so there is no per-day strain diversity. A per-day analysis was prepared but not run. The results are hypotheses for studies with several people and with samples before and after the diet change.
