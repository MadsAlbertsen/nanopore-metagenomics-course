Species composition shifted ~1 day after the dietary switch.

Figure 1a, Revision 1 (Claude): Bray-Curtis dissimilarity of each day's species profile (MAG shares) to the mean of days 1–5.

Day 6 is the first day of the new diet, yet its profile is still within the range of days 1-5; the departure starts on day 7. Caveats: gut transit time was not measured, so "about one day" is a lag, not a transit time. One person, ten days; diet is confounded with time and calories, and days 6-10 were a return towards the habitual diet.

The Lachnospiraceae story is compelling — but is it more than statistics?

Hypothesis: many bacteria share the same enzyme genes — losing one doesn't matter.

Test: randomly reshuffle enzyme genes across bacteria times.

Our data lie inside the null range (th and th percentile) — averaging explains it, no extra redundancy needed.

Figures 2a and 2b, Revision 1 (Claude): replicates of the carrier-preserving null; left, change of the CAZyme profile between the periods; right, carrier number against day-to-day variation (Spearman). The gene-token null was even steadier than our data.

The null keeps the real daily abundances of the genomes and each family's number of carrier genomes, and only moves which genome carries which family. Summing across many genomes is naturally steadier than any single genome (the averaging effect). Real families varied slightly more than random ones, because carriers tended to rise and fall together; compensation between genomes was not detected.

When dietary carbohydrates dropped, genes for digesting the gut's own mucus layer (host glycans) increased beyond the null — this fits a shift towards host-glycan use, which these data cannot show directly.

Figure 4b, Revision 1 (Claude): gene potential per 1,000 genes (relative), days 6–10 versus days 1–5. One person, ten days; diet is confounded with time and calories.

Gene potential and relative abundance, not activity. The host-glycan rise came mostly from Bacteroidota genomes. It is the one group of families whose change exceeds what random carriers produce.

Study design

  • n=1 — description, not a test
  • Diet and time fully confounded
  • No baseline before the intervention
  • Calories and carbohydrates changed together

Data

  • Relative abundance only — not cell counts
  • Gene potential only — not activity or expression

The null model

  • Built after seeing the result, not pre-registered
  • Keeps carrier counts fixed — may be too well matched to detect a real signal

The data come from one person over ten days with one switch. Days 6-10 were a return towards the habitual diet, and a similar high-calorie diet ran about a week before day 1.

  1. Gut microbiome composition shifted ~1 day after a dietary switch.
  2. Apparent stability of gene potential is explained by averaging across genomes — no redundancy beyond averaging was needed.
  3. Host glycan genes are the exception — above the null, worth following up (diet and time cannot be separated).
  4. Redundancy claims from metagenomic data need a null model — otherwise averaging can look like biology.

One person, ten days — a pilot that generated testable predictions, not a test of them.
