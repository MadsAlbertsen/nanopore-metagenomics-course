Why does the enzyme toolkit move so much less than the bacteria?

The idea: functional redundancy — many bacteria carry the same enzyme genes, so when one falls, another covers for it.

We built a null model: same bacterial abundances each day, but enzyme gene families placed randomly on bacteria.

The null keeps the real daily abundances of the genomes and each family's number of carrier genomes, and only moves which genome carries which family.

Random reshuffling of enzyme genes gave a result like ours: our data lie inside the null range — the stability can be explained by averaging.

Summing across many genomes is naturally steadier than any single genome: the averaging effect. No extra compensation between genomes is needed to explain it.

Figures 2a and 2b, Revision 1 (Claude): replicates of the carrier-preserving null. The gene-token null was even steadier than our data. Percentiles: 2a th, 2b th.

Left: how much the CAZyme profile changed between days 1-5 and days 6-10. Right: how steeply day-to-day variation falls with the number of carrier genomes. Both observed values sit inside the null. Real families did vary slightly more than random ones, because carriers tended to rise and fall together; that is the opposite of compensation.

When dietary carbohydrates dropped, genes for digesting the gut's own mucus layer (host glycans) increased beyond the null — this fits a shift towards host-glycan use, which these data cannot show directly.

Figure 4b, Revision 1 (Claude): gene potential per 1,000 genes (relative), days 6–10 versus days 1–5. One person, ten days; diet is confounded with time and calories.

Gene potential and relative abundance, not activity. The host-glycan rise came mostly from Bacteroidota genomes. It is the one group of families whose change exceeds what random carriers produce.

  1. Gut microbiome composition shifted from day 7, about a day after the dietary switch.
  2. The apparent stability of gene potential is what averaging across genomes predicts — compensation between genomes was not detected.
  3. Host-glycan genes are the exception: their gene potential rose above the null, which fits a diet-related shift (diet and time cannot be separated here).

Claims of functional redundancy from metagenomic data need a null model — otherwise averaging alone can produce the same stability.
