Nanopore metagenomics course · group presentation

An “unusually honest” and highly biased gut microbiome study

Group 2 · one person, ten daily stool samples, a diet switch, and an AI doing the analysis

Title slide. The study is our submitted (not peer-reviewed) ISME-style article. The word "biased" is our verdict on how the AI chased results that fit the hypotheses; the next slides show the figures and our evaluation. The deck presents our own opinions about working with the AI.

Aim, hypotheses, prompts

Three hypotheses, tested by an AI that we prompted

Aim and hypotheses

  • Aim: follow the community, its genomes and its carbohydrate-degradation genes over a diet switch (ten daily samples, one person)
  • H1: community structure changes with the diet transition; specific MAGs consistently rise or fall
  • H2: carbohydrate-utilisation potential differs between the periods
  • H3 (exploratory): rising and falling MAGs share traits that differ between the groups

What we prompted

  • An uncertainty with every effect size
  • Account for compositional effects (relative abundance)
  • Step at the switch or gradual trend?
  • Abundance-weighted carbohydrate-degradation potential, and which MAGs drive it
  • Capabilities shared within risers and within fallers, metabolic and other

MAG: metagenome-assembled genome. Prompts as listed in the methods rationale of our report; the hypotheses are from our article. One person, ten days: diet is confounded with time and calories.

Aim: describe how one person's gut community, single genomes and carbohydrate-degradation genes change over ten daily nanopore samples. Days 1-5 high-carbohydrate, high-calorie diet; days 6-10 low-carbohydrate, low-calorie diet; both vegetarian (information from the data owner).

H1 community structure changes with the diet transition, with specific MAGs rising or falling. H2 carbohydrate-utilisation potential differs between periods. H3 is the exploratory question: which genomic traits are shared within rising MAGs and within falling MAGs but differ between them.

What we prompted: uncertainty with every effect size; compositional effects; separating phase-associated shifts from general time trends; abundance-weighted potential and which MAGs drive the change; whether the MAGs that changed differ in carbohydrate potential; capabilities shared within each group, split into metabolic and other. The wording is paraphrased from the methods rationale document of our report.

H1 · results

MAGs changed two-fold or more: rose, fell

Fig. 4a: the of common MAGs (mean share at least 0.1%) that changed at least two-fold; log2 change of the MAG's mean share of the bases, days 6-10 over days 1-5 (1 = doubled, -1 = halved). Line: 90% day-resampling interval (descriptive, not a confidence interval). Filled dot: still two-fold when any one day is left out. All MAGs that fell are Bacillota.

Of the 124 MAGs with a mean share of at least 0.1%, 35 changed at least two-fold between the period means: 12 fell (all Bacillota) and 23 rose. The line is the middle 90% of 2000 recalculations after drawing each period's five days at random with replacement. It shows how much a change depends on which days were sampled. Because these MAGs were selected for a large change, an interval that excludes zero is close to built in. Relative abundance, one person, diet confounded with time.

Our evaluation, not a result of the data.

Our opinion: the AI chose analyses that suit hypothesis 1 and made meaningful figures. Overall we are satisfied with how H1 was handled. This is our own evaluation of working with the AI.

H2 · our discussion

For H2 the AI invented reasonable metrics, but it should have been more critical

  • Metabolic potential is hard to quantify; the AI created its own metrics
  • The metrics are reasonable overall
  • It should have been more critical of its own results

Our evaluation, not a result of the data.

Our opinion: metabolic potential is hard to quantify, so the AI had to create metrics. They are reasonable overall, but we would have liked the AI to be more critical of its own results.

  • The analysis itself is not really meaningful

Our opinion: the AI did reasonably on H3. It did what we asked and acknowledged that the hypothesis was ambitious, but the analysis itself is not really meaningful. At least it drew no ridiculous conclusions.

Our evaluation of the AI

Good at choosing analyses, poor at drawing meaningful conclusions

How it did

  • H1Well: good analysis, meaningful figures
  • H2Okay: metrics okay, too uncritical
  • H3Okay: admitted the hypothesis was ambitious, no ridiculous conclusions, but analysis pretty meaningless

Pitfalls

  • Skipped checks of sequencing, assembly and binning: experience still tells which checks are needed
  • Only analyses what you ask; does not say when an analysis is not meaningful (H3)
  • The peer reviewer caught what the first agent missed (e.g. big depth differences), but human experience is still needed
  • Needs a lot of context
  • Seems to chase results that support the hypothesis: a bias towards positive results, a problem if manuscripts are not properly assessed
  • AIs are tools made to satisfy customers: know their biases, judge output critically

Our own opinions about working with the AI.

How it did. H1: well, good analysis and meaningful visualisations. H2: okay, the metrics were okay but it should have been more critical. H3: okay, it acknowledged that the hypothesis was ambitious and drew no ridiculous conclusions, but the analysis was pretty meaningless.

Pitfalls. (1) It left out an evaluation of sequencing, assembly and binning. Experience still matters: you have to know which quality checks are needed. (2) It only analyses what you ask and does not say when an analysis is not meaningful (H3). (3) The peer reviewer caught things the first agent missed, for example big differences in sequencing depth. The reviewer helps, but human experience is still needed. (4) It needs a lot of context. (5) It is good at choosing analyses but poor at drawing meaningful conclusions, and it seems to chase results that support the hypothesis: a bias towards positive results that is a problem if manuscripts are not properly assessed. (6) AIs are tools made to satisfy customers: knowing their biases and evaluating the output critically is essential.
