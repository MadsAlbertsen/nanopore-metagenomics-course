How does the relative abundance of B. angulatum change when the diet goes from high to low carbohydrate?

The group's own words: Decided to look into one specific organism instead of the entire community. We knew that the sequencing would get us tons of data, so instead of trying to look at all of it, we narrowed it into one specific organism. Deciding for a specific organism was more difficult but from a very quick look in the data we identified the presence of a Bifidobacteria which was described in literature as a common gut microbiome carbohydrate degrading organism. Therefore, we decided to look into how the relative abundance of that specific microbe changed as a result of the change in diet from high carb to low carb. We based our analysis on the hypothesis that the relative abundance of Bifidobacterium angulatum would be high in the beginning of day 1 (because he was carb-loading for a week before) and would continue to be high until day 5, after which it would decrease from day 6-10 as a result of the low carb diet.

Diet: days 1-5 followed about a week of a similar high-carbohydrate diet (information from the data owner). The hypothesis is the group's own.

One stool sample per day for ten days from one person. Days 1-5 high in carbohydrate and calories, days 6-10 low in both; both periods vegetarian (information from the data owner). Lab workflow: DNA extraction, library with the Rapid Barcoding Kit (SQK-RBK114.24), all ten samples on one PromethION flow cell (R10.4.1). Reads were then basecalled, assembled together and binned into MAGs (genomes reconstructed from the mixture).

First check: % of the sequenced bases are in MAGs

Share (%) of each day's filtered bases (reads ≥ 1,000 bp, mean Q ≥ 10). Figure 1 of the group's article. Dashed line: diet switch.

The group first asked the AI agent to analyse abundance and carbohydrate-related genes, but realised they first needed to assess the quality of the data. They therefore asked how much of the sequencing data was represented in MAGs, what was missing, and how sequencing depth varied between samples. Percentages are shares of bases (not reads). Depth is the filtered bases per sample, in Gbp. MAG = genome reconstructed from the mixture; HQ and MQ = high and medium quality.

The group asked how reliable the gene annotations were, including Bakta's sequence identity threshold (at least 50% identity to a protein cluster; Bakta v1.11.4, light database), and whether GH13 genes are common across other MAGs (they are: 87% of the 421 MAGs carry at least one). Highlighted: 83 carbohydrate-related genes (39 carbohydrate-degrading enzymes, 21 glycosyltransferases, 12 sugar transporter parts and others) and 9 GH13 genes. Gene names are predictions; they do not show that the enzymes are active.
