Bifidobacterium angulatum: its genes and its share over ten days

A short research report on one genome from the ten stool samples: the MAG of Bifidobacterium angulatum (MAG ), compared with the other two Bifidobacterium MAGs of the study. Interpretation is marked as such ("this suggests", "one explanation is"); everything else is a measured result of this study or a cited source.

Summary

We asked what the genome of B. angulatum in this gut can do, with a focus on carbohydrate-active enzymes, and whether its share of the DNA falls after the switch from a high- to a low-carbohydrate diet, as the group predicted. The genome is one circular contig of (CheckM2 completeness %) with protein-coding genes. It carries genes for carbohydrate-degrading enzymes, about as many as the median MAG () in a genome with fewer genes. The largest family is GH13, a starch-degrading family ( genes; of MAGs have more). Compared with its two relatives it has fewer enzymes for plant-fibre sugars ( genes against and ). Its mean share fell from % of the bases on days 1-5 to % on days 6-10 (log2 change ), but single days varied more than that: the share peaked on day (%), was still high on day 6 (%) and low from day 7 on. Our interpretation: in this gut B. angulatum is a user of starch and its oligosaccharides whose share follows the carbohydrate supply with a delay of about a day. With one person, ten days and one sample per day this is a description, not a test.

Background

Bifidobacteria live on carbohydrates that escape digestion in the small intestine, many of them plant oligo- and polysaccharides, and their genomes encode many carbohydrate-modifying enzymes; strains differ in which sugars they can use [1]. A comparison of 47 Bifidobacterium (sub)species found more genes for breaking down and taking up carbohydrates than in many other gut bacteria, and suggested that species specialise and share sugars by cross-feeding [2]. Bifidobacteria break sugars down by the "bifid shunt", in which the enzyme phosphoketolase splits fructose 6-phosphate and xylulose 5-phosphate [3]. B. angulatum is less studied than B. longum or B. adolescentis; the complete genome of its type strain, isolated from human faeces, was published in 2015 [4]. In intervention studies with resistant starch, B. adolescentis is one of the bacteria that increase most consistently, although responses vary between people and studies [5]. Gut communities can respond to a change of diet within days [6].

The study: one person gave a stool sample on each of ten days. Both diet periods were vegetarian. Days 1-5 were high in carbohydrate and calories, days 6-10 low in carbohydrate and calories, closer to the subject's normal diet. The subject had eaten a similar high-calorie diet for about a week before day 1, so the switch at day 6 is the main change (information from the data owner). All ten samples were sequenced on one nanopore flow cell and assembled together into MAGs (genomes reconstructed from the mixture).

Question and hypotheses

The group asked which functions B. angulatum has in this gut and predicted that its share falls when the diet switches to low carbohydrate. This report answers two narrower questions the data can show. (1) Which genes does the genome carry, and which carbohydrates could its enzymes break down? A gene in the genome shows what the organism could do, not what it does. (2) What share of each day's DNA comes from this genome? The group spoke of reads classified as B. angulatum; here the share is the part of each day's bases that maps to the MAG, which is how the analysis measured abundance (reads were not classified one by one). Hypotheses written down before the numbers: the share falls, from near % on days 1-5 to near % on days 6-10, small in absolute terms (H1); the genome carries many enzymes for starch and plant oligosaccharides (families GH13, GH43, GH42, GH2), as known for bifidobacteria [1, 2] (H2).

Data and methods

All data come from the course's own sequencing run of the ten samples (module 8 lists every file). Reads were assembled and binned with mmlong2 (module 4). Share: percent of a sample's sequenced bases that map to the MAG, one value per MAG and day; the period mean is the mean of five days; the change is log2 of the ratio of the period means, with % added to both (the definition all group reports use). A share is a share of the DNA, not a count of cells, and it is relative: if other bacteria grow, the share falls even when this one does not change. Genome quality: CheckM2 (module 5). Genes: Bakta with its database [7]; a gene's name rests on a match to a protein cluster (identity to the cluster's representative of at least %; module 7). CAZymes: every MAG's predicted proteins were searched with the dbCAN protein models as dbCAN3 runs them [8]; a gene counts when a domain has an E-value below and covers more than % of the model. Degrading enzymes are glycoside hydrolases (GH), polysaccharide lyases (PL) and carbohydrate esterases (CE); each family gets a substrate category (starch, fructan, plant fibre, host glycan, mixed, other) from dbCAN's family-substrate table. Named genes: Bakta's names searched for a few groups (for example "phosphoketolase", "bile salt hydrolase", "CRISPR"), in the three MAGs and, for two groups, in all MAGs. Co-variation: Spearman rank correlation of the ten daily shares. Checks: the daily shares equal the study's abundance table; the gene and evidence counts equal Bakta's summary and module 7; the CAZyme counts equal the shared project tables; the script stops if a check fails. Analysis: np26 scripts/45_project_group5.py.

But within days 1-5 alone single days ranged -fold (-%), and only days were below every day of the first period. The shape is a rise to a peak on day , a high day 6, then a drop on day 7. One explanation is that the carbohydrate-rich food, eaten for about a week before day 1 and on days 1-5, let starch users grow, and that the stool of day 6 still reflected food eaten before the switch. Alternatives we cannot rule out: one food eaten on days 4-5 rather than the diet as a whole; day-to-day variation of a small population (about of every million bases); or a relative effect, if other bacteria fell on those days.

Per 1,000 genes it carries degrading enzymes, against in the median MAG, so these enzymes take up a larger part of its genome than of most. This suggests a niche based on starch and the oligosaccharides made from it, in line with the carbohydrate-centred lifestyle of bifidobacteria [1, 2]. Its two relatives carry more plant-fibre enzymes ( and against ), notably GH43, a plant-fibre family in dbCAN's table ( and genes against ); B. adolescentis also has more GH13 (). One reading is that the three divide the carbohydrates between them, as suggested for bifidobacteria in general [2]; another is that the differences are strain properties that say little about this gut. B. angulatum has GH32 genes (fructans such as inulin) against in each relative. Few host-glycan enzymes () suggest that host glycans such as mucus are not its main food. Whether these enzymes act outside the cell or on sugars already taken up cannot be told from these counts.

4. Other named genes

We searched Bakta's gene names for a few functions known from bifidobacteria and other gut bacteria, and counted them in the three MAGs.

Bottom: the matching genes of B. angulatum with position, evidence for the name and identity to the matched cluster's representative protein (a match needs % or more).

Interpretation. All three MAGs carry a phosphoketolase (in B. angulatum at % cluster identity), the enzyme of the bifid shunt [3], and a gene Bakta names "Bile salt hydrolase/transferase" (% identity). Such a gene is common: Bakta names one in of the MAGs here (%), and it is found in nearly all cultivated bifidobacteria [9]. Purified bifidobacterial bile salt hydrolases deconjugated all six major human bile salts, and the authors suggest this may help the bacteria colonise the gut [10]; whether this gene is active here is not shown. B. angulatum has CRISPR-associated genes in one block (type I-E by their names) and CRISPR arrays; B. adolescentis has such genes, B. longum . CRISPR-associated proteins are named in of all MAGs (%). One interpretation is that this strain has met phages, as spacers in bifidobacterial CRISPR arrays match bifidobacterial prophages [11]; matching the spacers of these arrays to contigs of this study would test that here. The sugar ABC transporter parts are how the sugars freed by the enzymes would be taken up.

Discussion

H1, a fall after the switch, holds for the period means (log2 ), but the fall is small next to the day-to-day spread, and its timing (peak on day , drop on day 7) is the most informative part. That the two relatives peak on the same days makes pure chance a less likely explanation for the peak, and a common cause, such as the carbohydrate supply at the end of the high-carbohydrate period, a more likely one. But three related bacteria in one gut are not independent observations, and a diet change can move many members of a gut community within days [6]. H2, many starch and plant-oligosaccharide enzymes, holds for starch (GH13) and partly for plant sugars: GH43, GH42 and GH2 are present but fewer than in the relatives. Together this suggests that B. angulatum in this person draws on starch-derived sugars, which a high-carbohydrate vegetarian diet supplies, and that its share is limited by that supply; resistant-starch studies point the same way for B. adolescentis [5]. What is solid: the genome (one circular contig, complete by CheckM2), its gene content and the ten measured shares. What is uncertain: whether the genes are used, how much of the change is the diet, and whether the share of DNA follows the number of living cells.

Limitations

  • One person, ten days, one sample per day, one diet switch: the results describe this gut; they do not test a diet effect.
  • Shares are of DNA and relative: a change can come from this bacterium or from the others, and DNA from dead cells counts too.
  • Genes are predicted from sequence; names rest on cluster matches at % identity or more; presence is not activity.
  • The CAZyme search used one of dbCAN's three methods (the protein models), so some families may be missed or miscounted.
  • About of every million bases come from this genome, so small differences in sequencing depth matter more than for abundant MAGs.

Open questions and possible next analyses

  • Which sugars does it take up? Match the sugar transporters to the enzymes next to them on the chromosome (gene neighbourhoods); the positions are in this report's data (on the Mac).
  • Starch users together: do other MAGs with many GH13 genes peak on the same days? Uses the shared tables of all MAGs (on the Mac).
  • Phages: do the spacers of the CRISPR arrays match phage or plasmid contigs of this study? Needs the spacer sequences and the assembly (assembly files on the Mac, or a server job).
  • Strains: is there one B. angulatum strain or several? Variable positions in each day's reads (a server job).
  • Food: which foods were eaten (for example dairy, bread, legumes)? Not recorded; it would have to come from the subject.
  • Activity: are the starch genes used, and more on high-carbohydrate days? Needs RNA or protein measurements, not taken in this study.
