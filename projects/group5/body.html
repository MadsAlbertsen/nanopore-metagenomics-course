Bifidobacterium angulatum: its genes and its share over ten days

This report looks at one genome from the stool samples: the MAG of Bifidobacterium angulatum (MAG ). It asks what the genome can do, carbohydrate-active enzymes in particular, and how its share of the DNA changes from the high-carbohydrate days (1-5) to the low-carbohydrate days (6-10). The other two Bifidobacterium MAGs of the study are shown next to it for comparison.

The question

The group asked which functions B. angulatum has in this gut, and predicted that its share falls when the diet switches to low carbohydrate. This report answers two narrower questions that the data can show. First: which genes does the genome carry, and which of them break down carbohydrates (glycoside hydrolases and related enzymes, grouped by the sugar they act on)? A gene in the genome says what the organism could do; it does not show that the gene is used. Second: what share of each day's DNA comes from this genome, on each of the ten days? The group spoke of reads classified as B. angulatum; here the share is the part of each day's bases that maps to the MAG, which is how the analysis measured abundance. Reads were not classified one by one. An answer would be the ten daily shares with the diet switch marked, and the genome's carbohydrate enzymes next to those of its two relatives.

Guided examples

Data used

All data come from the course's own sequencing run of the ten daily samples (module 8 lists every file). The share per day is the percent of a sample's bases that map to the MAG, one value per MAG and day (module 4). The genome itself is one circular contig of (% GC), rated high quality by CheckM2 (completeness %, contamination %; module 5). Its genes and their names come from Bakta, one row per gene (module 7). Carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) come from a search of every MAG's predicted proteins with the dbCAN protein models; each enzyme family has a substrate category from dbCAN's family table (starch, fructan, plant fibre, host glycan, mixed or other). The community comparison uses all MAGs. Diet: both periods were vegetarian; days 1-5 high carbohydrate and high calorie, days 6-10 low carbohydrate and low calorie, closer to the subject's normal diet. The subject had eaten a similar high-calorie diet for about a week before day 1, so the switch at day 6 is the main change (information from the data owner).

Approach

Step 1: the ten daily shares of the three Bifidobacterium MAGs, the mean of each diet period (five days each), the change between the periods as log2 of the ratio of the means (with 0.01% added to both means, as in the other reports), and the lowest and highest day within each period. Step 2: a map of every gene on the circular chromosome, coloured by the evidence for its name or by CAZyme class. Step 3: CAZyme genes that break down carbohydrates (glycoside hydrolases, polysaccharide lyases and carbohydrate esterases), counted per substrate category and per family, against the median MAG. Step 4: a few groups of named genes found by searching Bakta's gene names. Written down before the numbers: a share near 0.13% on days 1-5 and 0.07% on days 6-10, and many starch and plant-sugar enzymes (families GH13, GH43, GH42, GH2). Checks: the daily shares equal the study's abundance table, the gene count () and evidence counts equal Bakta's summary and module 7, and the CAZyme counts equal the shared project tables. The script stops if one check fails.

What this does and does not show

The genome is essentially complete: one circular contig with genes, % of them without a name. It carries carbohydrate-degrading enzyme genes, as many as the median MAG () in a smaller genome ( genes in the enzyme search, median MAG ); the largest family is (starch, genes), and a phosphoketolase (cluster identity %; in textbooks the key enzyme of the "bifid shunt", the sugar pathway of bifidobacteria) and a "Bile salt hydrolase/transferase" (Bakta's name, cluster identity %) are there, as in the other two Bifidobacterium MAGs. The share fell between the periods (mean % to %), as the group predicted, but the highest single day (% on day ) is right before the switch and the day after it is still high (%); only days are below every day of the first period. This is one person over ten days, one sample per day: it describes this gut, it does not test a diet effect. The share is a share of DNA, not a count of cells, and at about bases per million it is a small part of the community. Genes are predicted from sequence: present, not shown to be used.

Next steps and questions for tomorrow

1. Both diet periods were vegetarian, and the subject had eaten a similar high-calorie diet for about a week before day 1, so the switch at day 6 is the main change; the low-carbohydrate diet of days 6-10 was closer to the subject's normal diet (information from the data owner). Days 1-5 are therefore not a starting point but the end of a week on that diet. Which foods were eaten (for example milk products) is not recorded; would that help the question? 2. Which sugars could B. angulatum take up? The sugar transporters in Figure 4 could be matched to the enzymes next to them on the map (gene neighbourhoods), using the gene positions already in this report. 3. Is the peak on days 5-6 a response to the diet or day-to-day noise? A second person or more days would be needed to tell; with these data, the other two Bifidobacterium MAGs, which peak on day (B. adolescentis) and day (B. longum), are the only comparison. 4. Are the genes used? That needs RNA (gene expression), which this study did not measure.
