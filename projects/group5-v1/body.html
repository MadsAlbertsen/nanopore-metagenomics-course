Bifidobacterium angulatum: its genes and its share over ten days

A short research report on one genome from the ten stool samples: the MAG of Bifidobacterium angulatum (MAG ). It follows the group's own plan: a goal, a research question and a hypothesis, and the three outputs the group asked for, a graph of the share in percent over the ten days (Figure 1), a table with one row per sample (Tables 1 and 2) and an annotated gene map with the carbohydrate genes (Figure 2 and Table 3). Interpretation is marked as such ("this suggests", "one explanation is"); everything else is a measured result of this study or a cited source.

Summary

The B. angulatum genome is one circular contig of (CheckM2 completeness %) with protein-coding genes. of them have a carbohydrate role: carbohydrate-degrading enzymes in families, glycosyltransferases, sugar transporter parts, the bifid-shunt enzyme phosphoketolase and genes that convert galactose, xylose and arabinose. Against the median MAG it carries more genes for starch ( against ) and plant fibre ( against ). Its share of each day's DNA was small, -% of the bases. The mean fell from % on days 1-5 to % on days 6-10, as the group predicted, but single days varied more: the share rose to a peak on day , was still high on day 6 and dropped from day 7 on. Counting reads directly in a small random read sample found the most reads on days 5-6 too, but with - reads per sample there are too few to show the day-to-day pattern. Our interpretation: a genome built for carbohydrates, whose share follows the diet change only in part. With one person, ten days and one sample per day this is a description, not a test.

Goal

The group wants to understand which role B. angulatum may play in this gut. Two lines of evidence are combined: what its genes are predicted to do, and how much of the gut DNA it makes up from day to day while the diet changes. The genome tells what the bacterium could do; the daily shares tell whether it became more or less common. Neither shows what it actually did in the gut, so the report keeps the two apart and marks every link between them as interpretation.

Research question

Which functions do the genes of B. angulatum predict, with carbohydrate metabolism in focus, and how does its relative abundance vary over ten consecutive days that span a switch from a high-carbohydrate, high-calorie diet (days 1-5) to a low-carbohydrate, low-calorie diet (days 6-10)? Here relative abundance is the percent of a day's sequenced DNA that comes from the genome, measured in two ways (see Data and methods). Gene functions are predictions from sequence: a gene's name and enzyme family, not a measured activity.

Hypothesis

The group's reasoning: B. angulatum is saccharolytic, that is, it lives mainly by breaking down carbohydrates [1, 2], and bifidobacteria in general tend, in the group's view, to become more common when carbohydrates are plentiful and less common when they are scarce. The group therefore expected the share of B. angulatum to fall after the switch from the high- to the low-carbohydrate diet. From the scope card (the values were known from the first version of this report), this report expected the share to fall from near % on days 1-5 to near % on days 6-10, small in absolute terms (H1), and the genome to carry many enzymes for starch and plant oligosaccharides (families GH13, GH43, GH42 and GH2), as known for bifidobacteria (H2). Ten days from one person can support or weaken these expectations; they cannot test them statistically.

Background

Bifidobacteria live on carbohydrates that escape digestion in the small intestine, many of them plant oligo- and polysaccharides, and their genomes encode many carbohydrate-modifying enzymes; strains differ in which sugars they can use [1]. A comparison of 47 Bifidobacterium (sub)species found more genes for breaking down and taking up carbohydrates than in many other gut bacteria, and suggested that species specialise and share sugars by cross-feeding [2]. Bifidobacteria break sugars down by the "bifid shunt", in which the enzyme phosphoketolase splits fructose 6-phosphate and xylulose 5-phosphate [3]. B. angulatum is less studied than B. longum or B. adolescentis; the complete genome of its type strain, isolated from human faeces, was published in 2015 [4]. In intervention studies with resistant starch, B. adolescentis is one of the bacteria that increase most consistently, although responses vary between people and studies [5]. Gut communities can respond to a change of diet within days [6].

The study: one person gave a stool sample on each of ten days. Both diet periods were vegetarian. Days 1-5 were high in carbohydrate and calories, days 6-10 low in carbohydrate and calories, closer to the subject's normal diet. The subject had eaten a similar high-calorie diet for about a week before day 1, so the switch at day 6 is the main change (information from the data owner). All ten samples were sequenced on one nanopore flow cell and assembled together into MAGs (genomes reconstructed from the mixture). Two other Bifidobacterium MAGs, B. adolescentis and B. longum, serve as comparison.

Data and methods

All data come from the course's own sequencing run of the ten samples (module 8 lists every file). Reads were assembled and binned with mmlong2 (module 4). Share of bases (the main measure): percent of a sample's sequenced bases that map to the MAG, from all reads of the sample; this is how the study measured abundance. Share of reads (the group's "reads classified as B. angulatum"): the course's random read sample (about reads per sample, - after the study's own length and quality filter; module 2); a read counts for the MAG that holds its best alignment on the assembly (most matching bases), and its share is the percent of the sample's filtered reads. Reads were not classified by a taxonomy tool. A read share is of reads, a base share of bases; both are relative, so a change can also come from other bacteria.

Period statistics: mean, median, standard deviation (SD), coefficient of variation (CV = SD as % of the mean), minimum and maximum of the five days of each period; day-to-day change is log2 of the ratio of consecutive days (1 = doubling, -1 = halving); the change between periods is log2 of the ratio of the period means, with % added to both. Genes: Bakta with its database [7]; a gene's name rests on a match to a protein cluster (identity to the cluster's representative of at least %; module 7). CAZymes: every MAG's predicted proteins were searched with the dbCAN protein models as dbCAN3 runs them [8]; a gene counts when a domain has an E-value below and covers more than % of the model. Degrading enzymes are glycoside hydrolases (GH), polysaccharide lyases (PL) and carbohydrate esterases (CE); the predicted substrates of a family are up to three that dbCAN's family-substrate table lists for it (most listed first, ties alphabetical), and each degrading family gets a substrate category (starch, fructan, plant fibre, host glycan, mixed, other) from that table. Other carbohydrate genes: Bakta names searched for sugar transporters, the bifid shunt (phosphoketolase, transaldolase, transketolase), sugar conversion (galactose genes, xylose and L-arabinose isomerase) and the beta-galactosidase small subunit; a check confirms that four such genes found by hand are picked up. Checks: the daily base shares equal the study's abundance table; the read sample reproduces the study's base shares for the MAGs with a mean share of at least 1% (rank correlation , median ratio ) and the binned share of each sample's bases to within percentage points; gene and CAZyme counts equal Bakta's summary, module 7 and the shared project tables. The script stops if a check fails. Analysis: np26 scripts/45_project_group5.py.

Interpretation. Of genes, % got their name from a protein-cluster match and % remain hypothetical proteins: the genome is well covered by known proteins, which fits a cultivated species with a sequenced type strain [4]. The carbohydrate genes are spread over the whole chromosome, often several next to each other: degrading enzymes beside sugar transporters (for example the GH43, GH8 and GH1 genes next to a sugar ABC transporter near the start, or a sucrose transporter beside a GH32 sucrose-6-phosphate hydrolase), which suggests sugar-specific gene clusters, as described for bifidobacteria [1, 2]. All enzymes are predictions: the names rest on cluster matches that are often far from identical, and a gene that is present is not shown to be used.

Interpretation. All three MAGs carry a phosphoketolase (in B. angulatum at % cluster identity), the enzyme of the bifid shunt [3], and a gene Bakta names "Bile salt hydrolase/transferase" (% identity). Such a gene is common: Bakta names one in of the MAGs here (%), and such genes were found in 99.76% of the cultivated human-gut Bifidobacterium genomes in one large survey (1,512 genomes, 849 non-redundant) [9]. Purified bifidobacterial bile salt hydrolases deconjugated all six major human bile salts, and the authors suggest this may help the bacteria colonise the gut [10]; whether this gene is active here is not shown. The sugar ABC transporter parts are how the sugars freed by the enzymes would be taken up.

Discussion

H1, a fall after the switch, holds for the period means (log2 ), but the fall is small next to the day-to-day spread. The timing matters more: the share rose -fold within the high-carbohydrate period, on a diet that had not changed for about a week, peaked on day and dropped on day 7. The diet switch cannot explain the rise; the carbohydrate supply could explain the drop after day 6, but so could a particular food eaten on days 4-5, day-to-day variation of a small population, or a relative effect of other bacteria changing. That the two relatives peak on the same days points to a common cause rather than chance for each one, but three related bacteria in one gut are not independent observations, and a diet change can move many members of a gut community within days [6]. H2, many starch and plant-oligosaccharide enzymes, holds against the community: more genes than the median MAG for both starch and plant fibre. Against its relatives it is the opposite for plant fibre (fewer GH43 genes) and no different for starch. This suggests a broad carbohydrate repertoire, which a high-carbohydrate vegetarian diet could feed; by analogy, resistant-starch studies report increases of B. adolescentis [5], but resistant starch intake was not measured here. Counting reads, as the group planned, was also highest on days 5-6 but rests on reads in all, so it supports the base share rather than replacing it. What is solid: the genome (one circular contig, complete by CheckM2), its gene content and the ten measured shares. What is uncertain: whether the genes are used, how much of the change is the diet, and whether the share of DNA follows the number of living cells.

Limitations

  • One person, ten days, one sample per day, one diet switch: the results describe this gut; they do not test a diet effect.
  • Shares are of DNA and relative: a change can come from this bacterium or from the others, and DNA from dead cells counts too.
  • Genes are predicted from sequence; names rest on cluster matches at % identity or more; presence is not activity.
  • The read share comes from a random sample of about - filtered reads per day, so it counts only a few reads of this genome; reads were assigned by their best alignment on the assembly, not by a taxonomy tool.
  • The CAZyme search used one of dbCAN's three methods (the protein models), so some families may be missed or miscounted.
  • About of every million bases come from this genome, so small differences in sequencing depth matter more than for abundant MAGs.

Open questions and possible next analyses

  • Which sugars does it take up? Match the sugar transporters to the enzymes next to them on the chromosome (gene neighbourhoods); the positions are in this report's data (on the Mac).
  • Starch users together: do other MAGs with many GH13 genes peak on the same days? Uses the shared tables of all MAGs (on the Mac).
  • Phages: do the spacers of the CRISPR arrays match phage or plasmid contigs of this study? Needs the spacer sequences and the assembly (assembly files on the Mac, or a server job).
  • Reads on all data: count every read whose best alignment lies on the B. angulatum contig in each sample's full read mapping, for a read share without the sampling noise of Table 1 (the mapping files are on the server: a server job).
  • Strains: is there one B. angulatum strain or several? Variable positions in each day's reads (a server job).
  • Food: which foods were eaten (for example dairy, bread, legumes)? Not recorded; it would have to come from the subject.
  • Activity: are the starch genes used, and more on high-carbohydrate days? Needs RNA or protein measurements, not taken in this study.
