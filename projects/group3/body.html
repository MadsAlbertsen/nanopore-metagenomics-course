Butyrate genes across the two diet periods

Butyrate is a short fatty acid that gut bacteria make from fibre and that the cells of the colon use for energy. This report follows the genes for its last steps through the ten days: which genomes carry them, how much of the DNA those genomes make up on each day, and when a change shows.

The question

The group asked whether the high-carbohydrate and the low-carbohydrate diet change the abundance of genes for butyrate production, which genomes carry those genes, who those genomes are (as far down the taxonomy as possible), and from which day a change can be seen.

As answered here: the data are DNA, so "abundance of a gene" means the share of the sample's DNA that belongs to genomes (MAGs, metagenome-assembled genomes) carrying it. That is gene potential: a gene present is not a gene used, and no butyrate was measured. With one person and ten days the report describes the size and timing of changes; it does not test them. An answer is a count of genomes with the route, their share of the DNA per day, and the day the share leaves its range from the first diet period.

Guided examples

Data used

Three tables from module 8. (1) The genes of all MAGs and their names from the Bakta annotation (module 7); one row per gene. (2) The marker-gene search on the same genes (KOfam profiles); one row per gene hit. (3) The MAG table: GTDB names down to species, quality, and each MAG's share of each sample's bases (percent of the sample's sequenced bases that map to the MAG; module 4).

Days 1-5 were high-carbohydrate and high-calorie, days 6-10 low-carbohydrate and low-calorie; both periods were vegetarian, and the days 6-10 diet was closer to the subject's normal diet. The subject had eaten a similar high-calorie diet for about a week before day 1, so days 1-5 continue that diet and the switch at day 6 is the main change (information from the data owner). Only about -% of each sample's bases fall in MAGs (mean of days 1-5 and 6-10), so the rest of the community is not seen.

Approach

Genes. The acetyl-CoA pathway is the most common butyrate pathway in stool metagenomes (on average about 80% of the butyrate pathways found in the 15 stool samples studied by Vital et al. 2014). In it, two acetyl-CoA become butyryl-CoA, the last step of which is butyryl-CoA dehydrogenase (bcd), and then a final step releases butyrate, either by a CoA-transferase that swaps the CoA onto acetate (but) or by phosphate butyryltransferase plus butyrate kinase (ptb, buk) (Vital et al. 2014). A MAG counts as having a complete route when it carries bcd plus but, ptb or buk: the rule used in module 8. bcd, ptb, buk and the AtoD/AtoA-type transferases come from KOfam; but has no clean KOfam profile and is found by its Bakta name.

Positive control. Module 8 ran the same search on selected MAGs; this report's code gives the same gene counts and the same route call ( with a route) for all of them. The script stops otherwise.

Steps. Count the genes per MAG; group the route MAGs by the set of genes they carry; follow their share per day, alone and summed; place them on a map by gene set (PCoA) coloured by family; and find the day each series changes. Expected (written down before the numbers): the known producers Roseburia, Agathobacter, Anaerostipes and Lachnospira fall on days 6-10, so the summed share of route MAGs should fall, from about day 7.

What this does and does not show

The expected fall did not show. The route MAGs held % of the sample's bases on days 1-5 and % on days 6-10 (mean per day), and no day of 6-10 lies outside the range of days 1-5. As a share of only the binned bases the totals are % and %. The known producers did fall, as expected: the Roseburia, Agathobacter, Anaerostipes and Lachnospira route MAGs (GTDB's split genera such as Roseburia_D included) together went from % to %, much as Duncan et al. (2007) saw for Roseburia and Eubacterium rectale (now Agathobacter rectalis) on a low-carbohydrate diet. But MAGs with only the kinase route rose from % to %, led by Acetatifactor intestinalis, while MAGs with the CoA-transferase fell from % to %. "Kinase route" here means ptb and/or buk without but: of these MAGs carry both ptb and buk and went from % to %; the other carry buk without ptb and held % and %.

The strongest result is this split between the two final steps; it rests on gene calls that passed the positive control. It does not show butyrate output: a kinase-route genome may make less, more or no butyrate, and gene copies do not say how active a genome is. Shares are of the DNA, not cell counts, and the bases outside the MAGs are not seen. bcd is an upper bound (the profile also fits related enzymes); only of route MAGs have the partner Etf gene next to bcd, which may reflect contig breaks as much as wrong calls. Days 6-10 also cut calories, so carbohydrate is not the only change.

Next steps and questions for tomorrow

Does the kinase-route rise mean more or less butyrate? Only a measurement of butyrate in the stool, or RNA (which genes are read), would tell; neither is in this study.

Are all bcd calls real? Look at the genes next to each bcd (Etf, crotonase, thiolase) in the MAG explorer, and check the route MAGs without an Etf neighbour.

Other routes. Butyrate can also be made from amino acids such as lysine or glutamate (Vital et al. 2014); those genes were not searched. A second gene set would show whether the total changes when they are included.
