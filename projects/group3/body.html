Butyrate genes across the two diet periods

Which genomes in this gut carry the genes to make butyrate, how abundant those genes are on the high- and the low-carbohydrate days, who the genomes are, and when a change shows. A research report on gene potential in one person over ten days, in the five parts the group asked for: introduction and background, methods, results with figures, interpretation against a published study, and next steps.

Summary

We searched all genomes assembled from ten daily stool samples (MAGs) for six butyrate genes; MAGs carry a complete route. Weighted by how abundant each MAG is, the dehydrogenase gene bcd stayed level ( and copies per 1,000 genes on days 1-5 and 6-10), the CoA-transferase gene but fell and the kinase-route genes ptb and buk rose; each was first outside its days 1-5 range on day or , but part of the kinase rise began before the switch. Together the route MAGs held % of the sample's bases on days 1-5 and % on days 6-10. The fall came mostly from Agathobacter, Roseburia and Anaerostipes, the rise from three Lachnospiraceae led by Acetatifactor intestinalis. As in Duncan et al. (2007) [5], a study of high-protein weight-loss diets, the Roseburia / E. rectale group fell while the larger groups stayed level; here the fall was smaller, and the diet contrasts differ. Whether butyrate production changed cannot be said from DNA alone. How representative the MAGs are (part 3.5): HQ MAGs represent about % of the sequenced DNA and roughly % of the detected genomes (roughly species), and the unbinned contigs do not change the direction of the gene shift.

1. Introduction and background

Why butyrate. Butyrate, acetate and propionate are the main products of fibre fermentation in the colon. Butyrate is the main energy source of the cells lining the colon [4], and these short-chain fatty acids have been linked to the suppression of inflammation and colorectal cancer [1]. How much is made depends on which bacteria are present and what they are fed, so diet can change it [3].

Who makes it, and how. In the human colon the butyrate producers are mostly Bacillota (Firmicutes), and the two most abundant groups are related to Eubacterium rectale / Roseburia and to Faecalibacterium prausnitzii [2]. Most use the acetyl-CoA pathway: two acetyl-CoA are joined and reduced to butyryl-CoA, the last reduction done by butyryl-CoA dehydrogenase with its electron-transfer flavoprotein partners (Bcd-Etf). The final step releases butyrate either through butyryl-CoA:acetate CoA-transferase (but), which passes the CoA to acetate taken up from outside, or through phosphate butyryltransferase and butyrate kinase (ptb, buk), which makes ATP [4]. In most known producers the CoA-transferase does this step [2]. In stool metagenomes the acetyl-CoA pathway made up on average % of the butyrate pathways found, and but was the more common final-step gene of that pathway (median %, buk %) [4]. Of butyrate-producing isolates from human faeces, all had CoA-transferase activity and only butyrate kinase activity, and in those the ptb and buk genes are linked [12]. The same survey found that the ability to make butyrate is not consistent within a family [4].

Diet. In obese men on high-protein weight-loss diets, cutting carbohydrate lowered faecal butyrate and the Roseburia / E. rectale group, while most other major bacterial groups did not change significantly [5]; part 4 compares our numbers with theirs. Roseburia relatives are particularly sensitive to diet [2], and cross-feeding, one species using the lactate or other products of another, also feeds butyrate formation [3].

This study. One person gave a stool sample on each of ten days. Days 1-5 were high-carbohydrate and high-calorie, days 6-10 low-carbohydrate and low-calorie; both periods were vegetarian, and the days 6-10 diet was closer to the subject's normal diet. The subject had eaten a similar high-calorie diet for about a week before day 1, so the switch at day 6 is the main change in the study (information from the data owner). Protein and fat intake were not recorded.

Question and expectations. The group asked whether the two diets change the abundance of butyrate genes, which genomes carry them and who they are, and from which day a change shows. "Abundance of genes" here is gene potential: gene copies weighted by the share of the DNA of the genomes that carry them, not gene activity and not butyrate. Expected, written in the analysis script before the numbers: (1) the known producers Roseburia, Agathobacter, Anaerostipes and Lachnospira fall on days 6-10, as in [5], so the summed share of route MAGs falls by roughly a fifth to a half, from about day 7; (2) the CoA-transferase (but) is the more common final step [2, 4].

2. Methods

Data. Our own nanopore run of the ten samples, assembled and binned with mmlong2 v1.2.1 [6] into MAGs (module 4). From module 8: each MAG's share of each sample's bases (percent of the sequenced bases of that sample that map to the MAG), GTDB names (GTDB-Tk v2.5.2 with GTDB release 226 [8]), CheckM2 quality, the genes of every MAG with their Bakta names (Bakta v1.11.4 [7]; module 7) and the marker-gene search on those genes. Only about % (days 1-5) and % (days 6-10) of each sample's bases fall in MAGs; the rest of the community is not seen. The group asked for the high-quality MAGs; we use all , because the medium-quality MAGs are part of the binned community that the abundance weighting divides by, and of the route MAGs would otherwise be dropped. On the high-quality MAGs alone the gene abundances move the same way (copies per 1,000 genes, days 1-5 to 6-10): but to , ptb to , buk to , bcd to .

Genes. Genes were predicted with Pyrodigal 3.7.1 (a Python reimplementation of Prodigal [10]) and searched with PyHMMER 0.12.3 [11] against KOfam profiles with their own score thresholds [9]: bcd (K00248), ptb (K00634), buk (K00929) and the AtoD/AtoA-type transferases (K01034, K01035). The but gene has no clean KOfam profile and was found by its Bakta name. A MAG has a complete route when it carries bcd plus but, ptb or buk (the rule of module 8). The K00248 profile covers the short-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase family, so bcd is an upper bound; as an independent check we looked for an electron-transfer flavoprotein (Etf) gene within three genes of each bcd, by Bakta name.

Gene abundance (Figure 1). For each gene and day: copies per 1,000 genes of all MAGs, each MAG weighted by its share of that day's bases (sum of share × copies divided by sum of share × genes in the MAG, times 1,000), and the share of the sample's bases in MAGs that carry at least one copy. Genes are grouped by step: bcd (the dehydrogenase step), but, atoD and atoA (CoA-transferase final step), ptb and buk (kinase final step).

Positive control and checks. Positive control: module 8 ran the same search on selected MAGs; this report's code reproduces their gene counts and route calls exactly ( with a route). Known-answer checks: all high-quality MAGs of Faecalibacterium, Agathobacter and Roseburia, genera known to use the CoA-transferase [2], carry but; and in of the MAGs with both ptb and buk the two genes lie within three genes of each other on one contig, as expected for these linked genes [12] (gene positions are not used in the gene calls). Arithmetic checks: the gene abundances of Figure 1 were recomputed for every gene and day by a plain loop over the MAGs. The script stops if any of these fails.

Further analyses. A PCoA (principal coordinates analysis) of the route MAGs on Jaccard distances of gene presence, coloured by family (Figure 3). The deepest GTDB rank named for each route MAG, species where GTDB names one (Table 2). Shares per day of the route MAGs with a mean of at least % of the bases (Figure 4), and the change of every route MAG and genus in percentage points, mean of days 6-10 minus mean of days 1-5 (Figure 5). For the timeline (Figure 6) the "break" of a series is the split of the ten days into an early and a late block with the largest log2 change between the block means; the "first day outside" is the first of days 6-10 below the lowest or above the highest of days 1-5. Per-MAG log2 changes are those of the course's shared MAG table: log2((mean of days 6-10 + ) / (mean of days 1-5 + )), means in percent of the bases. Everything is described, not tested: one person and ten days give no basis for a statistical test.

What the MAGs capture (part 3.5). Each contig's read bases per sample are its length times its mean read depth in that sample (as for the MAG shares). Genes on the unbinned contigs ( genes) were predicted with Pyrodigal in metagenome mode, because a mix of organisms gives no single genome to train on; positive control: run on the contigs of module 8's MAGs, this path finds the same butyrate KOfam genes per MAG as the report's gene calls in nearly all cases. bcd, ptb, buk, atoD and atoA were searched with the same KOfam profiles. For but and for the marker genes (rpoB and eleven ribosomal proteins), which Bakta named only in the MAGs, we built a profile HMM from the MAG proteins with that name and set its score cut-off on the MAG proteins: it recovers % of the MAG proteins named "butyryl-CoA:acetate CoA-transferase" with % extra calls, and at least % of each marker gene, with at most % of its calls on proteins that Bakta names otherwise. In part 3.5 but means this exact name: the MAG genes that the rest of the report also counts as but because Bakta calls them "acetyl-CoA hydrolase/transferase" belong to a wider protein family whose members the profile cannot tell apart by name, so they are left out here. The profile is built from Bakta's names, so it reproduces the name, not the function; whether these proteins are But is checked against characterised enzymes in part 3.6. Every gene copy counts with its contig's read depth on that day. Check: on the MAGs alone this contig-level weighting gives the same copies per 1,000 genes as Figure 1 within 10% for bcd, ptb and buk, and for but as the exact-name subset of Figure 1's genes. Marker copies count when the hit covers at least half of the marker gene, so that a gene split over two contig ends counts once.

4. Interpretation against a published study, and biological insight

The reference study. Duncan et al. (2007) [5] gave healthy obese men three diets in succession: maintenance ( g carbohydrate per day) for days, then two high-protein weight-loss diets with medium ( g per day) and low ( g per day) carbohydrate for weeks each. Stool was collected at the end of each diet, and bacterial groups were counted by FISH (16S rRNA-targeted probes) as a percent of all bacteria. Faecal butyrate fell from to to mM, and the Roseburia / E. rectale group from % to % to %. The large groups, among them clostridial cluster XIVa and the Faecalibacterium prausnitzii group, did not change significantly. Table 4 sets our numbers beside theirs.

What agrees. The pattern is the same as in [5]: the Roseburia and Agathobacter (E. rectale) genomes fall (% to % of the bases, log2 ), while Lachnospiraceae as a whole (log2 ) and Faecalibacterium (log2 ) stay close to level. One explanation is a milder or shorter change here: five days instead of several weeks, and the grams of carbohydrate were not recorded for our subject, so the doses cannot be matched. The contrasts also differ in kind: their carbohydrate cut came with more protein, ours with fewer calories (protein was not recorded), so a difference between the studies need not be one of dose or duration. It is one person against a group mean, so the agreement is a consistency, not a replication.

What [5] does not cover. The new observation is that genomes with the kinase final step rose while the CoA-transferase genomes fell, so the summed butyrate gene potential stayed level (% and % of the bases). Duncan et al. counted taxa, not genes; they saw butyrate fall disproportionately, more than the total of short-chain fatty acids, and the Roseburia / E. rectale group follow that decline closely [5]. Together with our data, one reading is that a level gene potential can still go with less butyrate, because how much a producer makes depends on its substrate, growth and partners [3, 4].

Other studies. The fall of the but-carrying Roseburia / E. rectale group under less carbohydrate is a repeated finding. In 14 overweight men given controlled diets in turn, relatives of Eubacterium rectale (now Agathobacter) decreased on a reduced-carbohydrate weight-loss diet, and rose on a resistant-starch diet; such changes appeared rapidly after each diet change [13]. In a related trial from the same group, with a design like [5] and therefore not an independent repeat, a high-protein, low-carbohydrate diet lowered the share of butyrate among the faecal short-chain fatty acids together with the Roseburia / E. rectale group [14]. In volunteers who ate a diet of animal products only for a few days, Roseburia, E. rectale and Ruminococcus bromii, which break down plant polysaccharides, decreased [15]. Our pattern therefore fits the literature, with the caveat that all three studies also raised protein or fat, which we cannot judge here. For Acetatifactor we found no human diet study. The genus was described from the caecum of a mouse fed a high-calorie diet; the type strain makes acetate and butyrate and does not use glucose [16]. In virus-infected mice on a ketogenic (very low carbohydrate, high fat) diet, Acetatifactor was among the taxa that became more abundant while Roseburia decreased [17], a pattern like ours but in another host and diet. A PubMed search (4 November 2026) for Acetatifactor with low-carbohydrate, ketogenic, calorie-restricted or fasting diets found mouse studies only, so whether human Acetatifactor responds this way is not known. In short, the decline of the but route matches published human studies; the rise of the kinase-route genomes has no human counterpart that we could find.

Biological insight. The two final steps work differently: the CoA-transferase takes up acetate from outside the cell and passes the CoA to it, while the kinase step makes ATP [4]. One hypothesis is that the conditions of the two periods favoured producers with different final steps; the data show which genomes rose, not why. A second explanation is functional redundancy: several unrelated Lachnospiraceae can fill the butyrate-producing role, and one set did better in the second period than the other. A third is that Acetatifactor and Butyrivibrio rose for reasons unrelated to butyrate, for example other substrates in the new diet, and carry the route incidentally. That family does not predict the final step (Figure 3) means that a taxonomic profile alone would not tell which final step dominates in a sample.

What is solid and what is not. The gene calls passed the positive control, and the shift shows both in summed shares and in gene copies per 1,000 genes. Both use the same gene calls and MAG shares, so they rule out an artefact of the summing but not an error in the gene calls themselves. The shift rests on few genomes (Figure 5). The low-carbohydrate days were also low-calorie and closer to the subject's normal diet, and the first period followed a week of the same high-calorie diet, so the change cannot be tied to carbohydrate alone.

  • One person, ten days, one sample per day: a description of this gut, not a test or a general result.
  • Gene potential, not activity: a gene present is not a gene used, and no butyrate was measured.
  • Shares of the DNA, not cell counts; genome size and copy number shift shares.
  • The figures of parts 3.1-3.4 see only binned bases (about -% of each sample's bases); part 3.5 shows that the HQ MAGs hold most of each butyrate gene and that the unbinned part does not change the direction of the shift, but genomes too rare to assemble are not seen at all.
  • bcd is an upper bound (the profile fits related dehydrogenases; only of calls have an Etf neighbour); but is found by name, and part 3.6 shows that the genes with the broad name are probably other CoA-transferases; ptb lies close to its KOfam threshold; only the acetyl-CoA route's last steps were searched, not its upstream genes nor the amino-acid routes (lysine, glutarate, 4-aminobutyrate [4]).
  • Carbohydrate and calories changed together; protein and fat were not recorded.

5. Next steps

  • Are the bcd calls real butyryl-CoA dehydrogenases? Part 3.6 classes every call (only a third have an Etf neighbour). Next: check the wider neighbourhood (Etf, crotonase, thiolase, hydroxybutyryl-CoA dehydrogenase) of each bcd in the MAG explorer, starting with the route MAGs without an Etf neighbour. Data: on the Mac (genes and Bakta names, module 8).
  • The whole acetyl-CoA pathway. Search the upstream genes (thiolase, hydroxybutyryl-CoA dehydrogenase, crotonase) and require them all, as Vital et al. did [4]; Figure 1 would then cover the whole pathway. Data: on the Mac, a few more KOfam profiles in the same search.
  • Amino-acid routes. Search the lysine and glutarate pathways [4]; a lower-carbohydrate diet might favour them. Data: on the Mac, new KOfam profiles.
  • Why did Acetatifactor and Butyrivibrio rise? Compare their carbohydrate-active enzymes with those of the falling Agathobacter and Roseburia (CAZyme tables, module 8) to see whether the new diet offered them other substrates. Data: on the Mac.
  • Below species. Variants within the main route MAGs, day by day, could show whether one strain replaced another rather than a species changing in share. Data: reads of each day mapped to the MAGs and variants called, a server job.
  • Is the potential used? Faecal butyrate or RNA (which genes are read) on the same days would show it, and would allow a direct comparison with the butyrate numbers of [5]. Not measured in this study.
  • The part that did not assemble. Part 3.5 covers the unbinned contigs. Marker genes or butyrate genes counted directly in the reads (for example with SingleM or a protein search of a read subsample) would also cover organisms too rare to assemble. Data: the reads, a server job.
