Butyrate genes across the two diet periods

Which genomes in this gut carry the genes to make butyrate, how much of the DNA they make up on the high- and the low-carbohydrate days, and when a change shows. A research report on gene potential in one person over ten days.

Summary

Butyrate is a short-chain fatty acid that gut bacteria make by fermenting fibre; the cells lining the colon use it as their main energy source. We asked whether the genomes able to make butyrate change in abundance when the diet switches from high to low carbohydrate. In all genomes assembled from the ten daily stool samples (MAGs, metagenome-assembled genomes) we searched for butyryl-CoA dehydrogenase (bcd) and the enzymes of the final step: the CoA-transferase (but), or the phosphate butyryltransferase and butyrate kinase pair (ptb, buk).

MAGs carry a complete route. Together they made up % of the sample's bases on days 1-5 and % on days 6-10: the total did not fall, against our expectation. Underneath the level total the two final steps moved apart. MAGs with the CoA-transferase fell from % to %, and that fall came from the known butyrate producers Agathobacter, Roseburia and Anaerostipes. MAGs with ptb and buk rose from % to %, carried by three Lachnospiraceae genomes led by Acetatifactor intestinalis. Both moves show from day 7. One reading is that the community's butyrate potential was held by a different set of genomes on the low-carbohydrate days; whether butyrate production stayed level cannot be said from DNA alone.

Background

Why butyrate. Butyrate, acetate and propionate are the main products of fibre fermentation in the colon. Butyrate is the main energy source of the cells lining the colon [4], and these short-chain fatty acids have been linked to the suppression of inflammation and colorectal cancer [1]. How much is made depends on which bacteria are present and what they are fed, so diet can change it [3].

Who makes it, and how. In the human colon the butyrate producers are mostly Bacillota (Firmicutes), and the two most abundant groups are related to Eubacterium rectale / Roseburia and to Faecalibacterium prausnitzii [2]. Most use the acetyl-CoA pathway: two acetyl-CoA are joined and reduced to butyryl-CoA, the last reduction done by butyryl-CoA dehydrogenase with its electron-transfer flavoprotein partners (Bcd-Etf). The final step releases butyrate either through butyryl-CoA:acetate CoA-transferase (but), which passes the CoA to acetate taken up from outside, or through phosphate butyryltransferase and butyrate kinase (ptb, buk), which makes ATP [4]. In most known producers the CoA-transferase rather than the kinase does this step [2]. In 15 stool metagenomes the acetyl-CoA pathway made up on average about 80% of the butyrate pathways found, and but was the more common final-step gene (median 77%, buk 22%) [4]. The same survey found that the ability to make butyrate is not consistent within a family, and that a pathway found in a genome does not prove that it is used [4].

Diet. In obese volunteers, cutting carbohydrate from 399 to 164 and then 24 g per day lowered faecal butyrate, the Roseburia / E. rectale group and the bifidobacteria (P = 0.026), while most other major bacterial groups did not change significantly [5]; Roseburia relatives are particularly sensitive to diet [2]. Cross-feeding, one species using the lactate or other products of another, also feeds butyrate formation [3].

This study. One person gave a stool sample on each of ten days. Days 1-5 were high-carbohydrate and high-calorie, days 6-10 low-carbohydrate and low-calorie; both periods were vegetarian, and the days 6-10 diet was closer to the subject's normal diet. The subject had eaten a similar high-calorie diet for about a week before day 1, so days 1-5 continue that diet and the switch at day 6 is the main change in the study (information from the data owner). Protein and fat intake were not recorded.

Question and hypotheses

The group asked whether the high- and low-carbohydrate diets change the abundance of genes for butyrate production, which genomes carry those genes and who they are, and from which day a change can be seen. We answer it as: do the MAGs that carry a complete butyrate route change their share of the DNA between days 1-5 and days 6-10, which taxa are they (GTDB names, to species where given), do MAGs with the same genes belong to the same family, and on which day does each series leave the range of the first period? "Abundance of genes" is gene potential: the share of the DNA in genomes that carry them, not gene activity and not butyrate.

Expected (written in the analysis script before the numbers): (1) the known producers Roseburia, Agathobacter, Anaerostipes and Lachnospira fall on days 6-10, as earlier in this course and in [5], so the summed share of route MAGs falls by roughly a fifth to a half, from about day 7; (2) the CoA-transferase (but) is the more common final step [2, 4].

Data and methods

Data. Our own nanopore run of the ten samples, assembled and binned with mmlong2 v1.2.1 [6] into MAGs (module 4). From module 8: each MAG's share of each sample's bases (percent of the sequenced bases of that sample that map to the MAG), GTDB names (GTDB-Tk v2.5.2 with GTDB release 226 [8]), CheckM2 quality, the genes of every MAG with their Bakta names (Bakta v1.11.4 [7]; module 7) and the marker-gene search on those genes. Only about % (days 1-5) and % (days 6-10) of each sample's bases fall in MAGs; the rest of the community is not seen.

Genes. Genes were predicted with Pyrodigal 3.7.1 (a Python reimplementation of Prodigal [10]) and searched with PyHMMER 0.12.3 [11] against KOfam profiles with their own score thresholds [9]: bcd (K00248), ptb (K00634), buk (K00929) and the AtoD/AtoA-type transferases (K01034, K01035). The but gene has no clean KOfam profile and was found by its Bakta name. A MAG has a complete route when it carries bcd plus but, ptb or buk (the rule of module 8). The K00248 profile covers the short-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase family, so bcd is an upper bound; as an independent check we looked for an electron-transfer flavoprotein (Etf) gene within three genes of each bcd, by Bakta name.

Positive control. Module 8 ran the same search on selected MAGs. The code of this report reproduces their gene counts and route calls exactly ( with a route); the script stops otherwise.

Analyses. (1) Gene counts per MAG and gene sets of the route MAGs. (2) Shares per day of the route MAGs with a mean of at least % of the bases. (3) A PCoA of the route MAGs on Jaccard distances of gene presence, coloured by family. (4) Community series per day: summed shares of the route MAGs and of each final-step group, and gene copies per 1,000 genes of all MAGs with each MAG weighted by its share that day. For each series the "break" is the split of the ten days into an early and a late block with the largest log2 change between the block means; "first day outside" is the first of days 6-10 below the lowest or above the highest of days 1-5. (5) The change of every route MAG and genus in percentage points (mean of days 6-10 minus mean of days 1-5). Per-MAG log2 changes are those of the course's shared MAG table: log2((mean of days 6-10 + ) / (mean of days 1-5 + )), means in percent of the bases. Everything is described, not tested: one person and ten days give no basis for a statistical test.

Discussion

The first hypothesis, that the genomes able to make butyrate lose ground on the low-carbohydrate days, is not supported in this gut: their summed share is level (% vs % of the bases; % vs % of the binned bases). The part of it that came from the literature does hold: the Roseburia / Agathobacter group falls, as Duncan et al. found with less carbohydrate [5] and as the reviews describe for this diet-sensitive group [2]; the third known producer, Anaerostipes, fell as well. The second hypothesis, that the CoA-transferase is the more common final step, holds as well [2, 4].

The new observation is the opposite move of the two final steps, which keeps the total level. It rests on gene calls that passed the positive control, and it shows both in summed shares and in gene copies per 1,000 genes. Both use the same gene calls and MAG shares, so this rules out an artefact of how the shares are summed, but not an error in the gene calls themselves. Figure 5 shows it is carried by few genomes: three Lachnospiraceae rise and a handful of Agathobacter, Roseburia and Anaerostipes genomes fall. That makes the result depend on how those few genomes behave, and it reads better as "different butyrate-capable taxa were abundant in the two periods" than as "the community switched route".

What this means for butyrate itself is open. The kinase step gives the cell ATP, while the CoA-transferase uses acetate from outside the cell [4]; how much butyrate a genome makes also depends on substrate, growth and partners [3, 4]. Duncan et al. saw faecal butyrate fall with carbohydrate while most bacterial groups stayed stable [5], so a level gene potential could still go with less butyrate. The low-carbohydrate days were also low-calorie and closer to the subject's normal diet, and the first period followed a week of the same high-calorie diet, so the change cannot be tied to carbohydrate alone.

Limitations

  • One person, ten days, one sample per day: a description of this gut, not a test or a general result.
  • Gene potential, not activity: a gene present is not a gene used, and no butyrate was measured.
  • Shares of the DNA, not cell counts; genome size and copy number shift shares.
  • Only binned bases are seen (about -% of each sample's bases).
  • bcd is an upper bound (the profile fits related dehydrogenases); but is found by name only; only the acetyl-CoA route's last steps were searched, not its upstream genes nor the amino-acid routes (lysine, glutarate, 4-aminobutyrate [4]).
  • Carbohydrate and calories changed together; protein and fat were not recorded.

Open questions and possible next analyses

  • Are the bcd calls real butyryl-CoA dehydrogenases? Check the neighbourhood (Etf, crotonase, thiolase, hydroxybutyryl-CoA dehydrogenase) of each bcd in the MAG explorer, starting with the route MAGs without an Etf neighbour. Data: on the Mac (genes and Bakta names, module 8).
  • The whole acetyl-CoA pathway. Search the upstream genes (thiolase, hydroxybutyryl-CoA dehydrogenase, crotonase) and require them all, as Vital et al. did [4]. Data: on the Mac, a few more KOfam profiles in the same search.
  • Amino-acid routes. Search the lysine and glutarate pathways [4]; a lower-carbohydrate diet might favour them. Data: on the Mac, new KOfam profiles.
  • Why did Acetatifactor and Butyrivibrio rise? Compare their carbohydrate-active enzymes with those of the falling Agathobacter and Roseburia (CAZyme tables, module 8) to see whether the new diet offered them other substrates. Data: on the Mac.
  • Is the potential used? Faecal butyrate or RNA (which genes are read) on the same days would show it. Not measured in this study.
  • The unbinned part. Search the unbinned contigs or the reads for but and buk to cover the community outside the MAGs. Data: unbinned contigs on the Mac; the reads need a server job.
