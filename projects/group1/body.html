Group 1 · report

How fast does the gut community follow a diet switch?

One person, ten daily stool samples. Days 1-5: high-carbohydrate, high-calorie diet; days 6-10: low-carbohydrate, low-calorie diet. The group asked for a short report that tests their two hypotheses, with per-day community profiles, MAG heat maps and strain-level results; the results below are organised by hypothesis, each with a verdict. This is one person: the report describes and does not generalise.

Summary

Question. Does the community follow the diet switch at day 6 with a delay (H1), and do the genomes present on every day carry more strain diversity during the transition (H2)? We profiled each day by family and by MAG (percent of the sample's bases), compared days with the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity, followed the MAGs that changed at least two-fold, and used the variable positions in each MAG for strain diversity.

H1, delay: partly supported. Day , the first day of the new diet, still resembled days 1-5 (Bray-Curtis to day 1: ); the community moved over days - ( on day ). But of the changing MAGs reached their new level on day already, and a few taxa were still changing on the last day. H2: not testable with these data: the variant file holds the reads of all days pooled, so there is no per-day strain diversity; pooled, no core MAG looks like a strain mixture. Per-day strain profiling is a server job, described under next steps.

Background

The gut community can follow a change of diet quickly. In a controlled feeding study of ten people, the community changed detectably within 24 hours of a new diet, while the broad community type stayed the same over the 10-day study [1]. When people ate only animal or only plant products for a few days, the community changed within days; the animal-based diet raised Bacteroides and lowered Firmicutes that break down plant polysaccharides, such as Roseburia and Eubacterium rectale [2]. Daily sampling of 34 people showed that the community depends on several days of diet history, and that responses differ between people [3].

A stool sample is not a snapshot of one day's food. Gut transit time varies between people and is itself linked to the community and its metabolism [4, 5], so a delay between a diet switch and a change in stool can come from transit, from the time the community needs to change, or both. Within one person a species can be present as several strains; Prevotella copri, for example, forms a complex of four distinct clades [6]. Strain mixtures show up as variable positions, where the reads of one genome carry two different bases.

Study design and diet (information from the data owner). One person gave one stool sample per day for ten days. Days 1-5 were high-carbohydrate and high-calorie, days 6-10 low-carbohydrate and low-calorie; both periods were vegetarian. The subject had eaten a similar high-calorie diet for about a week before day 1, and the days 6-10 diet was closer to the subject's normal diet. The group's description had the diet changing at day 1 as well; with the data owner's information there is no switch at day 1, and the switch at day 6 is the one change in the study. This report uses the data owner's information.

Hypotheses and how they are tested

  • H1, delayed response. The group expected a delay of about one day (transit) and about five days for a new community to establish. Test: on which day after the switch at day 6 do the community and each changing MAG move to their new level, and is the community still moving on days 9-10? Expected (written before the analysis): days 1-6 alike, a change from day 7, days 8-10 most different.
  • Dropped: day 1 and day 10 most similar. The group first also expected day 1 and day 10 to be the most similar pair (both on the usual diet). Day 1 followed about a week of a similar high-carbohydrate, high-calorie diet (information from the data owner), so it does not represent the usual diet, and the group dropped this part to focus on the switch at day 6.
  • H2, more strain diversity during the transition in the core MAGs; the group expected popANI to be highest before a new community is established. popANI compares two samples (100% = no fixed differences between their populations), so we restate the prediction as two measures inStrain gives: per-day nucleotide diversity of each core MAG highest on the days around the switch, and popANI between consecutive days. Test: per-day values if the variant data have one column per day; otherwise only the pooled variable positions per MAG. Expected: pooled data only, and variable positions mostly below 1% of a MAG's positions.

Data and methods

Assembly, MAGs, taxonomy. All ten samples were assembled together and binned with mmlong2 [7] (myloasm , minimap2 , CoverM , GTDB-Tk ); modules 3-5 explain these steps. The MAGs are mmlong2's own bins.

Share of a MAG on each day (Figures 1, 2 and 5). Four steps, all inside mmlong2 [7] except the last: (1) each day's reads, after the quality filter and adapter trimming, are mapped with minimap2 to the contigs of the co-assembly (primary alignments only); (2) for each contig and day, the mean depth (the number of reads covering an average position of the contig, leaving out about 75 bp at each contig end) is computed with MetaBAT2's jgi_summarize_bam_contig_depths, counting only alignments of at least 95% identity; (3) depth times contig length gives about the bases of that day's reads aligned to the contig, and the sum over a MAG's contigs the bases on the MAG; (4) these bases divided by all bases sequenced on that day, times 100, give the MAG's share in percent of the sample's bases (card "MAG abundance on each day" in module 8). Bases are used rather than read counts because the reads differ in length. The MAGs hold -% of each sample's bases; the rest is outside MAGs and left out. Family and phylum shares are sums over MAGs. A share of the DNA is not a cell count.

Comparing days. Bray-Curtis dissimilarity on the MAG shares (0 = same composition, 1 = nothing shared), each day against every other day, against day 1, against the mean of days 2-5 and of days 8-10, and against the day before. Changing MAGs (Figure 5): for each MAG, the mean share over days 1-5 and over days 6-10; the change is log2 of the ratio of the two means, with % added to both so that a MAG absent in one period gives a finite value (1 = doubled, -1 = halved). A MAG counts as changing if its mean share over the ten days is at least % and it changes at least two-fold (log2 at least 1 up or down). The midpoint is halfway between the two period means; the settled day is the first day from which the MAG's share stays on the new side of its midpoint up to day 10.

Worked example. is of Mbp. On day 1 its mean depth was x, so day 1's reads put Mbp on it; day 1 had Gbp of reads, so its share was %. Its mean share was % over days 1-5 and % over days 6-10, a log2 change of (a -fold fall), so it counts as changing (Figure 5A). Its midpoint is %; its share is above it up to day and below it from day to day 10, so its settled day is (choose it in Figure 5B; Figure 5C counts it on that day).

Why a two-fold threshold. There is no standard threshold for calling a taxon "changed". A two-fold change (log2 = 1, the same distance up and down on a log scale) is a simple cut-off that was the usual one in early gene expression studies, where two-fold was typically considered a worthwhile change; but a fold-change cut-off does not take the variability between replicates (here, between days) into account, and studies that combined a statistical test with a fold change used cut-offs of 1.3-, 1.5-, 2- or 4-fold [10]. Microbiome studies mostly rely on statistical tests of differential abundance, and 14 such methods found very different sets of taxa in the same 38 data sets [11]; the tests treat the samples of a group as independent replicates, which neighbouring days of one person are not. Shares are also compositional: one MAG's share can change because other MAGs change [12], and one person's gut community varies from day to day without any intervention [13]. We therefore use the two-fold rule as a description, not a test, and show how much the list depends on it: MAGs change at least 1.5-fold, at least two-fold, at least three-fold and at least four-fold (each with a mean share of at least %, which keeps out rare MAGs whose shares are noisy). Interpretation: the number of "changing" MAGs depends strongly on the threshold, so it should be read as a ranking of the largest changes rather than as a count of genomes that responded to the diet.

Statistics and robustness (Table 1). Whole community: PERMANOVA pseudo-F [19] for days 1-5 vs days 6-10 on the Bray-Curtis distances, with an exact permutation test over all ways to assign five of the ten days to the first group (each division counted twice, once per group label); R2 is the share of the squared distances explained by the split. Robustness: the changing rule and the settled day recomputed ten times, each time without one day (period means from the remaining days); and, per changing MAG, the largest log2 change between consecutive days within days 1-5 as a measure of day-to-day noise. No other tests are made; numbers elsewhere describe this one person and are not tested. A check with Euclidean distances on simulated data, where pseudo-F must equal the ordinary analysis-of-variance F, passed.

Carbohydrate-active enzymes (Figure 6). Genes of all MAGs were predicted with Pyrodigal 3.7.1 [16] and searched against the dbCAN HMMs with PyHMMER 0.12.3 [17] (only the HMM step of dbCAN, without its consensus of three methods) (module 7). Degrading families (glycoside hydrolases, polysaccharide lyases, carbohydrate esterases) are grouped by their main substrate following dbCAN3 [18] and module 7. Per MAG: degrading CAZyme genes per 1,000 predicted genes, so that the value does not grow with genome size or completeness. Groups are compared by their medians; the numbers describe the MAGs and are not tested.

Strain diversity. Variable positions per MAG: the percent of its positions where Longshot [8] called a single-base variant (at least 6 reads, the other base in at least 3 reads and 10% of them), from the reads of all days pooled. The variant file ( variant records) has sample column, so it gives one value per MAG, not per day. Core MAGs are at % of the bases or more on all ten days; % variable positions or more would mark a likely strain mixture (rule set before running).

Checks. Bray-Curtis values reproduce the course's overview analysis for all pairs; the changing MAGs ( down, up) match the earlier analysis; variable positions recomputed from the contig table match the MAG table; family shares add up to the binned total of each day and to the phylum table; every heat-map cell equals the MAG table. An independent reviewer recomputed the numbers of the first version of this report, and the family shares, heat maps and distances of this version.

How robust are these results?

The group asked for statistics behind the results. With one person and ten consecutive days, the days are not independent replicates and the diet switch is also the passage of time, so we use one permutation test on the whole community and otherwise check how much the results depend on single days (methods under Data and methods).

Whole community. Splitting the ten days into days 1-5 and days 6-10 explains % of the variation in the Bray-Curtis distances (pseudo-F ). Of all ways to divide ten days into two groups of five, this split ranks (permutation p = ; the smallest possible value is ). The best-separating division puts day 6 with days 1-3 and 5, and day 4 with days 7-10, which fits the one-day lag (Figure 4) and the large step from day 4 to day 5. Interpretation: the days before and after the switch form two different groups, but the p-value treats the days as independent and cannot separate the diet from time, so it supports "the community changed between the periods" and not "the diet changed it".

Changing MAGs, one day left out at a time. Repeating the two-fold rule ten times, each time without one day, keeps of the changing MAGs in all ten reruns and in at least nine; the least stable ones pass in of ten. other MAGs pass the rule in at least one rerun, because many MAGs lie just below two-fold (see the threshold counts under Data and methods). Settled day: MAGs keep their settled day in every rerun, move by one day and by two days or more (Table 1). Day-to-day noise: for of the MAGs, the largest change between two consecutive days within days 1-5 (no diet change) is at least as large as their change between the periods; the median largest step is and the median period change (both log2). Interpretation: most of the list and most settled days do not hang on a single day, but the MAGs close to two-fold and those with a large step before the switch are weak cases; the exact settled day of a single MAG is uncertain by about a day, and by more for the MAGs whose range in Table 1 spans several days.

Hypothesis 2: more strain diversity during the transition

What the existing variant data can and cannot show

H2 needs strain diversity for each day (for example popANI between days, or nucleotide diversity per day), which requires each day's reads mapped on their own. The variant file from mmlong2 has sample column: Longshot was run once on the reads of all ten days pooled. It can show which MAGs carry strains that coexist over the ten days (or replace each other, since a strain swap in pooled reads also gives variable positions above the 10% filter); it cannot show whether diversity rises or falls around the switch. We therefore show the pooled result and give the per-day analysis as the next step.

Discussion

H1. The community changes after the switch with a short delay: unchanged on day , moving on days -. A lag of about a day fits transit time [4, 5] and the feeding studies where the community changed within one to a few days [1, 2]; a further day fits a community that depends on several days of diet history [3]. The five-day establishment the group expected is not clearly seen for the community as a whole, but some taxa still grow or shrink on the last day, and the series ends there.

H2. The question is good but needs per-day data. The pooled result is the least certain one in the report, because method limits (read errors, the 10% filter, a caller made for diploid genomes) and biology are mixed.

Solid and uncertain. Solid: the two blocks of days in Figure 3, which rest on all MAGs together, and the stable family profile. Uncertain: the exact settled day of single MAGs, which one odd day can shift, and anything about strains. Carbohydrate and calories changed together, so no cause can be named. The CAZyme comparison (Figure 6) is solid as a description but shows that gene content follows family: it does not explain which MAGs changed.

Limitations

  • One person, one switch, one sample per day: the timing is described, not tested; responses differ between people [3].
  • With one person and no control series, change over time that has nothing to do with the diet cannot be told apart from a diet effect; the largest day-to-day step (day , ) comes before the switch.
  • Shares are of DNA, not cells, and only the binned part of each sample (-% of the bases).
  • No sample before day 1 or after day 10, so neither the usual community nor the end of the change is observed.
  • Variable positions come from pooled reads; strains below 10% of a MAG's reads are not counted.

Next steps

  • Per-day strain diversity (H2), a server job of some hours (script written and tested; waiting for the server run). Each day's trimmed reads are mapped on their own to the co-assembly (minimap2, the same long-read setting as mmlong2) and the MAGs are profiled with inStrain [9]; inStrain's compare then gives popANI between every pair of days for each MAG, the group's own measure, and profile gives nucleotide diversity per MAG and day. inStrain was written for short reads; its manual's section on long reads recommends, for Nanopore reads, a minimum base quality that matches the reads (here Q20, which most of our bases reach, instead of the default Q30), keeping unpaired reads, skipping the per-mismatch-level profiling and a lower read identity cut-off. A simulated test with a known answer (a second strain that differs from the first at a known set of positions, reads with random errors) found nearly all planted differences as variable positions in a mixture of the two strains and none in the pure strains, and gave the expected popANI between the two pure strains. The test also shows that nucleotide diversity has a floor set by read errors, so the days are compared with each other rather than read as absolute values. Job script: scripts/51_instrain_days.sbatch in the analysis repository.
  • The end of the change. Do the rising families level off after day 10? Needs samples after day 10 (not measured).
  • The P. copri_B pair. One genome or two strains? Compare the two MAGs' sequences and genes (on the Mac, from module 8).
  • Fast and slow genomes. Figure 6 shows that CAZyme content follows family rather than change; whether the MAGs that settle on day 6 differ from the slower ones in other functions (for example the marker genes of module 8) is open. On the Mac.
  • Transit time. A marker such as the blue-dye method [5] would separate gut transit from community change (not measured).
