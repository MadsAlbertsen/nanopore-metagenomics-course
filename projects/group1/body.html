How fast does the gut community follow a diet switch?

One person, ten daily stool samples. Days 1-5: high-carbohydrate, high-calorie diet; days 6-10: low-carbohydrate, low-calorie diet. This report describes what happened in this one person; it does not test or generalise.

Summary

We asked when the gut community changed after the switch from a high-carbohydrate, high-calorie diet to a low-carbohydrate, low-calorie diet on day 6, which genomes changed, and whether the genomes present on every day look like mixtures of strains. We compared the genome (MAG) composition of every pair of days with the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity, followed the MAGs that changed at least two-fold, and looked at the share of variable positions in each MAG.

Day 6 still resembled days 1-5, day 7 lay between the two states, and from day 8 on the samples were clearly different: the distance to day 1 stayed at or below up to day 7 and was on day 8. Single MAGs were faster: of reached their new level on day 6 itself. Most falling MAGs were Lachnospiraceae ( of ), and many rising ones Bacteroidaceae ( of ), a direction that matches published short-term diet studies. Day 1 and day 10 were among the most different pairs (rank of ). None of the ever-present MAGs stood out as a strain mixture.

Interpretation: the pattern suggests a response within one to three days, with some genomes answering faster than the community as a whole. With one person and one switch this is a description, not a tested result.

Background

The gut community can follow a change of diet quickly. In a controlled feeding study of ten people, the community changed detectably within 24 hours of starting a high-fat/low-fiber or low-fat/high-fiber diet, while the broad community type (dominated by Bacteroides or by Prevotella) stayed the same during the 10-day study [1]. When people ate only animal or only plant products for a few days, the community changed rapidly; the animal-based diet raised Bacteroides and lowered Firmicutes that break down plant polysaccharides, such as Roseburia and Eubacterium rectale [2]. Daily sampling of 34 people over 17 days showed that the community depended on several days of diet history, and that responses differed between people [3].

A stool sample is not a snapshot of one day's food. How long food takes through the gut varies between people and is itself linked to the composition and metabolism of the community [4, 5]. So a delay between a diet switch and a change in stool could come from transit, from the time the community needs to change, or both.

Within one person, a species is often a population of closely related strains. Over months, resident strains are rarely replaced by distant ones; more often they gain a few changes [6]. Some species are known to be genetically diverse: Prevotella copri, for example, forms a complex of four clades that differ by more than 10% [7]. Variable positions in a MAG, where the reads show two bases, are one way to see such mixtures.

Study design: one person gave one stool sample per day for ten days. Days 1-5 were high-carbohydrate and high-calorie, days 6-10 low-carbohydrate and low-calorie. Information from the data owner: both diet periods were vegetarian, the subject had eaten a similar high-calorie diet for about a week before day 1, and the days 6-10 diet was closer to the subject's normal diet. So the diet did not change at day 1; the switch at day 6 is the main change in the study.

Question and hypotheses

The group asked whether the community answers a diet change straight away or with a delay, whether day 1 and day 10 are the most alike pair of days, and how much strain diversity the abundant genomes carry. This report answers three narrower questions that the data can address.

  • Timing. On which day after the switch at day 6 does the community, and each genome that changes most, move to its new level? Expected: days 1-6 alike, a change from day 7, days 8-10 most different, a delay of one to two days.
  • Day 1 vs day 10. Expected: not the most similar pair, since days 8-10 differ most from days 1-5 and day 1 already follows a week of the high-calorie diet.
  • Strain mixtures. Which genomes present on every day carry many variable positions? Expected: mostly below 1% of positions, a few higher in abundant genomes, and more variants found in deeper genomes.

Strain diversity on each day, and whether it changes around the switch, needs each day's reads mapped on their own; that is out of reach here and listed under open questions.

Data and methods

Data. The MAG abundance on each day (module 4; card "MAG abundance on each day" in module 8): one value per MAG and day, the percent of the sample's bases that map to the MAG. The MAGs hold -% of each sample's bases; the rest is in contigs outside MAGs and is left out. A share of the DNA is not a cell count: genome size and copy number shift it.

Assembly and MAGs. All ten samples were assembled together and binned with mmlong2 [8] (assembler myloasm , read mapping minimap2 , abundance CoverM , taxonomy GTDB-Tk ); modules 3-5 explain these steps.

Variable positions (module 5, "microdiversity"; card "Sequence variants within MAGs" in module 8): the share of a MAG's positions where Longshot [9] called a single-base variant (at least 6 reads, the other base in at least 3 reads and 10% of them). Longshot was made for diploid genomes; with these filters a strain at less than 10% of a MAG's reads is not counted. The reads of all ten days are pooled: one value per MAG, not per day. Depth is the mean number of pooled reads per position (x).

Community differences. Bray-Curtis dissimilarity on the MAG shares, each sample first scaled to sum to one (0 = same composition, 1 = no MAG in common). Each day was compared with every other day, with day 1, with the mean composition of days 2-5 and of days 8-10 (the end state), and with the day before.

Changing MAGs. MAGs with a mean share of at least % and a log2 change of at least 1 (up) or at most -1 (down) in the MAG table. That table adds % to both period means before the log2, so MAGs near zero do not give infinite changes; on the raw means the rule would give MAGs. For each, the midpoint between its two period means splits old from new level; the settled day is the first day from which the MAG stays on the new side up to day 10.

Core MAGs are present at % of the bases or more on all ten days. A core MAG with variable positions at % of its positions or more would count as a likely strain mixture (a rule written in the analysis script before it was run).

Checks (positive controls). The Bray-Curtis values reproduce the course's overview analysis for all pairs; the mean within days 1-5 (), within days 6-10 () and between periods () match the known values; the changing MAGs ( down, up) match the earlier analysis; variable positions recomputed from the contig table match the MAG table; phylum shares match the overview analysis. Discussion

The timing hypothesis holds in part. As expected, days 1-6 are alike and days 8-10 differ most, and the community as a whole moves over days 7-8. But many single genomes already reached their new level on day 6, the first day of the new diet, and the step into the new diet is not larger than ordinary day-to-day steps. Interpretation: a response within about a day for the fastest genomes fits the feeding studies, where the community changed within 24 hours or a few days [1, 2]. The slower movement of the whole community may reflect transit time [4, 5], large genomes that change late, or a community that depends on several days of diet history [3].

The direction of change, fewer Lachnospiraceae and more Bacteroidaceae on the low-carbohydrate diet, resembles what short-term studies report when less plant polysaccharide reaches the gut [2]. Here both diets were vegetarian, and carbohydrate and calories changed at once, so the cause cannot be narrowed down. The second hypothesis, that day 1 and day 10 are not the most alike, holds (rank of ).

The strain hypothesis does not hold as stated: no core MAG has many variable positions, and depth goes with fewer, not more. This is the least certain result, because pooled variant calls mix method limits with biology. Strain change within a person over months is usually small [6], so few variable positions over ten days is plausible, but per-day data would be needed to say more.

What is solid: the block structure of Figure 1, which rests on all MAGs at once, and the families that change. What is uncertain: the exact settled day of single MAGs, which can move with one unusual day, and anything about strains.

Limitations

  • One person, one switch, one sample per day: the timing is described, not tested; responses differ between people [3].
  • Shares are of DNA, not cells, and only the binned part of each sample (-% of the bases) is used.
  • Carbohydrate and calorie intake changed together; the diet before day 1 is known only from the data owner, and there is no sample from before the study.
  • Settled days use period means that include the days being judged.
  • Variable positions come from pooled reads and a caller made for diploid genomes; strains below 10% of a MAG's reads are not counted.

Open questions and possible next analyses

  • Strain diversity per day. Does strain diversity change around the switch? Map each day's reads on their own to the MAGs and run a per-sample strain tool (for example inStrain, which also compares days by population ANI). A server job of some hours.
  • The P. copri_B pair. One genome or two strains? Compare the two MAGs' sequences and genes (on the Mac, from the MAG sequences in module 8), and check reads that span both (read mapping on the server).
  • Fast and slow genomes. Do the MAGs that settle on day 6 share something, for example carbohydrate-active enzymes (module 7), that the slower ones lack? On the Mac, from the gene tables in module 8.
  • Day 4 to day 5. Is the largest step sampling noise? Two samples from the same stool, or several from the same day, would show how large it is (not measured).
  • Transit time. A transit-time marker such as the blue-dye method [5] in a new study would separate gut transit from community change (not measured).
  • The usual diet. Days 6-10 were closer to the normal diet; samples from before the study or after day 10 would show whether day 10 is near the subject's usual community (not measured).
