Group 1 · draft report

Does the gut community answer the diet switch with a delay?

One person, ten daily stool samples. Days 1-5: high-carbohydrate, high-calorie diet; days 6-10: low-carbohydrate, low-calorie diet. This page describes what happened in this one person; it does not test or generalise.

The question

The group asked whether the gut community answers a change of diet straight away or with a delay, whether day 1 and day 10 are the most alike pair of days, and how much strain diversity the abundant MAGs carry.

Information from the data owner changes how day 1 should be read: both diet periods were vegetarian, the subject had eaten a similar high-calorie diet for about a week before day 1, and the days 6-10 diet was closer to the subject's normal diet. So the diet did not change at day 1, and no switch is expected there; the main change in the study is the switch at day 6.

This report therefore asks three narrower questions. (1) How different is every pair of days, and where does the day 1 vs day 10 pair rank? (2) On which day after the switch at day 6 does the community, and each MAG that changes most, move to its new level? (3) Which MAGs present on every day carry many variable positions, a sign of a strain mixture? Strain diversity per day is out of reach here (see the last section).

Guided examples

Data used

The MAG abundance on each day (module 4; card "MAG abundance on each day" in module 8): one row per MAG and day, as the percent of the sample's bases that map to the MAG. The MAGs together hold -% of each sample's bases; the rest is in contigs outside MAGs and is left out. A share of the DNA is not a cell count.

Variable positions per MAG (module 5, "microdiversity"; card "Sequence variants within MAGs" in module 8): the share of a MAG's positions where the reads show a second base (at least 6 reads, the other base in at least 3 reads and 10% of them). The reads of all ten days are pooled, so this is one value per MAG, not one per day. Depth is the mean number of pooled reads covering a position (x).

The diet periods come from the sample sheet; the diet before day 1 from information from the data owner.

Approach

Differences between days use the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity on the MAG shares: 0 means the same composition, 1 means no MAG in common. Each sample is first scaled so its MAG shares add up to one. For the timing, each day is compared with day 1, with the mean composition of days 2-5 and with the mean of days 8-10 (the end state), and each day with the day before.

For single MAGs, we took the MAGs with a mean share of at least % and at least a two-fold change between the two period means. For each, the midpoint between its two period means splits "old level" from "new level"; the settled day is the first day from which the MAG stays on the new side up to day 10.

Core MAGs are the MAGs at % of the bases or more on all ten days. Before the analysis we set a rule: a core MAG with variable positions at % of its positions or more is a likely strain mixture. We expected the change one to two days after the switch, day 1 vs day 10 not to be the most similar pair, and more variants in deeper MAGs.

Checks that the method works (positive controls): the Bray-Curtis values reproduce the table of the course's overview analysis for all pairs; the mean within days 1-5 (), within days 6-10 () and between the periods () are the known values; the count of changing MAGs ( down, up) matches the earlier analysis; variable positions recomputed from the contig table match the MAG table.

What this does and does not show

In this person, the community did not change on the day of the switch. Day 6 still looks like days 1-5, day 7 lies between the two states, and from day 8 on the samples are clearly different (Figures 1 and 2). Most changing MAGs reached their new level on day 6 to 8 (Figure 3). This fits a delay of one to two days, but it is one switch in one person: the timing is described, not tested. A delay could come from the time food takes through the gut, from the time the community takes to change, or both; these data cannot tell them apart.

Day 1 and day 10 are among the most different pairs. Given that the subject had eaten a similar high-calorie diet for about a week before day 1, day 1 is not a sample of the normal diet, so there was no reason to expect it to match day 10; the data agree.

The strongest result is the block structure of Figure 1, which rests on all MAGs at once. The weakest is the settled day of single MAGs, which can move with one unusual day. The variable positions do not point to any core MAG as a strain mixture. Two strains of one species may also end up as two separate MAGs (as may be the case for the two Prevotella copri_B MAGs, at %), and then each looks clean. Shares are of DNA, not cells.

Next steps and questions for tomorrow

  • Strain diversity per day. The question "is there more microdiversity around the switch?" needs each day's reads mapped on their own and a per-sample strain tool (for example inStrain, which also gives popANI between days). That is a server job of some hours, not something for this laptop analysis. Which MAGs should it focus on?
  • Two strains or one genome? The two Prevotella copri_B MAGs keep a fixed ratio over the ten days. Mapping both to one reference, or comparing their genes, could tell whether they are two strains or one genome split in two.
  • Day 4 and day 5. The largest day-to-day step is inside the first diet period. Is this day-to-day variation of stool, or something in the samples? A second sample of the same day would show how large sampling noise is.
  • A longer series. With one switch in one person the delay cannot be tested. More days after the switch, or a switch back, would show whether the new state is stable.
