Diet periods: genes or species, and stability

One person gave a stool sample on each of ten days: days 1-5 on a high-carbohydrate, high-calorie diet and days 6-10 on a low-carbohydrate, low-calorie diet. This report asks whether the genes for breaking down carbohydrates tell the two periods apart, and whether the community is steadier in one of them.

The question

The group asked two things: can the diet be predicted from the community's gene potential, and can the diet the person is used to be read from how stable the community is, assuming a community changes less on a familiar diet.

With one person and one diet switch there is nothing to train a predictor on, so this report asks a narrower question: do the days of the two periods differ in their carbohydrate-degrading genes (CAZymes), enough that a simple rule sorts the ten days into the right period? And does the species mix sort them better or worse than the genes? For stability it compares how much the days differ within each period. An answer would be a count of correctly sorted days for genes and for species, and a within-period difference for each period.

Guided examples

Data used

Three tables from module 8, all from the course's own sequencing run of the ten samples. First, the share of each day's DNA (bases) on each of the MAGs (module 4): one row per MAG and day, in percent of the sample's bases. This is a share of the DNA, not of the cells. The MAGs together hold -% of each day's bases; the rest is not in any MAG and is left out.

Second and third, the CAZyme genes of every MAG (module 7): genes per CAZy family per MAG, and degrading CAZyme genes (glycoside hydrolases GH, polysaccharide lyases PL, carbohydrate esterases CE) grouped by the substrate the family mostly acts on: plant fibre, starch, fructan or host glycans (the sugars of the gut's own mucus). Families with mixed or other substrates are not in the four groups. A gene in a MAG is potential, not activity.

About the diets (information from the data owner): both periods were vegetarian; the days 6-10 diet was closer to the person's normal diet; and the subject had eaten a similar high-calorie diet for about a week before day 1. So by day 1 the community had already had about a week on the first diet, and days 6-10 are a return towards the usual diet.

Approach

Community gene potential. For each day, the genes of a group are counted in every MAG, weighted by the MAG's share of that day's bases, and divided by all genes of the MAGs weighted the same way: genes per 1,000 genes of the binned community. A MAG at 10% of the bases counts ten times more than one at 1%.

Sorting the days. Each day has two profiles: its species mix (the MAG shares) and its gene profile (the weighted count of each of the degrading CAZy families). One day at a time is left out; the other days of each period are averaged (so the left-out day's own period mean is built from 4 days, the other period's from 5), and the left-out day goes to the period whose average it is closer to. Closeness is the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity (0 = identical profiles, 1 = nothing shared), after scaling each day to sum 1. A day sorted right counts one.

Stability. Bray-Curtis between all pairs of days within a period, and between neighbouring days only. Because day 6 is the first day of the new diet, the second period is also shown as days 7-10.

Checks. Before the numbers were seen, the expected result was written down: species sort most days right with day 6 likely wrong; genes differ less between days and sort fewer right. The positive control reproduces the published Bray-Curtis between the days (largest difference at four decimals; within days 1-5 , within days 6-10 , between periods , as in module 4's analysis), and the sorting rule puts two made-up, clearly different periods of 10 right. A second distance (Hellinger) gives the same sorting.

What this does and does not show

The two diet periods differ in both profiles: of 10 days sorted right by the species mix and by the genes, and between-period Bray-Curtis times the within-period mean for species against times for genes. The one-day difference is day 4, and it is not a real difference: its margin is with species and with genes, both close to zero (day 1 with genes: ). Day 6, the first day of the new diet, sorts with days 1-5 in both, which fits a community that takes more than a day to respond (in Figure 3 the species mix changes from day 7).

The gene potential moves less than the species mix, likely because many MAGs carry the same families, so when one MAG drops, others with similar genes keep the community's total similar. The biggest change in the four groups is host glycans, % (mean of days 6-10 against days 1-5), then fructan % and starch %. These are genes present in the MAGs, not genes used: whether the bacteria broke down more mucus in days 6-10 cannot be read from DNA.

The gene profiles differ less overall (all pairs for days 1-5, for days 6-10, for days 7-10; neighbouring days , and ), and leaving out day 6 does not bring them down to the days 1-5 level: the drop for days 7-10 shows only in the species mix.

We now know which diet was closer to the usual one: days 6-10 were closer to the person's usual diet (information from the data owner). The group's idea predicts a steadier community there; instead the days of the second period differ more. But this cannot be a test of the idea: the community had already had about a week on the high-calorie diet before day 1, so steadier days 1-5 fit "a week of adapting already done", and more different days 6-10 fit "the community changing after the switch". Both readings follow from the timing, not from which diet is habitual. This is a description of one person's ten days.

Next steps and questions for tomorrow

Separating timing from habit. Days 6-10 were a return towards the usual diet, after the week before day 1 and days 1-5 on the high-calorie diet. To tell "a familiar diet gives a steadier community" from "a community needs time after any switch", one would need samples from before the first diet started, or a longer series after the return; which would you choose?

Rarer families. Figure 4 lists only common families (at least per 1,000 genes). Rare families such as specialised fructan or mucin enzymes may change more; their numbers are small, so each would need a look at the MAGs that carry them (module 7).

Beyond CAZymes. The same sorting could be run on other gene sets (for example the KOfam marker genes in module 8), or on the share of reads that are not in any MAG.

More days or people. Telling a slow drift from a less stable community needs a longer series after the switch, a switch back, or more people; which of these would answer the group's second question best?
