Diet periods: genes or species, and stability

One person gave a stool sample on each of ten days: days 1-5 on a high-carbohydrate, high-calorie diet and days 6-10 on a low-carbohydrate, low-calorie diet. This report asks whether the community's genes for breaking down carbohydrates tell the two periods apart as well as its species mix does, and whether the community is steadier in one of the periods.

Summary

We followed the genomes (MAGs) of the course data set over the ten days and described each day in two ways: its species mix (the share of the day's DNA on each MAG) and its carbohydrate gene potential (CAZyme genes of each family, each MAG weighted by its share). A simple leave-one-out rule sorts of 10 days into the right diet period by species mix and by genes; the difference is one borderline day, and day 6, the first day of the new diet, is sorted with days 1-5 in both.

The gene potential changes much less than the species mix: the four substrate groups change by % (starch) to % (host glycans) between the period means, and the median CAZy family varies about times less from day to day than the median common MAG. Families carried by many MAGs vary least (Spearman ), which suggests that functional redundancy, many MAGs carrying the same genes, buffers the gene potential against changes in the species mix. The same holds for all genes with a specific name and for the marker genes of module 8; at the same number of carrying MAGs, carbohydrate genes vary somewhat more than other functions (CAZy families times as much), but how many MAGs carry a gene matters far more than what the gene does.

In an exploratory look at strains, MAGs with more variant sites (a sign of a mix of strains, from the reads of all days pooled) had slightly steadier shares over the ten days (Spearman ), and only of the MAGs that rose at least two-fold were in the most variable third; no MAG reached the level of a clear strain mixture. Of the genes, % have a specific name, but only % have at least 90% identity to the representative protein of the matched UniRef50 cluster; named genes hold -% of each day's DNA, genes without any function -%.

Days 6-10 differ more from each other than days 1-5 (Bray-Curtis against ), but neighbouring days differ about as much in both periods: after the switch the species mix mostly moves further away from days 1-5 rather than becoming more erratic. Many MAGs share in that change (the 10 largest account for % of it), whereas most of the small change in the gene profile is accounted for by a few Bacteroidota (%), led by Prevotella copri_B and Phocaeicola MAGs. Days 6-10 were closer to the person's usual diet, so one reading is a return towards the usual community; with one person and one switch, this is a description, not a test.

Background

Diet is one of the strongest short-term influences on the gut microbiota. In a controlled study, switching people to an entirely plant-based or animal-based diet changed the community rapidly, and plant-polysaccharide-degrading Firmicutes such as Roseburia and Eubacterium rectale fell on the animal-based diet [1]. The two diets here differ less (both vegetarian), but the amount of carbohydrate changed at day 6.

Communities that differ in their species can still carry similar genes. The Human Microbiome Project found (across people) that the metabolic pathways carried by the metagenome were stable although community structure varied widely [2]; many coexisting microbes encode the same functions, which is called functional redundancy [3]. Carbohydrate-active enzymes are classified in the CAZy database into families of glycoside hydrolases, polysaccharide lyases and carbohydrate esterases, with known substrates for many families [4]. When dietary fibre is scarce, gut bacteria can turn to the sugars of the host's mucus layer, as shown in mice colonised with a defined human gut community [5].

How fast does the community respond, and how much does it vary anyway? In the controlled diet study, with a stool sample on most days (16S rRNA gene sequencing), the community changed "a single day after the diet reached the distal gut microbiota", as shown by a food dye, and returned to its original structure 2 days after the animal-based diet ended; this was the animal-based diet only, as the plant-based diet gave no significant shift in overall composition [1]. In 34 people sampled daily for 17 days with shotgun metagenomes, "microbiome composition depended on multiple days of dietary history" [14]. Without a planned diet change, the gut community still varies from day to day: in 20 women sampled daily for six weeks, day-to-day shifts within a person were at times as large as differences between people, and samples one day apart differed as much from each other as samples one week or one month apart [15]; weekly samples over three months showed the same lack of a time trend [16].

The group's second idea builds on the ecology of stability and resilience, the ability of a community to return to its previous state after a disturbance [6]. A community adapted to its usual conditions might then fluctuate less on a familiar diet.

The study. One person, one stool sample per day for ten days; days 1-5 on a high-carbohydrate, high-calorie diet and days 6-10 on a low-carbohydrate, low-calorie diet. Information from the data owner: both periods were vegetarian; the days 6-10 diet was closer to the person's normal diet; and the subject had eaten a similar high-calorie diet for about a week before day 1. By day 1 the community had therefore already had about a week on the first diet, and days 6-10 are a return towards the usual diet.

Question and hypotheses

The group asked whether the diet can be predicted from the community's gene potential, and whether the diet the person is used to can be read from how stable the community is. With one person and one switch there is nothing to train or test a predictor on, so this report answers a narrower, descriptive question in two parts.

1. Do the two periods differ in carbohydrate gene potential, and do genes or species separate them better? Expected before the analysis: most days sorted right by species, with day 6 probably sorted with days 1-5; a weaker separation by genes, because genes are shared across many MAGs; changes of the substrate groups below about 30%.

2. Is the community steadier in one period? Expected: Bray-Curtis within days 1-5 about 0.21 and within days 6-10 about 0.24 (the overview analysis of the course data), with most of the extra spread in days 6-10 coming from the transition after the switch.

Data and methods

Data. All from the course's own sequencing run of the ten samples (module 8). Reads were assembled and binned with mmlong2 v1.2.1 [7] into MAGs, which together hold -% of each day's DNA; the rest is not in any MAG and is left out. Abundance is the share of a day's DNA (bases) on a MAG (module 4), not a share of cells. Genes of every MAG were predicted with Pyrodigal and searched with the dbCAN HMMs (run_dbcan release db_v5-2-9_5-5-2026) using pyhmmer; families were grouped by their main substrate (plant fibre, starch, fructan, host glycans) with dbCAN's family-substrate table [8] (module 7). Only degrading families (GH, PL, CE; families) are used. A gene in a MAG is potential, not activity.

Gene potential per day. For each day, the genes of a family or group are counted in every MAG, weighted by the MAG's share of that day's DNA, and divided by all genes of the MAGs weighted the same way: genes per 1,000 genes of the binned community.

Sorting the days. Each day has two profiles, the species mix (MAG shares) and the gene profile (genes per 1,000 genes of each degrading family), each scaled to sum 1. One day at a time is left out; the other days of each period are averaged (the left-out day's own period mean is built from 4 days, the other period's from 5), and the day is assigned to the period whose average is closer by Bray-Curtis dissimilarity (0 = identical, 1 = nothing shared). As checks, all CAZy families (including glycosyltransferases and binding modules) and a second distance (Hellinger) were used instead; both give the same days.

Stability and drift. Bray-Curtis between all pairs of days within a period, between neighbouring days only, and for days 7-10 (the second period without the switch day). Drift: each day's Bray-Curtis to the average of days 1-5 (for days 1-5, the average of the other four).

Redundancy. For each degrading family carried by at least one of the common MAGs (mean share of at least 0.1% of the DNA): the number of common MAGs that carry it, and its day-to-day variation, the coefficient of variation (standard deviation over mean, %) of its genes per 1,000 genes over the ten days. The same measure on the common MAGs' shares gives the species side.

Other gene sets. The same measure, with the same code, on two more sets of genes: the KOfam marker genes of module 8 (KEGG orthologs for butyrate, propionate, acetate, hydrogen, host and protein metabolism, found with the KOfam profiles and their score thresholds [10]), and all genes grouped by the product name Bakta v1.11.4 (database v6.0, light) gave them [9]. Only names more specific than a domain or family count as a function: not "hypothetical protein", genes without a Bakta match, or names that give only a domain or family (for example "DUF1234 domain-containing protein"). Such a name can still be broad, for example "Transposase" or "ABC transporter"; this leaves of genes (%) in names. A name is counted as carbohydrate-active when more than half of its genes carry a degrading CAZy domain. The sets are compared at the same number of carrying MAGs (Figure 3): the coefficient of variation of the other Bakta names is fitted against the number of carriers (both log scale), and each set's median distance from that line is given as a ratio (1.2 = varies 20% more than other functions carried by as many MAGs), with a 95% interval from resampling the functions 2,000 times. That interval treats the functions as independent, although functions carried by the same MAGs vary together; as a check, the ten days were resampled instead (1,000 times). As a second method, each function is compared with the median of the other Bakta names carried by exactly as many MAGs. As a further check, broad names (a class of protein without a substrate or pathway, such as transposases, kinases, transporters and regulators) were left out. The leave-one-out sorting was repeated on the named-gene and marker-gene profiles.

Which MAGs account for the changes. The change between the period means (days 1-5 against days 6-10; each MAG's share of the binned DNA averaged over each period, scaled to 100%) is split exactly into MAG parts. Species mix: the Bray-Curtis between the two period-mean profiles is half the summed absolute change of the MAG shares, so each MAG's part is half its own absolute change. Gene profile: a family's genes per 1,000 genes is the average of the MAGs' own genes per 1,000 genes, each MAG weighted by its share of the DNA times its number of genes; its change is the sum over MAGs of (change in weight) × (the MAG's own value minus the community value of days 1-5). For the whole profile, the summed absolute change of the degrading families is split the same way. The parts are summed by taxonomic family and phylum. Single-MAG parts of the gene profile depend on the reference value (days 1-5; with the midpoint of the two periods instead, some neighbouring ranks swap), while the totals and the family and phylum shares hardly change (Bacteroidota % with days 1-5, % with the midpoint). The profile split here is built from period-mean abundances; Figure 6 averages the daily profiles, which gives nearly the same values. This splits the change into parts; it does not show why each MAG changed.

Strain mixtures (exploratory). mmlong2 mapped the reads of all ten days together to the MAGs and called single-base variant sites with Longshot [11] (at least 6 reads, the other base in at least 3 reads and 10% of them; module 5). For each common MAG: the share of its positions that are variant sites (%; sites over the MAG's length, not rounded), and the median minor allele fraction of its sites, the share of reads with the less common base (in a mix of two strains, roughly the minor strain's share). Because the reads are pooled, these numbers say whether a MAG is a mix over the whole study, not whether the mix changed from day to day. They are set against the MAG's response: its day-to-day variation (coefficient of variation of its share over the ten days, %) and its change between the periods (log2). Low depth lowers the power to call variants, so the correlations are repeated with the depth taken out (partial Spearman), the split of the MAGs that rose is tested by shuffling the variant shares within fifths of the depth (5,000 times), and the main correlation is repeated within the two largest phyla. A second, independent sign of strains is CheckM's strain heterogeneity (module 5). This part is exploratory: its expectations were written after a first look at the correlations, and about correlations were looked at.

Genes with a known function. Each gene gets one of four levels: a specific Bakta name with at least 90% identity to the representative protein of the matched UniRef50 cluster (Bakta's light database searches only these clusters, which group proteins at least 50% identical to their representative [13]; the closest known protein may be more similar), a specific name with 50-90% identity to that representative, a name that gives only a domain or family, or no function (hypothetical protein or no match). The split at 90% is therefore a lower bound on how close the nearest known protein is. Shares of genes, and of each day's DNA: for each MAG the bases in genes of each level over the MAG's length, weighted by its share of the day; the rest of a MAG is outside genes, and the DNA in no MAG is a class of its own, since genes were only called in MAGs. These are shares of bases, not of reads: a long read of several thousand bases spans several genes.

Replication-rate feasibility. For each MAG, its mean coverage over all reads and on each day was taken from mmlong2's mapping (the mean depth of each contig per day, weighted by contig length), and set against the rule the group asked for (at least 75% complete, at least 5x on at least 5 days). No replication rates were calculated.

Checks. The expected results were written down before the analysis. Positive controls: the Bray-Curtis between days reproduces the overview analysis (largest difference at four decimals; within days 1-5 , within days 6-10 , between periods ); the sorting rule puts two made-up, clearly different periods of 10 right; and the families carried by a single common MAG vary like that MAG (Spearman ). For the other gene sets: the shared code reproduces the family results exactly; the marker gene counts per MAG equal module 8's table; and the named genes carried by a single common MAG vary like that MAG (Spearman ). The variant sites read from the variant file reproduce mmlong2's count for every MAG and its variant share; the share of genes without a function (%) matches Bakta's own count of hypothetical proteins (%); and the DNA classes add up to 100% on every day. The MAG parts add up to the Bray-Curtis of the period means and to the change of every family and substrate group. The per-day coverages reproduce each MAG's share of the day's DNA, and the ten days add up to the pooled coverage. Analysis: np26 scripts/44_project_group4.py (Python, pandas).

Interpretation. This is an exploratory analysis (see Data and methods). The variant share falls with depth (Spearman ), but depth hardly relates to the day-to-day variation (), and with the depth taken out the correlation stays at . It holds within Bacillota (, MAGs) but not within Bacteroidota (, MAGs), so it may not apply to every group. The link with the size of the change between the periods is weaker (Spearman , p ), but of the MAGs that rose at least two-fold, are in the least and only in the most variable third, and this split remains when the depth is held (p ). One explanation is redundancy at the strain level: when one strain of a mixed population drops, another may hold the total, just as many MAGs carrying one gene family steady it (Figures 2 and 3). Another is that the MAGs that rose after the switch were clonal populations growing from low numbers. Two cautions: some variant sites may come from reads of related species ( of the 12 most variable MAGs are Faecalibacterium), and because the reads were pooled, this cannot show whether the strain mix itself shifted after the switch. In a study following people over months, resident strains were rarely replaced [12].

Discussion

Genes or species. Both profiles separate the two diet periods, and neither does it clearly better: the one-day difference in the sorting is a borderline day. The species mix changes more, and the gene potential much less, in line with the Human Microbiome Project's finding (across people) that pathway content varies much less than community structure [2]. The redundancy analysis (Figure 2) shows the same pattern within one person over ten days: the more MAGs carry a family, the steadier it is, and the same holds for all named genes (Figure 3). Carbohydrate genes are slightly less steady than other functions carried by as many MAGs, even within one diet period. Here, in this one person, the switch mainly reshuffled which MAGs carry the gene potential, not how many such genes the community has.

Timing. Day 6, the first sample after the switch, still resembled days 1-5, and the distance to days 1-5 rose from day 7 (Figure 5). This fits the controlled diet study, where the community changed a single day after the new food reached the distal gut, timed from the food's arrival (marked by a dye) rather than from the first meal [1], although that was for an animal-based diet; the plant-based diet gave no significant shift, and our switch between two vegetarian diets is closer to the plant-based arm, so the match is weaker; and it fits the finding that composition depends on several days of dietary history [14]. Our data cannot pin the lag down: we do not know the person's transit time, and the day-6 sample may have been passed before much of the new diet reached the colon. A one-day lag with no shift on day 6 is therefore consistent with these studies, not a measurement of the lag.

How large is our day-to-day variation? Neighbouring days differ by a Bray-Curtis of about on MAG shares and on genera (days 1-5). Published values are not directly comparable. The six-week daily study computed the within-person Bray-Curtis on quantitative genus-level profiles: 16S counts corrected for gene copy number and scaled to cells per gram of stool, that is absolute abundances, not shares [15]; we could not find a consecutive-day value in its text (they are shown in its figures). The weekly study used UniFrac distances on 16S sequence groups, rarefied to 10,000 reads, not Bray-Curtis, and its relative 16S profiles count genomes with more 16S gene copies more [16]. Our profiles are shares of sequenced DNA on genomes (no rarefying), which count larger genomes more and leave out the DNA in no MAG; our genera are GTDB genera, which split some classic genera (for example Blautia and Blautia_A). What does compare is the pattern: as in both studies, days 1-5 show no drift with time (neighbouring days differ by , all pairs of days 1-5 by ), while after the switch the distance to days 1-5 grows.

Stability and the habitual diet. The group's idea predicts a steadier community on the familiar diet, which here is days 6-10. Instead, days 6-10 differ more from each other. But the timing could explain this equally well: days 1-5 follow about a week on a similar high-calorie diet, so the community had already had time to adapt, while days 6-10 start right after a switch and include the transition. A steadier days 1-5 fits "a week of adaptation already done", and a less steady days 6-10 fits "the community changing after the switch"; both could follow from timing as well as from which diet is habitual. Neighbouring days differ about equally in both periods, which argues against a generally less stable community on either diet. Resilience, a return to a previous state after a disturbance [6], would show as a drift towards the usual community after the switch, which fits what Figure 5 shows; but without a sample from before the high-calorie week, we cannot tell a return from a new direction.

Strains. Within the MAGs, the more mixed populations were slightly steadier (Spearman ; Figure 8), which fits the same idea of redundancy one level down; but the analysis is exploratory, variant sites are few, the effect is seen in Bacillota but not in Bacteroidota, the reads are pooled over the days, and part of the signal may come from related species. Whether the strain mix moved after the switch is open.

What is solid and what is not. Solid: the periods differ in species mix, day 6 still resembles days 1-5, and the gene potential is far steadier than the species mix, most of all for families carried by many MAGs. Uncertain: the direction of the small changes in substrate groups, the cause of the drift, anything about habitual diet and stability, and whether strain mixtures cause the steadier shares.

Limitations

  • One person, ten days and one diet switch: descriptions, not tests; nothing here generalises to other people.
  • Gene potential, not activity: a CAZyme gene in a MAG says nothing about whether it was expressed.
  • Abundance is the share of DNA (bases), not of cells, and only the binned -% of each day's DNA is included.
  • Substrate groups come from a family-level table; families acting on several substrates ("mixed") are left out of the four groups.
  • Grouping genes by their Bakta name is coarse: one function can carry several names and one name can cover several functions, some names are broad (for example "Transposase"), and the light database names many genes only by a domain (left out here).
  • Variant sites come from the reads of all ten days pooled: they show mixtures over the whole study, not day-to-day changes in the strain mix; reads from related species can add sites.
  • Function levels come from Bakta's light database, which gives only the identity to the representative of a UniRef50 cluster: a specific name below 90% identity is a likely, not a certain, function, and the 90% split understates how close the nearest known protein may be.
  • Days 1-5 are not a baseline: the subject had already eaten a similar diet for about a week, so the stability pattern after the switch could reflect timing as much as which diet is habitual.

Open questions and possible next analyses

Does the drift level off? Whether the distance to days 1-5 (Figure 5) slows down by day 10 cannot be judged from five days; a longer series after the switch, or a sample from before the high-calorie week, would be needed (not measured).

Do the main contributors follow the diet? Figure 7 names the MAGs that account for the change, but not why they changed. Following the largest contributors (the two Prevotella copri_B MAGs, Acetatifactor intestinalis, Phocaeicola vulgatus) day by day against what was eaten would need a food diary per day (not available to us).

Rarer families. Figure 6 lists only common families (at least per 1,000 genes). Specialised fructan or mucin enzymes are rarer and carried by fewer MAGs, so by Figure 2 they may change more; each needs a look at the MAGs that carry it (module 7).

Why do carbohydrate genes vary a little more? Split each function's day-to-day variation into the contributions of the MAGs that carry it, to see whether carbohydrate genes depend on fewer, more variable MAGs than their carrier count suggests (on the Mac, from the shared tables).

Did the strain mix change after the switch? Count the reads with each base at the known variant sites separately for each day (the reads of each day mapped to the MAGs, a server job; the mappings exist on the server), and follow the minor allele fraction of the most variable MAGs, such as , over the ten days.

Replication rates. Figure 10 lists the MAGs that meet the published thresholds. Estimating rates would need the coverage along each genome for each day, from the per-day mappings made for the methylation analysis if they were kept, or from new ones (on a server; not run in this course), and then a test of how even long-read coverage is before trusting the rates.

Genes without a function, and closer matches. Annotate the genes with Bakta's full database (UniRef90 clusters and exact matches, which give the identity to a much closer known protein) or eggNOG, and search the no-function genes for Pfam domains (a server job), to see how many named genes have a close known relative and how much of the rise after the switch comes from poorly known MAGs.
