How much of the sequenced data does the assembly represent?

A short research report on one question about the whole study: how much of each day's sequenced DNA ends up in the assembly, and how much in the genomes recovered from it (MAGs). It answers the group's first question with the plot they asked for (Figure 1) and will grow with their next questions. Interpretation is marked as such ("this suggests", "one explanation is"); everything else is a measured result of this study or a cited source.

Summary

The group asked how much of the data the assembly actually covers, per sample, and how much of it is in bins. We measured this in bases (the summed length of the reads), because nanopore reads differ greatly in length and the study's abundances are shares of bases. Over all reads used for the assembly ( Gbp after the quality and length filter), -% of each sample's bases map to the assembly and -% to the MAGs (pooled % and %). A random sample of the raw reads, mapped separately, gives the same picture: -% of the filtered bases align to the assembly and -% to MAGs. The MAGs hold four fifths of the bases although their contigs are only % of the assembly's length. Our interpretation: almost all of the sequenced DNA is represented in the assembly, and most of it in MAGs, so the MAG-based results of this course describe most of each day's DNA; about one sixth lies on contigs that were not binned.

Background

A metagenome is sequenced as a mixture of all the DNA in a sample. Assembly joins overlapping reads into longer contigs, and binning groups the contigs that seem to come from one genome into metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs). Every later result of this course, from the share of a MAG on a day to the genes it carries, rests on the MAGs, so it matters how much of the sequenced DNA they represent. Part of the DNA never makes it: genomes with too few reads to assemble, regions that are hard to assemble, and contigs that the binning tools cannot place. A common way to say how well a genome collection represents a community is to map the reads back and report the share that lands on it; adding MAGs from new studies to the reference collections raised the share of sequencing data that could be classified, both in human gut samples [1] and in soil and sediment samples sequenced with nanopore reads and assembled with mmlong2 [2], the workflow used here.

The study: one person gave a stool sample on each of ten days. Both diet periods were vegetarian. Days 1-5 were high in carbohydrate and calories, days 6-10 low in carbohydrate and calories, closer to the subject's normal diet. The subject had eaten a similar high-calorie diet for about a week before day 1, so the switch at day 6 is the main change (information from the data owner). All ten samples were sequenced on one nanopore flow cell and assembled together (one co-assembly of Gbp in contigs), then binned into MAGs from contigs.

Question. For each of the ten samples, which share of the sample's data maps to the assembly, and which share to the contigs in bins (MAGs)? Expected (written before the analysis, from the study's earlier results): MAGs -% of the filtered bases per sample, as in the study's abundance table; the assembly 95-99% in every sample; a lower share for all sequenced reads than for the filtered reads, most of all when counted in reads, because the filter removed many short and low-quality reads.

Data and methods

Which data. "The data" can mean two things here. (1) The reads used for the assembly: the study kept reads with a mean quality of at least Q and a length of at least bases and trimmed their adapters ( million reads, Gbp, - Gbp per sample; module 2). (2) All sequenced reads, before that filter. The filter removed % of the reads but only % of the bases (read sample, below), because the removed reads are mostly short.

Unit. All shares are percent of a sample's bases (summed read length), per sample. A nanopore read can be a few hundred or tens of thousands of bases long, so one long read carries as much DNA as many short ones, and a share of reads would weight the short reads too heavily. Read shares are shown next to them in Table 1 and Figure 2.

Full data (Figure 1, filled points). mmlong2 [2] assembled the reads with myloasm , mapped each sample's filtered reads back to the contigs with minimap2 [3] and computed the mean depth of every contig in every sample with MetaBAT2's depth tool (version ) [4]. The bases of a sample on a contig are its mean depth times the contig's length. The assembly share is the sum over all contigs, the MAG share the sum over the contigs in bins, each divided by the sample's filtered bases. This uses every read; it is how the study computes MAG abundance (module 4).

Read sample (Figure 1, open points; Figure 2). The course's random sample of raw reads (median per sample, in all, before any filter) was mapped to the assembly with minimap2 [3] (module 2). For each read we counted the read bases covered by its alignments (overlapping alignments once), and those on contigs in bins. This is the only measure available for all sequenced reads, including those the filter removed; the filtered reads are the sampled reads that pass the study's filter (- per sample). Its 95% intervals come from resampling the reads ( bootstrap resamples).

Checks (the script stops if one fails): the full-data MAG share per sample equals the study's MAG abundance table; the pooled profile equals the base-weighted mean of the ten samples; the read-sample counts equal module 2's; independently, the read sample's MAG share is within percentage points of the full data in every sample, and the pooled shares agree with mmlong2's own run summary (% of the bases assembled, % binned, % of the assembly length in bins). Analysis: np26 scripts/46_project_group6.py.

Interpretation. In every sample almost all of the filtered bases map to the assembly (-%), and the share hardly differs between days. The MAG share varies more, from % () to % (); it was lower on days 1-5 (mean %) than on days 6-10 (%). One explanation is that more of the early samples' DNA came from bacteria whose genomes stayed on unbinned contigs (low coverage or fragmented); with one person and the diet confounded with time this is a description, not a diet effect. The read sample puts the assembly share -%, about three points above the full data. For the MAG share the two measures agree within points. Limitations

  • The full-data shares rest on mmlong2's depth table, which counts only alignments that pass the depth tool's filters; the assembly share may be a few points too low (see Figure 1).
  • For all sequenced reads (before the filter) only the read sample is available, about reads per sample; a base share from so few reads is sensitive to single very long reads.
  • "Maps to the assembly" means a read aligns to a contig; it does not mean that the contig is correct or that the read comes from that genome alone.
  • Shares are relative to what was sequenced: DNA that the extraction or library preparation missed is not counted at all.
  • One person, ten days, one sample per day: differences between days are descriptions, not effects of the diet.

Open questions and possible next analyses

  • What is on the unbinned contigs? % of the bases lie on contigs that were not binned: their length, coverage and taxonomy (contig table, on the Mac).
  • Why do days 1-5 have a lower MAG share? Which unbinned contigs are more abundant in those samples (contig depths per sample, on the Mac).
  • All reads, full data: map every raw read of each sample (before the filter) to the assembly for exact shares without sampling noise (a server job).
  • Assembly share without the depth filter: count aligned bases directly in mmlong2's read mappings, to confirm the explanation for the three-point gap (a server job).
