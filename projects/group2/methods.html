Methodological rationale and limitations

The separate document group 2 asked for, written for the manuscript. It explains why each analysis was chosen, what it assumes, where it can mislead and what credible alternatives exist, how uncertainty and multiple comparisons are handled, which decisions were taken after seeing the data, and how to reproduce the work. Numbers come from the same data file as the manuscript.

1. Inference framework

What the design allows. One person gave ten consecutive daily samples, five on each diet. The days are a short time series, not ten independent replicates: neighbouring days share most of their community (autocorrelation), and the diet periods are confounded with time, with the order of the diets and with the week of the same high-calorie diet before day 1. No sample was taken before that week. A difference between the periods can therefore come from the diet, from a change that was under way anyway, or from carry-over, and the data cannot tell these apart. Every result is a description of this person over these days.

No tests, so no multiple-testing correction. We report no p-values and test no hypotheses. A test that treats the five days of each period as independent would overstate the evidence, and with one switch there is no replication of the diet effect to test against. Because there are no tests, there is nothing to correct for multiple testing. The multiplicity problem does not go away, however: we looked at MAGs, the substrate groups, CAZy families and cut-off settings, and among many contrasts some will look large by chance. We handle this by (i) writing the expected result before each analysis, (ii) reporting all contrasts in the supplementary tables rather than only the large ones, (iii) never ranking or selecting results by a p-value, (iv) labelling views added or changed after seeing the data as exploratory, and (v) treating a result as solid only when it holds across the sensitivity analyses.

Effect sizes and their uncertainty. Effect sizes are differences of period means: log2 fold changes for MAGs, percent changes for CAZyme potential, differences in percentage points for phyla. Their uncertainty is described by a day-resampling interval (the middle 90% of resamplings of the five days within each period) and by the range over leaving out one day at a time. Both treat the days within a period as exchangeable, which they are not; the intervals describe how much a number depends on the particular days sampled, are not confidence intervals and are not adjusted for the number of contrasts. A MAG with a steady trend inside a period gets a wide interval, which is the intended warning.

2. Inputs and missing information

The analysis started from an inventory of the available tables (below; manuscript Supplementary Table 1). All come from the course's own sequencing run of the ten samples, processed with mmlong2 and the course's annotation steps. Missing for the group's questions: food records or nutrient intake per day, a sample before the high-calorie week, replicate DNA extractions or sequencing runs, RNA, protein or metabolite data (activity), cell counts or spike-ins (absolute abundance), and a read-based profile of the bases outside the MAGs. The diet facts (both periods vegetarian, days 6-10 closer to the person's normal diet, a similar high-calorie diet for about a week before day 1) come from the data owner.

3. Analysis by analysis

3.1 Relative abundance from mapped bases

WhyThe share of a day's sequenced bases that maps to a MAG is the most direct per-genome abundance a long-read metagenome gives, and it is comparable between days of different depth.
AssumesReads map to the right MAG; DNA extraction and sequencing recover all taxa equally on every day; MAG boundaries are right.
PitfallsShares are compositional: a rise in one MAG lowers all others. DNA from dead cells or food counts too. Only the -% of bases in MAGs are seen.
AlternativesCoverage depth per MAG (depends on sequencing effort); read-based taxonomic profiles (include unbinned reads, lose genome resolution); absolute abundance with spike-ins or cell counts (not measured).

3.2 Bray-Curtis dissimilarity and principal coordinates

WhyA standard, easily read summary of how different two days are, weighted by abundance, so that it follows the dominant MAGs; PCoA shows the main gradient across the days.
AssumesShares of the binned bases are comparable between days; the distance captures the change of interest.
PitfallsDominated by abundant MAGs; not a compositional distance; within- and between-period means use overlapping pairs of days, so they are not independent numbers. With only ten days, one dominant gradient can give the first axis a large share of the variation (here %).
AlternativesAitchison distance (3.3, done); Jaccard (presence only); phylogenetic distances (UniFrac); non-metric scaling (NMDS). PERMANOVA would add a p-value, but its permutations assume exchangeable samples, which autocorrelated days are not.

3.3 Centred log-ratio and Aitchison distance

WhyThe group asked to consider compositional effects. Log-ratios against the day's geometric mean remove the closure of shares [7], so a MAG's change is read relative to the community rather than to a fixed total.
AssumesA pseudocount (0.01%) for zeros; the reference (geometric mean of the MAGs) is stable enough.
PitfallsResults depend on the pseudocount and on which MAGs form the reference; a CLR change is relative, not absolute.
AlternativesAdditive log-ratios against a reference taxon; balances (isometric log-ratios); tools built on log-ratios for groups of samples. Here CLR agreed with the plain log2 change (Spearman ), so the choice does not change the conclusions.

3.4 Diversity

WhyShannon diversity, shown as its effective number of MAGs, is easy to read (the number of equally common MAGs with the same diversity) and responds to both richness and evenness.
AssumesThe MAGs with a mean share of at least 0.1% are detected on every day regardless of depth.
PitfallsRare MAGs are left out on purpose (their detection depends on depth); only binned bases count; a stable index can hide a reshuffle of members (which is what happened here).
AlternativesRichness after rarefying reads; Simpson or Hill numbers of other orders; diversity on all MAGs (in the script, same conclusion).

3.5 Period change of single MAGs

WhyThe log2 ratio of the period means is a plain effect size with a symmetric scale (+1 = doubled, −1 = halved); the two-fold and 0.1% cut-offs keep the list to MAGs that change visibly and are measured on every day.
AssumesA pseudocount of 0.01% keeps absent MAGs finite; the two periods are the relevant contrast.
PitfallsCut-offs are arbitrary and shape the counts (3.9); a large pseudocount shrinks changes of small MAGs (seen: two-fold MAGs at 0.1% against at 0.01%); a period mean hides the timing (3.6).
AlternativesDifferential-abundance tools such as DESeq2, ANCOM-BC, ALDEx2 or MaAsLin2. They are designed for groups of independent samples; on five against five autocorrelated days their p-values would look more certain than the data are, so we did not use them.

3.6 Step or trend: the split check

WhyThe group asked to separate phase-associated shifts from general time trends. Fitting each of the nine possible early/late splits and a straight line, each with two parameters, shows where a MAG changes most and whether a step at the switch describes it better than a steady drift, without a test.
AssumesOne change point or one linear trend; log2 shares; the same weight for every day.
PitfallsChoosing the best of nine splits favours a step over a line (so the fair comparison uses the 5|6 step only); the two-fold MAGs were selected on the 5|6 split, which favours a step there; ten days cannot separate a delayed response from a trend that began by chance.
AlternativesSegmented regression or Bayesian change-point models; models with a trend and a step together; autoregressive or state-space models; smoothing splines. All need more time points than ten to estimate their extra parameters reliably.

3.7 Day resampling

Why it was done. The research question asks which MAGs differ between the two diet periods. With one person and one sample per day there are no replicate samples, so a "two-fold change" could rest on one unusual day, for example the weakly sequenced day 2 or the transition day 6. Day resampling and leaving out one day at a time (3.8) ask the same practical question: would the MAG still look changed if a somewhat different set of days had been sampled? This matters because the list of risers and fallers (Fig. 4a), and the interpretation built on it, should not depend on a single day.

Step by step, with one MAG. The numbers below are for the MAG chosen in the menu (Fig. R1); Acetatifactor intestinalis is a clear riser, Roseburia_D yibonii the one two-fold MAG whose interval includes zero.

    Fig. R1 | Day resampling and leaving out one day, for one MAG

    Fig. R1 | Day resampling and leaving out one day, for one MAG. a, The MAG's measured share of each day's bases (top row of each period, percent) and the first of the random draws of five days with replacement (second row; days drawn twice or more appear twice or more, days not drawn are missing). The box at the right is the mean of the row; the two means give the log2 change written below. b, The log2 changes from all draws (bars, 0.25 log2 units wide; values beyond the axis are counted in the end bars), the observed change (dot), the 5th and 95th percentiles that form the 90% interval in Fig. 4a (solid lines) and two-fold (dashed). c, The log2 change with each of the ten days left out (dots); dashed line: two-fold. A filled dot in Fig. 4a means every dot here lies beyond the dashed line.

    WhyA simple, transparent way to show how much each contrast depends on the particular days sampled, as the group asked for uncertainty with every effect size.
    AssumesDays within a period are exchangeable.
    PitfallsThe intervals are too narrow, for three reasons: a percentile bootstrap of a five-day mean is too narrow even for independent days (its spread is about √(4/5) of the true standard error); each period has only 126 distinct resamples; and neighbouring days are autocorrelated, so they carry less independent information than five days would. A trend inside a period widens them. They are not adjusted for the number of contrasts. And for MAGs selected because they changed a lot, an interval that excludes zero is close to built in: " of exclude zero" shows mainly that no single day carries the change, not that the change is certain.
    AlternativesMoving-block bootstrap (blocks of two or three days leave almost no distinct resamples in five days); permuting the position of the switch (only nine splits, so the switch can at best rank first of nine); Bayesian models with autocorrelation (too few days for the extra parameters).

    3.8 Leave one day out

    WhyShows whether a single day, for example the weakly sequenced day 2 or the transition day 6, drives a result.
    HowEach of the ten days is removed once; the period means, the log2 changes and the list of two-fold MAGs are recomputed on the other nine days (four in one period, five in the other). For the worked example in 3.7 the ten runs are shown in Fig. R1c.
    AssumesNine days still describe each period.
    PitfallsRemoving a day changes which MAGs pass the 0.1% cut-off, so counts can move for reasons unrelated to the change itself.
    AlternativesLeaving out pairs of days; jackknife estimates of variance (assume independence).

    3.9 Cut-off sensitivity

    WhyEvery count of "changed MAGs" depends on three choices (fold change, minimum share, pseudocount); a grid of settings shows which conclusions survive other reasonable choices.
    AssumesThe grid spans the reasonable range.
    PitfallsCounts at strict cut-offs are small and jumpy; looking at many settings invites picking the one that fits.
    AlternativesReporting a continuous ranking of all MAGs (done in Supplementary Table 4) instead of counts.

    3.10 MAG quality

    WhyMedium-quality MAGs can be incomplete or contaminated, which distorts both abundance and gene content; repeating the analysis with the high-quality MAGs only shows which results depend on the medium-quality ones.
    AssumesCheckM2 estimates are right [9]; the quality classes follow the MIMAG thresholds [10].
    PitfallsDropping MAGs changes the community being described; a key MAG can be medium quality for reasons other than completeness (missing rRNA genes). Here the plant-fibre result changed (% with all MAGs, % with HQ only) because both P. copri_B MAGs are medium quality.
    AlternativesWeighting by completeness; correcting gene counts for completeness; manual refinement of the key MAGs.

    3.11 Sequencing depth

    WhyDepth varied between samples ( to Gbp) and was higher in days 6-10, so a period difference could partly be a depth effect on which MAGs are detected.
    AssumesShares of bases do not depend on depth on average; well-covered MAGs are measured equally well on every day.
    PitfallsDepth runs with time (Spearman with PCoA axis 1), so the two cannot be separated by correlation. On the MAGs above 0.5% the result is the same, but within days 1-5 depth and axis 1 have the same rank order (Spearman ; days 6-10 ): not explained.
    AlternativesSubsampling every sample's reads to the same number of bases and repeating the mapping (a server job); sequencing the shallow samples again.

    3.12 CAZyme annotation and substrate groups

    WhyThe dbCAN HMMs are a standard way to find CAZyme families in predicted proteins, and dbCAN3's substrate information groups families by what they degrade [14]; searching every MAG with the same models keeps the community comparable.
    AssumesAn HMM hit means a functional enzyme of that family; a family's substrate group describes its typical substrate.
    PitfallsOne tool, no consensus of several; some families are weak (GH109: many hits are not glycoside hydrolases); families act on several substrates; incomplete MAGs miss genes; gene presence is potential, not activity.
    AlternativesdbCAN's consensus of HMM, DIAMOND and subfamily searches; polysaccharide utilisation loci for Bacteroidota [5]; metatranscriptomics or metaproteomics for activity (not measured).

    3.13 Abundance-weighted community potential

    WhyThe group asked for abundance-weighted potential. Weighting each MAG's genes by its share gives the community's gene content per 1,000 genes, which does not grow with genome size or completeness and can be compared between days.
    AssumesA share of bases is proportional to the share of genomes; the MAGs represent the community.
    PitfallsLarge genomes take more bases per cell; unbinned bases are left out; a stable total can hide opposite changes within it (seen here).
    AlternativesWeighting by genome copies (share divided by genome size); annotating all reads directly (includes unbinned reads, loses genome links).

    3.14 Contribution decomposition

    WhyThe group asked which MAGs drive the CAZyme change. The decomposition splits the change at the period-mean shares exactly into one term per MAG, positive when a MAG rises and is richer than the community (or falls and is poorer), and sums by phylum.
    AssumesThe change at the period-mean shares stands for the change of the daily means (they differ slightly).
    PitfallsA large contribution needs a large change in share or an unusual gene content, so abundant MAGs dominate; it is an accounting identity, not a cause.
    AlternativesLeaving each MAG out in turn and recomputing (does not add up); Shapley-value decompositions; taxon-level contribution methods developed for groups of samples.

    3.15 CAZy family view (exploratory)

    WhyChecks whether single enzyme families change behind stable substrate groups.
    AssumesFamilies with at least genes per 1,000 genes are measured well enough.
    PitfallsAdded after the main analysis; its cut-off was lowered after seeing the data; with families, the largest changes are partly chance. Its heatmap uses a diverging blue-grey-orange scale rather than viridis because it shows signed changes around zero (log2 relative to days 1-5).
    AlternativesSubfamilies; utilisation loci; activity data.

    4. Expected and observed

    The expected result of every analysis was written in the analysis script before it was run; results inside and outside the expected range got the same checks. Inside the range: Bray-Curtis within and between the periods; change from day 7; the day-resampling intervals ( of two-fold MAGs exclude zero; total CAZyme potential includes zero; host glycan above zero); the compositional check; the community split check (best split ); the phylum decomposition; completeness and CAZyme rate unrelated.

    Outside the range, each checked before it was used: PCoA axis 1 held % (expected 25-50%; all axes rebuild the matrix exactly, so the share is right). Only CAZy families reached the first family cut-off (expected 30-60; the cut-off was lowered, see 5). One leave-one-day-out run gave two-fold MAGs, one above the expected maximum. A pseudocount of 0.1% halved the count of two-fold MAGs (expected less than 20%; a pseudocount the size of the smallest MAGs shrinks their changes, checked by hand). With high-quality MAGs only, plant fibre changed by % instead of % (expected within 5 points; traced to the two medium-quality P. copri_B MAGs). Depth correlated with axis 1 (, expected below 0.6; follow-up on well-covered MAGs in 3.11).

    5. Decisions taken after seeing the data

    • The CAZy family view was added after the main analysis, and its cut-off lowered from to genes per 1,000 genes once only families reached the first one; it is labelled exploratory.
    • The depth follow-up (well-covered MAGs, depth within each period) was added after the first depth check came out of range; its expected result was written before it was run.
    • The manuscript form, the resampling intervals, the sensitivity grid, the compositional and quality checks and the phylum decomposition were added at the group's request, after the first version of the report; the main numbers (Bray-Curtis, two-fold counts, CAZyme changes) did not change.

    6. Limitations

    • One person, ten days, one switch: no biological replicates, no tests; nothing generalises beyond this person.
    • Diet is confounded with time, order and the high-calorie week before day 1; there is no sample before it and none after day 10.
    • Autocorrelation and carry-over: neighbouring days are not independent, and the community may respond over several days.
    • Shares of DNA, not cell counts; dead cells and food DNA count; only binned bases are seen.
    • Depth differed between samples and ran with time; a depth link within days 1-5 is not explained.
    • Two medium-quality MAGs drive part of the CAZyme change.
    • CAZyme genes are potential, not activity; one annotation tool; broad substrate groups.

    7. Reproducibility

    Code. One analysis script, scripts/42_project_group2.py of the course's analysis code, reads the input tables (section 2), runs every analysis, checks its positive controls (it stops if one fails) and writes the result tables and the data file behind the manuscript. It needs Python with pandas and numpy and runs in seconds on a laptop; the resampling uses a fixed seed, so a rerun gives the same numbers. The code is available from the course teacher; the expected results and their outcomes are written in the script's header.

    Processed data and figure source data. Every figure in the manuscript has a source-data download (CSV or TSV) under its legend, and the ten supplementary tables can be downloaded in the same formats. They hold derived data only (shares, distances, gene counts per MAG), no reads.

    Upstream steps. Read filtering, assembly, binning, quality and taxonomy (mmlong2 [8], CheckM2 [9], GTDB-Tk) and the gene and CAZyme search are documented in the course modules (module 8, module 4, module 5, module 7).
