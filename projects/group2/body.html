Group 2 · research report (draft)

Community and carbohydrate potential over the ten days

A short research report on group 2's question, worked out on the course data. Every number is computed from the data by the course's analysis script (np26 scripts/42_project_group2.py); hover over a chart to read its values. Interpretation is marked as such.

Summary

We followed the gut community of one person over ten daily stool samples, five days on a high-carbohydrate, high-calorie diet and five days on a low-carbohydrate, low-calorie diet, using genomes (MAGs) assembled from nanopore reads. The community changed between the two periods (Bray-Curtis dissimilarity between days of different periods against and within them), but mostly from day 7 on, not at the diet switch. Its diversity did not change (about effective MAGs in both periods).

Of the MAGs with a mean share of at least 0.1%, fell and rose at least two-fold; all that fell are Bacillota, among them the plant-fibre degraders Roseburia and Agathobacter rectalis. A break-point check puts the largest change of only of these MAGs at the switch and of one or two days later. The community's potential to break down carbohydrates, measured as CAZyme genes per 1,000 genes weighted by each MAG's share, hardly moved ( and , %), and no common CAZy family changed 1.5-fold. Our interpretation: the diet changed who is there more than what the community as a whole can digest, because the MAGs that replaced each other carry similar enzyme repertoires; the delay of one to two days fits a community that reflects several days of diet.

Background

The gut microbiome responds fast to diet: in a controlled study, switching people to an entirely plant-based or animal-based diet changed community structure within days, and the animal-based diet lowered Firmicutes that break down plant polysaccharides (Roseburia, Eubacterium rectale) [1]. With daily sampling, community composition depended on several days of dietary history and the responses were personal [2]. Lowering carbohydrate intake reduced the Roseburia / E. rectale group and faecal butyrate in a weight-loss study [3].

Which carbohydrates a community can use is written in its carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZyme) genes. Gut bacteria carry a large and varied CAZyme repertoire [4], and Bacteroidota organise much of theirs in polysaccharide utilisation loci, gene clusters dedicated to one glycan each [5]. When dietary fibre is scarce, gut bacteria can turn to host mucus glycans [6], so a change in diet could shift the balance between plant-fibre and host-glycan enzymes. Finally, sequencing measures shares, not amounts: a rise of one group pushes all others down, so relative abundance has to be read as a composition [7].

The study. One person gave a stool sample on ten consecutive days; days 1-5 were high carbohydrate and high calorie, days 6-10 low carbohydrate and low calorie (course sample sheet). Both periods were vegetarian, and the days 6-10 diet was closer to the person's normal diet; the subject had eaten a similar high-calorie diet for about a week before day 1, so days 1-5 continue that diet and the switch at day 6 is the main change in the study, with no sample from before the high-calorie diet (information from the data owner).

Question and hypotheses

The group asked two questions. (1) How do composition, diversity and single MAGs change over the ten days and between the two diet periods, and is a MAG's shift a step at the diet switch or a gradual trend? (2) Does the community's carbohydrate-degradation potential differ between the periods, and which MAGs account for the difference? The group asked for a manuscript; this report gives the analyses with short methods, interpretation and limits. With one person and one switch everything is described, not tested, and diet cannot be separated from time.

Expected, written before the analysis. From the course's earlier overview: days 1-6 alike, change from day 7, days 8-10 most different; many MAG shifts later than the switch; fewer plant-fibre degrading Bacillota on the low-carbohydrate diet [1, 3]. For the CAZymes: a few tens of genes per 1,000 genes, a period difference below about 20%, plant fibre down if the Bacillota that fell drive it, host-glycan families up with Bacteroides and Phocaeicola [5, 6].

Data and methods

Data. All from the course's own sequencing run of the ten samples (module 8). MAG abundance: for each of the MAGs (all high or medium quality, module 5) and each day, the percent of that day's sequenced bases that map to the MAG (module 4); the MAGs together hold -% of each day's bases. CAZymes: per MAG, the degrading CAZyme genes (glycoside hydrolases, polysaccharide lyases, carbohydrate esterases) per family and per substrate group, and the MAG's number of genes (module 7).

Composition. Phylum shares are summed MAG shares. Bray-Curtis dissimilarity between days uses all MAG shares, each day scaled to sum 1 (so it compares shares of the binned bases; 0 = identical, 1 = nothing shared); principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) maps the ten days so that distances follow Bray-Curtis. Diversity is the Shannon index on the MAGs with a mean share of at least 0.1%, shown as its effective number of MAGs.

Single MAGs. Period change = log2 of the mean share in days 6-10 over days 1-5 (0.01% added to both). Break check: on log2(share + 0.01%), each of the nine splits of the ten days into an early and a late block is fitted with two block means, and its share of the day-to-day variation (R²) is compared with a straight line over the days; the split with the highest R² is the best split. Separation = number of low-carbohydrate days beyond all five high-carbohydrate days.

CAZyme potential. Per day: Σ share × CAZyme genes / Σ share × all genes × 1,000, over all MAGs, per substrate group and per family; a period value is the mean of its five days. "All CAZyme genes" counts each gene once, so the groups do not add up to it. A MAG's contribution to the change is 1,000 × (its change in mean share) × (its genes in the group − the community's days 1-5 rate × its genes) / (the community's weighted gene count in days 6-10); the contributions add up exactly to the change at the period-mean shares. The family view uses the families with at least 0.2 genes per 1,000 genes (of found).

Checks and tools. Recomputed phylum shares, Bray-Curtis values and two-fold counts equal the course's earlier tables; the PCoA axes rebuild the Bray-Curtis matrix; CAZyme genes per MAG and per family equal module 7's own gene search of 17 MAGs; with one MAG at 100% the weighting returns that MAG's own rate (Bacteroides cellulosilyticus: CAZyme genes per 1,000 genes, as in module 7). MAGs come from the course's mmlong2 run, taxonomy from GTDB-Tk 2.5.2 [8]; genes from Pyrodigal 3.7.1 [9]; CAZymes from the dbCAN HMMs (release db_v5-2-9_5-5-2026, substrate groups from dbCAN3 [10]) searched with pyhmmer 0.12.3 [11]; the analysis itself is Python (pandas, numpy).

Interpretation. The two large phyla do not move in one step: Bacteroidota first rises right after the switch and then falls below its days 1-5 level, while Bacillota ends slightly higher (% in days 1-5, % in days 6-10). Because these are shares, the late rise of Cyanobacteriota (one MAG, Zag111) and Pseudomonadota takes room from the others by itself [7]; the Bacteroidota fall on days 9-10 may partly be this. That diversity stays at about effective MAGs suggests a reshuffling of the same community rather than a loss or gain of many members.

Interpretation. Day 6 is as close to days 1-5 ( on average) as those days are to each other (at most ); day 7 is at and days 8-10 at -. One gradient, from days 1-6 to day 10, dominates the map. A stool sample reflects food eaten one or more days earlier, and composition depends on several days of diet [2]; a delay of one to two days is therefore what one would expect if the diet drives the change. It is not proof: with one switch, a change that was under way anyway would look the same.

Figure 3. Single MAGs: step or trend, and where the break falls

For the MAGs with a mean share of at least 0.1% we asked where in the ten days each MAG changes most.

What it shows. Top left: the chosen MAG's share of each day's bases (%). Top right: for each of the nine splits into an early and a late block, the share of the MAG's day-to-day variation (log scale) explained by the two block means (R²); orange = the diet switch (5|6), dark blue = the best split. Bottom: for the MAGs that changed at least two-fold, how many have their best split at each place.

Interpretation. of the two-fold MAGs change most after the switch, at it and before it; are completely separated (all five low-carbohydrate days beyond all five high-carbohydrate days). Compared without choosing the split, a step at the switch describes of them better than a straight line and a line ; the MAGs were selected on the 5|6 split, which slightly favours a step there. The fallers fit published work: Roseburia and A. rectalis (E. rectale) are the group that fell when carbohydrate intake was lowered [3] and on an animal-based diet [1]; here both periods were vegetarian, so the drop more likely follows the amount of carbohydrate or calories than its source. The largest gain in share is Acetatifactor intestinalis (% to %), with Bacteroides and Phocaeicola among the other risers. One explanation for the risers is less competition once the fallers decline; with shares only, a rise can also be the mirror of a fall elsewhere [7].

We then weighted every MAG's CAZyme genes by its share of each day and asked which MAGs move the community value.

What it shows. Left: degrading CAZyme genes of the chosen substrate group per 1,000 genes of the binned community, each MAG weighted by its share of the day's bases. Right: the twelve MAGs that add most to the change between the periods (genes per 1,000 genes; positive = pushes the value up in days 6-10; formula in Data and methods), coloured by phylum. All CAZyme genes: in days 1-5, in days 6-10 (means of the days, %); "all" counts each gene once, so the groups do not add up to it.

Interpretation. The community's potential hardly changes: starch %, fructan %, plant fibre %, host glycan %. Plant fibre did not fall although plant-fibre degraders fell: of the fallers carry fewer plant-fibre genes per gene than the community, so losing them even adds genes per 1,000 genes (the risers add ). The largest contributions come from Bacteroidota replacing each other: the two Prevotella copri_B MAGs fall (% to % and % to %) while Phocaeicola and Bacteroides rise. Bacteroidota carry rich CAZyme repertoires [4, 5], so their reshuffling matters more than their share suggests. The host-glycan rise fits the idea that a lower-fibre diet favours bacteria that use mucus glycans [6], but it is small in genes, and it is potential, not activity.

Discussion

Community dynamics. The first hypothesis holds in a narrow sense: the community changed between the periods, and specific MAGs rose or fell consistently ( of two-fold MAGs completely separated). The change came one to two days after the switch rather than at it, in line with earlier work showing that the community reflects several days of diet [2] and responds within days [1]. Bacteroidota is the exception that jumps at the switch, then falls; the two phases may be different responses (for example to the change in food, then to the lower calories), but ten days cannot tell. The fallers are the butyrate producers that earlier work saw drop with less carbohydrate [3], which makes the diet the most likely driver, though time and order are confounded with it.

Functional dynamics. The second hypothesis, that carbohydrate potential differs between the periods, is not supported in any large way: the community's CAZyme genes per 1,000 genes changed by % and no common family changed 1.5-fold. This is what one expects if MAGs with similar repertoires replace each other: the community keeps its toolkit while its membership changes, often called functional redundancy (our interpretation). The small shifts trace to Bacteroidota (Prevotella copri_B down, Phocaeicola and Bacteroides up), whose utilisation loci make them major carriers of CAZymes [4, 5], and the host-glycan rise fits a lower-fibre diet [6]. Gene content does not say how much each enzyme is used; in [1] diet changed microbial gene expression across people, which a DNA-only study cannot see.

What is solid and what is not. Most solid: the timing of the community change (Figures 2 and 3, consistent across MAGs and with Bray-Curtis) and the stability of the CAZyme potential (small changes in every group and family). Less solid: why single MAGs rose (shares, not cell counts [7]), whether the Bacteroidota jump at the switch is the diet or chance (one day), and anything about activity.

Limitations

  • One person, ten days, one switch: no replicates and no tests; diet is confounded with time and order, and there is no sample from before the high-calorie diet.
  • Shares of DNA, not cell counts: a rise in one MAG lowers all others; DNA from dead cells and food is included.
  • Only binned bases (-% per day) are counted; the unbinned part may behave differently.
  • CAZyme genes are potential, not activity; calls come from one HMM search (no consensus of tools), the substrate groups are broad, and MAGs may miss genes.
  • The break check chooses the best of nine splits, and the two-fold MAGs were selected on the 5|6 split; both bias the comparison of step and trend.

Open questions and possible next analyses

  • A return towards the usual state? Days 6-10 were closer to the person's normal diet after about two weeks of the high-calorie diet. Samples from a normal week (not measured) would show whether days 8-10 approach them.
  • The two Prevotella copri_B MAGs drive much of the CAZyme change and keep a fixed ratio over the days: one genome split in two, or two strains? Their variant and coverage data (module 8, on the Mac) could tell.
  • Butyrate potential. The fallers are butyrate producers [3]; the same weighting on butyrate pathway genes (module 7's marker genes, run for all MAGs on the Mac) would show whether the community's butyrate potential fell.
  • Polysaccharide utilisation loci. Grouping the Bacteroidota CAZymes into loci [5] (on the Mac, from gene positions) would show which glycans the rising and falling MAGs specialise in.
  • The unbinned fifth. A read-based taxonomic profile (a server job on the reads) would show whether the bases in no MAG change the same way.
