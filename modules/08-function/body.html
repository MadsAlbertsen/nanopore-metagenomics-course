Module 8

Function: what the genes may do, and how sure we are

Module 7 names the organism a MAG comes from. This module asks what it can do. Bakta () finds the genes in each MAG and names them by similarity to known proteins. Every gene name is a prediction. This page shows what a name rests on, how two tools compare on the same genes, what happens when the reference database lacks a gene's relatives or a genome is incomplete, and how a family tree with enzymes of measured activity can help tell related enzymes apart.

2. What a gene name rests on

With the light database used here, most names come from a match to a UniRef50 cluster: known proteins at least 50% identical to the cluster's representative (Suzek et al. 2015). A match counts when the proteins align over at least 80% of both and are at least 50% identical. The median identity is ; are 90% identical or more. So most names are carried over from a related protein, not an identical one. named proteins are only a domain of unknown function (DUF) or "uncharacterized". Of the hypothetical proteins, carry a Pfam domain (Mistry et al. 2021): a hint at their function that Bakta does not turn into a name.

3. Two tools, one gene

A second tool gives a check. KOfam (Aramaki et al. 2020) holds one profile per KEGG orthology group (KO), each with its own score threshold, and gives a gene a KO only above that threshold. We ran it on all CDS of the four complete, single-contig test MAGs of module 7. Bakta gives of these genes a name other than "hypothetical protein" (vague, domain-only names included), KOfam gives a KO, and get neither. Where both name a gene, do they agree? For such genes both sides carry an EC number (the enzyme code), and share one; the rest mix renumbered EC codes with related enzymes. Genes without EC numbers need another check: random genes named by both were graded by hand, by a reviewer who saw only the two names (chart, right).

4. When relatives are missing from the database

Module 7 removed a test MAG's species, genus, family or order from the reference database, and the classifiers then gave confident names one rank too deep. Here the same was done for function. The proteins of module 7's reference genomes were predicted, and each test-MAG gene took the KO of its best remaining match (DIAMOND, Buchfink et al. 2021), scored against the gene's own KOfam KO ( genes with a KO). Function holds up far better than names do: with the species removed keep their KO, with the whole order removed still , from best matches only identical (median). A wrong KO stays rare (at most ); the rest find a match without a KO or none. This counts only the genes KOfam can label at all, mostly conserved, well-studied families, and "same KO" means both proteins pass the same profile, not that their activity was measured.

A model can be missing too. For each of the genes with a KO, its own profile was set aside. still get a KO above threshold: genes that two profiles claim. match another profile only below its threshold, and match nothing. So the thresholds are what stop a gene from taking a relative's KO when its own model is missing: taking the best match regardless of threshold would give three quarters of these genes another KO.

5. Incomplete MAGs and pathway calls

A pathway is often called present when all its steps are found. KEGG modules (Kanehisa et al. 2023) define such pathways as steps, some with alternative genes. In each test MAG we counted the modules that are complete with the KOfam KOs (), then removed parts of the genome at random, as if the MAG were 95%, 85% or 70% complete, and counted again (100 draws each). Two ways of removing DNA were used: 10 kb blocks, or random cuts into pieces (mean 50 kb, like MAG contigs) of which whole pieces are dropped. Keeping 100% kept every module, as a control. The genes with a KO that are lost follow the DNA removed (), but a pathway needs all its steps: at 95% complete of the complete modules already look incomplete, at 70% (mean over draws and MAGs, 10 kb blocks). Long pathways look incomplete in most draws (table). A missing pathway in an incomplete MAG is weak evidence that the organism lacks it.

6. Related enzymes: which glycyl radical enzyme?

Glycyl radical enzymes (GREs) are one protein family of anaerobic bacteria. All carry a radical on a glycine that lets them break bonds other enzymes cannot, but the members act on very different molecules (Backman et al. 2017): pyruvate formate-lyase (PFL) splits pyruvate in fermentation, choline trimethylamine-lyase (CutC) makes trimethylamine from choline, others remove water from glycerol, 1,2-propanediol or hydroxyproline (HypD), or start the breakdown of toluene (benzylsuccinate synthase, BssA). More were found later, such as enzymes that cut sulfite from sulfonates (Peck et al. 2019, Liu et al. 2020). HypD is widespread in the gut microbiomes of healthy people (Levin et al. 2017). With Pfam's own cut-off, genes in of the 421 MAGs carry the family's domain (PFL-like). Gene names follow sequence similarity, and in this family similar sequences can have different substrates, so the name can point to the wrong reaction.

A tree with enzymes of measured activity helps. The genes whose domain covers at least 70% of the Pfam model and that are 650-950 amino acids long were aligned (MAFFT, Katoh and Standley 2013) with reference enzymes of activities (table below), and a tree was built (IQ-TREE with ModelFinder and 1,000 ultrafast bootstraps; Kalyaanamoorthy et al. 2017, Hoang et al. 2018); the other genes are partial or of unusual length. The tree has no root, so a group here is the set of leaves on one side of a branch. Each gene takes the activity of the references on the smallest side of a well-supported branch (bootstrap 95 or more) that holds the gene and at least one reference; if several activities are there, or no such branch exists, it stays unresolved. Reference enzymes

The tree changes the call for several groups of genes. Of the genes in the tree, Bakta names glycerol dehydratase, but all sit in a well-supported group with the Roseburia inulinivorans enzyme, at identity (closer than to the Clostridium butyricum glycerol dehydratase). LaMattina et al. (2016) showed that this enzyme, 48% identical to the glycerol dehydratase, dehydrates 1,2-propanediol: it is a diol dehydratase. It names benzylsuccinate synthase; of them sit in the HypD group at identity to Clostridioides difficile HypD, and KOfam also gives them the HypD KO. genes named pyruvate formate-lyase sit in the diol dehydratase group, though their closest reference is the glycerol dehydratase ( identical): the tree and the identity agree on a dehydratase, not on which one. Of the genes in the tree named formate acetyltransferase or pyruvate formate-lyase, come out as PFL.

The tree also has limits. genes named formate acetyltransferase come out as CutC, and KOfam agrees, but they are only identical to any reference. genes take the label BssA or PhdB from a single distant reference; none is more than identical to any reference. A well-supported group with one distant reference shows a relative, not the same activity. Of the genes Bakta names 4-hydroxyphenylacetate decarboxylase (HpdB, which makes p-cresol), one sits with the measured HpdB enzymes ( identity, with the HpdB KO); the other stay unresolved, at identity to the nearest reference and without the KO: here the tree cannot confirm the name. genes stay unresolved, at a median identity to the nearest reference: none of the reference enzymes is close to them. The same caution holds for other families: bile salt hydrolase, used in module 9, shares its domain with penicillin V acylase, and the two are often confused in annotations (O'Flaherty et al. 2018). A tree with measured enzymes narrows the choice; only an assay shows what an enzyme does.

8. Resistance genes: a name is not a trait

Bakta runs AMRFinderPlus, NCBI's tool for resistance genes (Feldgarden et al. 2021). It named genes in MAGs by a match to a reference resistance protein, most often tetracycline ribosomal protection proteins (table). A further genes in MAGs matched only a resistance-protein family model, including regulators that do not give resistance on their own. A match says that a gene like a known resistance gene is there, not that it is active or that the bacterium survives the drug; that needs growth tests. Only the MAGs were annotated, so plasmids outside them are missing here.

9. Where annotation falls short

Every name on this page is a prediction from similarity. Lineages known mainly from genomes have few studied proteins, so many of their genes stay hypothetical, and a tool built for bacteria leaves an archaeon's key genes unnamed (example 1). Two tools mostly agree where both name a gene, but about one in twenty such genes gets two different functions, often related proteins in other systems (section 3). A missing reference costs few KOs, and the thresholds keep wrong calls rare (section 4), but a missing pathway in an incomplete MAG means little (section 5). Related enzymes need more than a name: a tree with measured references helps, and only an assay settles it (section 6).
