Module 6

Methylation: misassemblies, contamination and placing contigs

Bacteria mark their own DNA. Each genome's methyltransferases add a methyl group at short sequence motifs, and nanopore reads carry those marks: the basecaller reads them along with the bases. A genome's set of methylated motifs is therefore a signature of the cell its DNA came from, a signal separate from depth and sequence composition. This page shows how the example analysis uses that signature three ways: to find contigs that do not belong in their MAG, to find places inside a contig where two different sequences were joined, and to place contigs that binning left out, such as plasmids, in a MAG. Each use has a test with a known answer, and each has limits.

Guided examples

1. A genome's own signature

Methyltransferases recognise a short motif, such as GATC, and methylate an adenine (6mA) or a cytosine (5mC, 4mC) in it. Many are part of restriction-modification systems: a restriction enzyme cuts DNA where the motif is not methylated, which protects the cell against phages. (The Nanomotif paper, Heidelbach et al. 2024, a bioRxiv preprint, describes this background.) Because the enzyme works on every copy of its motif, nearly all sites are marked: here the median motif is methylated at of its sites in its MAG (Nanomotif reports only motifs methylated at most sites). Using methylation to bin genomes and to link plasmids to their hosts was shown earlier with PacBio reads (Beaulaurier et al. 2018, Nature Biotechnology).

2. A MAG's fingerprint

For every contig with at least 5x depth, the analysis counts, for each motif Nanomotif found in any MAG, how many of its sites are covered and how many are methylated (a site counts as methylated when at least half of its reads say so). The share of methylated sites per contig and motif is the fingerprint: a column per motif, a row per contig. Contigs of one genome should look alike. A motif counts as methylated at 60% of sites or more, unmethylated at 25% or less; with fewer than 10 sites it is unknown. Pick a MAG below, or open a guided example.

3. Contamination: each contig against the rest of its MAG

The screen compares each contig with the rest of its MAG (a median over the other contigs, weighted by their sites). A contig that lacks a motif the rest has, or carries one the rest lacks, is in conflict; two conflicts or more flag it. Known answer first: circular single-contig high-quality MAGs were cut into 100 kb pieces and each received one piece from a genome with other motifs, three times over. The screen flagged moved pieces and pieces left in place. On the real MAGs it flags of contigs with enough data, in MAGs; binned contigs have too little data (mostly below 5x).

4. Misassemblies: each 100 kb window against the rest of its contig

The same comparison inside a contig finds joins: every contig of 200 kb or more is cut into 100 kb windows, each compared with the rest of its contig. In a simulation, a stretch of another genome inserted into a contig was flagged, the methylation change was placed within about 1 kb of both joins, and reads were clipped at both, so splits were proposed; an island with its own methylation in reads of the same genome was flagged without clipping (methylation only). On real data, of windows were flagged in the cut-test genomes, in contigs of MAGs and in contigs outside MAGs. Reads cross the edges of flagged windows like any other position (median span ratio , as the baseline's ; edges weak), so most flags are stretches with their own methylation, not assembly errors. 5. Placing contigs and plasmids

A cell's methyltransferases also mark plasmids and phages inside it, so a contig binning left out can be placed by matching its methylated motifs to a MAG's (Nanomotif does this too; the analysis uses its own version). A contig fits a MAG when no motif conflicts and at least one methylated motif is shared; it is placed when one MAG shares more methylated motifs than any other. Known answer: each binned contig was taken out of its MAG and placed as if unbinned. Of such contigs with a methylated motif, went back to their own MAG, to another, the rest nowhere or to a tie. Contigs of single-contig MAGs have no MAG left to go to (host absent): were placed anyway; these contigs are all 200 kb or more, so they say nothing about short ones. Of unbinned contigs with a methylated motif, were placed by methylation; of circular ones (likely plasmids and phages), . Wrong placements are mostly short contigs and contigs that share one distinct motif (related forms such as CCWGG, CCAGG and CCTGG count once): of placements under 50 kb and on one distinct motif were wrong.

Second checks that do not use methylation: depth and composition

Depth: a contig from a cell should rise and fall with that cell's genome over the ten days. The check compares the shape of the depth profile (log depth per Gb sequenced, centred and scaled), not its level, so a plasmid with a constant number of copies per cell still matches its host; a copy number that changes over the days makes the check miss true placements, and days with depth near zero make it noisier. Composition: the contig's tetranucleotide profile against each MAG's. For each check all MAGs are ranked, and it passes when the placed MAG is among the 3 best (a binned contig's own MAG without it). In the control, wrong placements fell from of those kept with methylation alone to with depth, with composition and with both; composition kept more of the right ones (, depth ). Binning used depth and composition, so these numbers are an upper bound. Composition kept only circular placements: plasmids and phages can differ in composition from their host, so it is no usable check for them, and for circular contigs the page leans on methylation and depth ().

Conclusion. Methylation alone over-places short contigs and contigs that share few distinct motifs with a MAG. A second check cuts the wrong share in the control to 2-3%. With depth support, placements of contigs of 50 kb or more were right, short ones only (few contigs). The short control contigs favour both checks (binners added them because composition and depth fit), and unbinned short contigs are shorter still. So for unbinned contigs expect roughly 5-10% of placements with composition or both checks to be wrong, and about 1 in 10 with depth alone; all are lower bounds. Trust placements of contigs of 50 kb or more that share at least 2 distinct methylated motifs (related forms such as CCWGG, CCAGG and CCTGG count once) and whose depth follows the MAG: in the control such placements were wrong; unbinned contigs qualify, and circular contigs of any length share at least 2 distinct motifs with depth support. A placement means "carries a compatible methylation pattern and follows the MAG's depth (or composition)", not proof of belonging.

6. Where it fails

Methylation only helps where it can be measured and where genomes differ. Below 5x depth there is no data: all MAGs under 5x lack motifs, and binned contigs have too little methylation data to be screened. Common motifs carry little information: GATC is in the motif lists of MAGs, and a contig methylated only at GATC-like motifs fits many MAGs equally well, so it stays unplaced (a tie). Two MAGs that share all their motifs cannot be told apart, which can happen for close strains; a strain that differs in one methyltransferase can, as module 5's MAG 2.441 shows. Short contigs have few motif sites, so fewer motifs are known and conflicts can be missed, which fits short placements going wrong more often. A low-depth MAG with little known methylation conflicts with almost nothing, so it can attract wrong placements (example 5). Plasmids and phages can carry their own methyltransferases, move between hosts, or block methylation; then their pattern does not match their host's.
