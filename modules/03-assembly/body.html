Module 3

Assembly: from overlapping reads to contigs

An assembler gets millions of reads in no order and has to rebuild the genomes they came from. It finds reads that share sequence (overlaps), joins them into a graph and reads contigs off that graph. Repeats and closely related strains are what make this hard. Our data were assembled with myloasm (version 0.2.0, inside mmlong2), all ten samples together. Sections 1 to 3 are simulations: toy genomes and toy rules, made to show one idea each. They are not myloasm itself; the panel after them says what myloasm does, from its authors' paper. Sections 4 and 5 show myloasm's real assembly graph from our data. Before this step the reads were filtered and trimmed (module 2). Next: module 4 sorts these contigs into genomes.

Guided examples

1. Why long reads make assembly easier

simulated

Genomes contain repeats: stretches that occur more than once, such as ribosomal RNA operons or insertion sequences. Reads that fall inside a repeat look the same whichever copy they came from, so the assembler cannot tell where to go next. A read that runs through the whole repeat and into unique sequence on both sides settles it. This is a main reason why long reads give much longer contigs than short reads (see Koren and Phillippy, 2015).

The toy genome below is 60 kb long with one repeat (R) in two copies between three unique pieces (A, B, C). Reads of one fixed length are placed at random to 10x depth. Toy rule: the assembler follows the genome through a repeat copy only if at least one read covers that whole copy plus 1 kb of unique sequence on each side. Every unspanned copy ends the contig there and becomes a separate repeat node; if both copies are unspanned, they are merged into one.

Simulated: random read positions with a fixed seed, no sequencing errors, all reads the same length. Real reads vary in length, so a few long reads can span a repeat that most reads do not. Real assemblers also use other clues, such as depth (myloasm does, see below).

2. From reads to overlaps to a graph to contigs

simulated

Nine error-free toy reads from a 24 kb genome, step by step through the overlap (string graph) approach that myloasm builds on (Myers, 2005). The assembler never sees where a read came from; it only sees which reads share sequence. Each read is a node, each overlap longer than 500 bp an edge (myloasm's default minimum). Reads lying wholly inside another read add nothing to the path and are set aside. An edge that can be reached in two hops anyway is redundant ("transitive") and removed. What is left is a chain without branches: a unitig, which becomes a contig.

Simulated: hand-placed reads without errors, so every overlap is exact. With real reads the overlaps must be found despite errors, which is where most of an assembler's work goes.

3. Closely related strains

simulated

A gut sample often holds two or more strains of one species that differ at a small share of positions. Their reads overlap as well as reads of one strain do. An assembler built for noisy reads has to accept some mismatches in an overlap; if it accepts as many as the strains differ by, it merges (collapses) the strains into one mixed contig.

myloasm takes another route: it first finds positions where the reads consistently disagree (likely true differences, not read errors), then refuses overlaps that disagree at such a position, while other mismatches are tolerated. Two 30 kb toy strains, reads of 5 kb with random substitution errors. Left: a mismatch-tolerant rule (join if at most 5% of the overlap differs; a generic rule, not a specific assembler). Right: the variant-aware idea. Each read keeps only its longest allowed overlap on each side: a shortcut for removing transitive edges (section 2), exact only when all overlaps are allowed; a real string graph can keep branches. Coloured bars are groups of reads joined this way; each group would become one or more contigs.

Mismatch-tolerant rule

Variant-aware rule (the idea behind myloasm)

Simulated: one region of 30 kb, every read 5 kb, substitution errors only, single positions compared instead of myloasm's 21-letter words. As in the myloasm paper, a position counts as a variant when the two most common bases are each seen in reads from both DNA strands and the rarer one passes a binomial test against an error rate of 0.025. Not modelled: the paper's strand-bias test (Fisher's exact test; a pair is rejected at odds ratio above 1.5 and p below 0.005), myloasm's base-quality check at variant positions and its relaxed threshold when no exactly matching reads exist, both of which reduce the breaks caused by read errors; and its use of depth to clean the graph.

What myloasm does, step by step

From the myloasm paper (Shaw, Marin and Li, bioRxiv 2025; published in Nature Biotechnology 2026) and its documentation. The authors call it a "metagenomic noisy long-read assembler" that "uses polymorphic k-mers to construct a high-resolution string graph and then leverages differential abundance for graph simplification". The paper describes version 0.1.0; we ran 0.2.0, whose listed changes concern polishing, filters for low-depth contigs, removal of duplicated plasmids, input formats and the output format. The right column gives the times and counts from our run's log.

StepWhat it does (the authors' terms in quotes)Our run
1. Find variant positionsCounts all 21-letter words (k-mers) in the reads and keeps pairs that differ only in the middle letter: "SNPmers", "pairs of k-mers that differ by a substitution in the middle base". Rare words (likely read errors) are filtered out with a bloom filter and a test for strand bias and a binomial test. This is "a form of reference-free variant calling".1.8 h (k-mer counting with a 100 GB bloom filter and --aggressive-bloom, which the documentation says "may save some memory, but lead to non-deterministic results")
2. Index and compare readsReads are indexed with SNPmers and "open syncmers" (a sample of about 1 in 11 k-mers). Two reads are compared by chaining exact syncmer matches and then SNPmer matches ("double chaining"). From the share of SNPmers whose middle letter differs, myloasm estimates the true difference between the two reads with the read errors "divided out". This step is "permissive for missing syncmer or SNPmer hits between reads; error correction is not required. However, it is strict for mismatched SNPs".38.0 M reads of 1 kb or more; 0.25 h to index them
3. Set aside contained readsReads lying inside a longer read with no differing SNPmer are removed; the rest are "outer reads".36.9 M reads contained, 1.14 M outer reads (first round), 0.75 h
4. Depth and chimeric readsAll reads are mapped to the outer reads to get each read's depth, at three identity thresholds (about 99%, 99.75% and 100%), because "under extensive strain variation ... few perfect mappings may exist". Where a stretch of a read is backed by almost no other reads (depth 2 or less) right next to a stretch with more than 5 times that depth, the read is taken as chimeric or as carrying a large error and is broken in two there; then containment and mapping are repeated.2.1 h mapping (288 GB of memory at this step in myloasm's log; the job's peak, measured by the pipeline, was 323 GB); 449,913 reads (1.18%) split as "possibly chimeric or noisy"; two more rounds 1.5 h
5. Build the string graphOverlaps of more than 500 bp between outer reads become edges when no SNPmer differs (with a relaxed threshold where depth shows no exactly matching reads exist). Transitive edges are removed and chains without branches are collapsed into unitigs.1.1 h for overlaps, cleaning, circular contigs and removing duplicates
6. Clean the graphFirst light cleaning (remove tips, pop bubbles, cut much shorter overlaps). Then a "random path model": paths whose depth stays consistent, with long and identical overlaps, get a high probability; edges rarely used by such paths are cut. This is repeated "from high to low temperature" (inspired by simulated annealing): first only clear cuts, then stricter ones on the longer unitigs. Cuts must be "safe", so they do not leave dead ends.
7. Circular contigsUses the "progressive coverage filter of metaMDBG": low-depth parts are removed step by step, and an isolated unitig that loops onto itself is taken as a circular contig. Small circular elements (plasmids, phages) get a separate heuristic.122 possibly circular contigs of 1 Mb or more
8. Polish and remove duplicatesAll reads are aligned to the unitigs and a consensus is computed with partial order alignment (SPOA) in 400 bp windows. Contigs with more than 99.9% identity over more than 99% of their length to a larger contig are duplicates; with more than 99% identity and 90% aligned they go to a separate file of "alternates".2.7 h read alignment, 1.2 h polishing; 20,442 primary contigs (plus 9,435 alternates and 16,853 duplicates in separate files)

Each contig name in the output carries what myloasm found: for example u2658272ctg_len-2275750_circular-possibly_depth-10-9-9_duplicated-no mult=1.00 (example from the documentation). "circular-yes" needs a self-loop, depth above 5x and no other connections; "possibly" means a self-loop without the rest. The three depths are for about 99%, 99.75% and 100% identity. "mult" is how often 21-mers repeat within the contig; for a single genome it should be near 1.00, and above 1 can mean two strains joined into one contig.

What is not settled. The documentation lists as a limitation that myloasm may "occasionally produce chimeric misassembled contigs (like all metagenome assemblers)", and the paper says errors arise "from sequencing artifacts ... to erroneous graph resolution for regions with high similarity"; they recommend CheckM2 on long contigs. They also question the term "strain-resolved": where strains recombine, it is "not even clear what a genome assembly means". The documentation says myloasm "can resolve up to 99% ANI quite confidently"; how close two strains in our data can be and still come apart is not measured here.

4. Real graphs from our assembly

our data

myloasm saves its graph as a GFA file. Each node is one contig before polishing, each link an overlap between the ends of two contigs, or between the two ends of one contig (a loop). The nodes carry the contig names (u…ctg), so the graph can be tied to the final contigs and the MAGs. We checked this first: all contigs of myloasm's primary assembly are nodes of the same name and depth. For of the linear ones the polished length is within 2% of the node's (all contigs of 100 kb or more); circular contigs are shorter than their node by about the overlap of their loop (correlation ). mmlong2 adds np26_coassembly_ to each name and _start-end to the pieces of the contigs it split.

The graph also holds nodes () that are not in the primary assembly: myloasm removed them after writing the graph. Its log counts 16,853 duplicates and 9,435 alternates (near-identical versions of kept contigs) among them, but does not itemise the rest. 5. Small circles: what happens to them

our data

A contig whose end overlaps its own start is a loop in the graph, and myloasm marks it circular ("yes" or "possibly", see the panel on myloasm above). primary contigs loop onto themselves. Of the of 1 Mb or more, are in a MAG, of them of high quality by CheckM2 ( of these were split by mmlong2 at likely misassemblies, so not every one is a closed genome in the MAG; the one not in a MAG is a loop). The small ones are another story: are under 100 kb (median ). mmlong2 keeps of them (it drops most contigs under 3 kb), and only of these are in a MAG; of those are only "possibly" circular and is a piece of a circle that mmlong2 split. Counting whole contigs marked circular-yes, of are in a MAG, the same as mmlong2's own circular flag gives. myloasm's authors treat small circles as plasmids and phages; we have not checked what each one is. Binning groups contigs by depth and composition (module 4), and a plasmid's copy number can give it another depth than its host's chromosome. Module 6 tries to place such contigs by their methylation.
