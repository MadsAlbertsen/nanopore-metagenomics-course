Module 8

Your project: what data we have

Each group will write a short report on a question of its own about these stool samples. This page lists what the report can draw on: the tables, sequences and results behind the earlier modules, plus your own libraries from the course run. Each item says what it holds, at what level of detail (per sample, per MAG, per gene), and what kind of question it can help answer. The level tells how quickly it can be used: ready tables exist now; quick to compute means minutes on a laptop from files that are here; needs a server job means hours on the cluster with all the reads, and is not part of the group reports; not measured is outside this study.

From an idea to a question the data can answer

  1. Start from the design. One person, ten days, two diet periods. A question about change over the days or between the periods fits the data; a question about people in general does not.
  2. Name what you will look at. A group of MAGs (a family, the ones that change most, the circular ones), a set of genes (a function, a CAZyme family, resistance genes), a kind of contig (plasmids, phages) or your own libraries.
  3. Find the table for each part. Use the topics above. If a part has no table at the ready or quick-to-compute level, change that part of the question.
  4. Say what an answer would look like. A figure, a number or a comparison you expect to see, so the report can show whether it came out that way.

Two examples of questions that fit: "Do the MAGs that fall on the low-carbohydrate diet carry more starch-degrading enzymes than those that rise?" (MAG abundance per day, CAZymes). "Which plasmids can be placed in a host MAG, and do they rise and fall with it over the ten days?" (contig depth per day, methylation placement).
