Module 8

Interpretation: what changes over ten days

One person gave a stool sample every day for ten days: days 1-5 on a high-carbohydrate, high-calorie diet, days 6-10 on a low-carbohydrate, low-calorie diet. The reads of each day were mapped to the MAGs from the co-assembly, so each MAG has an abundance on each day. This page shows how that abundance is measured, how the community changes over the days, and how far one person's time series can be read. Pick a MAG on any chart to follow it over the ten days. The MAGs and their quality are in module 4 and module 5.

Guided examples

How abundance is measured

Each read of a sample is aligned to the contigs, and mmlong2 counts the depth of every contig with jgi_summarize_bam_contig_depths (from MetaBAT2), which leaves out contig ends and poor alignments. A MAG's abundance on a day is the read bases counted on its contigs (contig length times mean depth, summed over the MAG's contigs), as a share of all read bases sequenced that day. It is a share of bases, not of reads: a long read counts more than a short one. Samples were sequenced to very different amounts (1.7 to 25.6 Gb), so raw amounts mostly follow sequencing effort; shares remove that.

But shares are relative: they add up to 100%, so when one genome takes a larger share, all others take a smaller share even if their own amount in the gut did not change. Sequencing cannot tell which happened. Not every base is counted on a MAG: some are counted on contigs that were not binned, and a few percent on no contig at all. Who could do what: metabolic potential

Which of these genomes could break down which food? This section looks at 17 MAGs, chosen by a rule written down before looking at their genes: among the high-quality MAGs with a mean of 0.1% or more of all read bases, the six that rose most from days 1-5 to days 6-10, the six that fell most, the four most abundant that changed less than 1.4-fold, and one Faecalibacterium as a known butyrate producer. Genes were predicted with Pyrodigal, then searched with two sets of profile HMMs: dbCAN's for carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes; here the glycoside hydrolases, polysaccharide lyases and carbohydrate esterases that take sugars apart; profile search only; dbCAN's recommended set also asks for agreement with one of two other tools) and KEGG's KOfam for a short list of marker genes (butyrate, propionate, acetate from hydrogen, bile salts, urease, protein fermentation). dbCAN's substrate table (Zheng et al. 2023, Nucleic Acids Res) lists the known activities of each family; a family gets a food category only when at least two thirds of its listed activities fall in that category, and some families rest on one or two listed activities. Read the categories as a rough guide. Note who is in which group: the risers are mostly not Bacillota (two Pseudomonadota, a cyanobacterial relative, a Verrucomicrobiota and a Bacteroides), while all six fallers are Bacillota, so differences between the groups largely follow phylum. A gene in a genome is potential: it says what the bacterium could do, not what it did on these days. Pick a MAG in the chart or the table to see its families and marker genes.

Marker columns count genes above KOfam's threshold for each KEGG orthologue (hover a column title for the genes). The butyryl-CoA:acetate CoA-transferase that most gut butyrate producers use has no clean KEGG profile and is found by its Bakta name; a complete butyrate route needs butyryl-CoA dehydrogenase plus a last step. A dash means no gene was found: MAGs are not complete, and a gene can fall just below a profile's threshold (the Prevotella c.36 gene Bakta names methylmalonyl-CoA decarboxylase scores 814 against a threshold of 830, so c.36 shows no succinate-pathway gene). The succinate-pathway gene of Ornithomonoglobus s.14 is named propionyl-CoA carboxylase by Bakta, a related enzyme. Checks: Pyrodigal in metagenome mode reproduces every Bakta gene's stop codon, and the genes used here (trained on each MAG) share the stop codon with 96.6-99.4% of Bakta's; of 214 genes in ten well-known CAZyme families that Bakta named, 196 (92%) carry a name that fits the family; of the genes Bakta named as carbohydrate enzymes, 65-100% per MAG have a dbCAN call (606 of 669 pooled, nearly half of them in Bacteroides c.119).

What one person's time series can and cannot show

It can show which genomes are present, how large a share of the read bases each takes, and which shares go up or down over the ten days. It cannot say why. There is one person and one sample per day, with no replicate samples and no second person, and the diet changed at the same time as the days went by: anything else that changed over the ten days would look the same as a diet effect. Day-to-day variation is the yardstick: a change between the two periods means more when the days within each period agree with each other, and less when single days jump about as much. Try other splits of the days: if many splits that are not the diet periods give changes as large, the diet split is not special. Look at the single days, not only the means: a few days can carry a whole period's mean. And when the 124 MAGs with 0.1% or more of the read bases are ranked by their change, some will change two-fold or more by chance alone; a list of the largest changes always has a top, whether or not anything happened.
