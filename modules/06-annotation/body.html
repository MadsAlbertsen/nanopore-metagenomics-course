Module 6

Annotation: what is in a genome, and how sure are we

Annotation answers two questions about a MAG: which organism is it, and what can it do? Both answers are predictions made by comparing the MAG with reference databases, and each comes with evidence of different strength. GTDB-Tk () names every MAG from its marker genes and its similarity to reference genomes. Bakta () finds the genes in each MAG and names them by similarity to known proteins. For each claim this page shows what it rests on: how close the nearest reference genome is and how much of it aligns, and for each gene which database match gave it its name, or that nothing did.

Guided examples

1. How GTDB-Tk names a MAG

GTDB-Tk finds 120 bacterial (or 53 archaeal) marker genes in a MAG, places the MAG in a tree of reference genomes from the Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB), and compares it with the closest reference genomes by average nucleotide identity (ANI) (Chaumeil et al. 2022, Bioinformatics). A MAG gets a species name when its ANI to that species' reference genome is within the species' radius (95% for most species) and enough of the genomes align: an alignment fraction (AF) of 0.5 or more, where GTDB-Tk takes the larger of the two genomes' aligned shares (GTDB-Tk documentation, version 2.5: --min_af and summary.tsv). Here MAGs got a species name, at a median ANI of and a median AF of . When no reference is close enough, the name stops at a higher rank, chosen from the MAG's position in the tree, and GTDB-Tk reports the relative evolutionary divergence (RED), a scaled depth in the tree that GTDB uses to set ranks (Parks et al. 2018, Nature Biotechnology); all MAGs without a species name here have one. 2. Latin names, placeholder names and genus only

A species name is not always a Latin name. GTDB gives a species without a published Latin name, often one known only from MAGs, a placeholder name such as CAG-417 sp000432835, after the genome that defines the species. Here MAGs have a Latin species name, a placeholder name and are named only to genus; no MAG stops above genus. 4. What a gene name rests on

Bakta looks for evidence in steps (Schwengers et al. 2021, Microbial Genomics). With the light database used here, the main evidence for a protein is a match to a UniRef50 cluster: a group of known proteins that are at least 50% identical to its representative (Suzek et al. 2015, Bioinformatics). A match counts when the two proteins align over at least 80% of both and are at least 50% identical; the cluster's name becomes the gene's name. The median identity of the matches is , and are 90% identical or more: most names are carried over from a related protein, not an identical one. Some names say little: named proteins are a domain of unknown function (DUF) or "uncharacterized". Hypothetical proteins are searched for Pfam domains (Mistry et al. 2021, Nucleic Acids Research); have one, a hint at their function that Bakta does not turn into a name (the bar below counts in "Pfam domain only" those without a cluster match).

6. Resistance genes: a name is not a trait

Bakta runs AMRFinderPlus, NCBI's tool for resistance genes (Feldgarden et al. 2021, Scientific Reports). It named genes in MAGs by a match to a reference resistance protein, most often tetracycline ribosomal protection proteins (table). A further genes in MAGs matched only a resistance-protein family model; these include regulators and sensors such as VanR- and BlaR1-family proteins, which do not give resistance on their own. A match says that a gene like a known resistance gene is there. It does not say that the gene is active or that the bacterium survives the drug; that needs growth tests. Only the MAGs were annotated, so plasmids and other contigs outside the MAGs are missing here. The data are from one person and have no drug tests, so these genes say nothing about the diet or about how this person would respond to antibiotics.

7. Where annotation falls short

Every name on this page is a prediction from similarity, and its strength depends on the reference data. Lineages known mainly from genomes have few studied proteins, so most of their genes stay hypothetical, and a database built for bacteria leaves an archaeon's key genes unnamed even where a Pfam domain shows what they are (example 4). The light database matches proteins to clusters at 50% identity or more, so a name often comes from a distant relative and says the protein family more than its exact role. GTDB names follow fixed lines (a 95% ANI radius, AF 0.5): for a MAG that is about 94-95% identical to many species, as in example 3, GTDB-Tk cannot assign an existing species, and a fixed boundary cannot separate genomes this close to it. A gene missing from a MAG may sit in the part of the genome that was not binned (most MAGs here are less than 100% complete, module 4), and genes on plasmids or phages outside the MAG are not annotated at all. This page does not compare the two diet periods; module 7 does, using abundance, not genes.
