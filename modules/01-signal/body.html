Module 1

Signal: what the sequencer measures, and how methylation shows up in it

A nanopore sequencer does not read letters. It measures the electric current through a tiny pore while one DNA strand is pulled through it, thousands of times per second, and a basecaller (Dorado) turns that current into bases. This page looks at the measurement itself: one read's current over time, how Dorado's move table splits the current into bases, how well a simple model of the pore predicts the current, and how a methylated base changes it. The data are not the stool samples of the other modules (their raw current is not part of the example analysis) but a small set that keeps it: reads from eight activated-sludge samples, aligned to four genome bins of their co-assembly.

Guided examples

1. What the sequencer measures

The flow cell holds many pores, each in a membrane with a voltage across it. Ions flow through an open pore; when a DNA strand is pulled through, the bases inside the pore block part of the flow, and the current, in picoampere (pA), changes with them. A motor protein feeds the strand at about bases per second (the setting of this run), and the current is sampled times per second, so a base lasts about samples on average. The trace below is one whole read: of current for bases. The strand enters the pore with the sequencing adapter and the barcode first. Dorado basecalls them, recognises the barcode and then trims these samples (adapter and barcode) from the read: its tag ts gives the number of samples trimmed from the start (Dorado's SAM specification, software-docs.nanoporetech.com/dorado/1.4.0/basecaller/sam_spec/), here and a median of over all reads (shaded). The read's bases then start with the genome: in of reads at most 10 bases come before the first base that aligns to a bin. The last are not basecalled either. 2. From signal to bases

Each base gets the samples between two moves. The current does not belong to one base, though: several bases sit in the narrow part of the pore at once. Oxford Nanopore publishes the expected current for every 9-base stretch (k-mer) of R10.4.1 pores (the 9-mer level table, github.com/nanoporetech/kmer_models), in normalised units. To compare the read with that model, Remora (github.com/nanoporetech/remora) places every base of the bin's reference sequence on the signal: it starts from the move table and shifts the base boundaries a few samples to fit the expected levels, after scaling the read's current to the table. Pick a stretch: the top shows the samples (grey dots) with the measured mean per reference base (dark line) and the model level (dashed; ONT's 9-mer levels as Remora rescales them, in pA through the read's scaling); below are the reference bases on Remora's boundaries and the read's own bases on the move table's, coloured by their quality. Where the quality is low, bases are often wrong or missing, and the read's bases and the reference no longer line up one to one.

3. Seeing methylation

Besides the bases, Dorado's modified-base models give, for every A and C of a read, the probability that it carries a methyl group (6mA on A; 5mC and 4mC on C), stored in the read's MM and ML tags (SAM tag specification, samtools hts-specs). Bacteria methylate short sequence motifs (module 6), so a bin's motifs show up as sequences whose A or C is called modified far more often than the rest. A crude search: per bin, every exact sequence of 4-6 bases, ranked by the share of calls at its A (or C) with probability 0.5 or more. The table shows three motifs read from that list, each methylated in one bin only; the list for any bin is below it. What a methylated base does to the current

For every occurrence of a motif in every read, the measured current minus the model's level, base by base around the modified A (position 0; negative positions come before it in the read), averaged per bin. The model knows only unmodified DNA. In the bins that do not methylate the motif the lines agree closely, so what is left there is the model's own small error at this sequence. In the bin that methylates it the current differs over several bases, from about 2 before to 6 after the A, as expected when several bases in the pore shape the current. The shift is small: normalised units at its largest (a unit is about pA), with the move table's boundaries; since refining partly absorbs it, read these as lower bounds. It is less than the spread between single occurrences (shaded: middle half of the methylated bin's values). One position of one read therefore says little; Dorado's modified-base models, trained with Remora, look at a stretch of signal and bases around each site (Remora's documentation, github.com/nanoporetech/remora).

A site's methylated share is the share of its reads called modified, and a motif's share pools all its sites. These reads give a median of only 1-2 reads per site, so single sites are noisy; the chart compares sites with 3 reads or more with what independent calls at the motif's pooled share would give. They are close, with a few more sites at low shares than independent calls would give (GATC: ): some sites may be less methylated, but most calls that are not methylated are spread over many sites rather than marking a few unmethylated ones. 4. Why this matters for the rest of the course

Everything later in the course starts from Dorado's output, so its limits travel along. Bases are called from a current shaped by several bases at once, and the quality score says how sure the basecaller was; filtering (module 2) and assembly (module 3) have to live with the errors. A methylated base changes the current, and the bases show it too: over the three motifs, reads differ from the reference far more often over the motif in the bin that methylates it than in the other bins (table; a comparison between bins, not a test of the cause), while the bins' error per base overall is similar (). The same modification calls are a signature of the genome a read came from: a cell's methyltransferase marks nearly every copy of its motif, so the methylated motifs help to check bins and to place contigs such as plasmids (module 6).
